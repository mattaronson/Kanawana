## Brief History of Camp Kanawana: Chronological Narrative

### **Founding and Early Years (1892-1923)**

Camp Kanawana traces its origins to 1892 when Lemuel Cushing of the Montreal YMCA brought boys to Lake Saint-Joseph in Saint-Adolphe-d'Howard (near Sainte-Agathe), with organized camping activity documented by 1893. In summer 1894, Billy Ball formally established **Camp Jubilee** at the same location, taking approximately 20 campers to islands in Lake St. Joseph. The name "Jubilee" commemorated the YMCA's fiftieth anniversary (founded 1844). By 1903, attendance had grown fourfold to 210 campers.

**John Roy** is the earliest identified director by name (letters dated 1901). Around 1910, seeking a larger site, the YMCA acquired land at Saint-Sauveur-des-Monts from the Page family and relocated the camp. Sixty-six members attended the first summer at the new site (June 19 - July 17, 1910), with thirty-one "deciding for the Christian life." The camp was renamed **Camp Kanawana** and has operated continuously at this location since.

By 1923, Kanawana had become a sophisticated operation with Camp Chief **Philip G. Paterson**, structured programming including two age divisions (Younger boys 12-15, Older boys 16-18) offering 32 subjects for Honour Awards, and a stable staff with deep connections to Montreal's Anglo-Protestant educational institutions (McGill and Westmount YMCA ties). Cost was $8.00-$8.50 per week including transport. The physical plant included two large pavilions, diving boards, a zinc slide, outdoor chapel, Council Ring for woodcraft meetings, and tent accommodations.

### **Growth and Institutionalization (1920s-1940s)**

The 1920s saw formalization of programming. In 1921-1922, brochures detailed structured activities and a badge system with 42 subjects. In 1925, canoe trips were introduced for the first time (at $3.50 extra charge), and **"Kickers and Knockers"** camp councils gave campers formal governance voice. The **Camp Leaders' Training Class** (documented 1922-1927, 1930) represented an early leadership development pipeline.

Staff showed remarkable longevity: Harry Smith served as camp chef from approximately 1913-1922 (10+ years); Johnston W. Abraham as Business Manager from approximately 1914; F.H. Spinney as Educational Director from 1917; and Allan B. Cornell from approximately 1918. The 1923 *Gas Bag* publication provides a camper's-eye view of daily life in this period.

By 1930, the section structure had evolved to Juveniles (10-12), Juniors (12-14), and Seniors (14-17), lowering minimum age. In 1937, the St-Jean Ambulance Association sent a qualified first aid instructor, formalizing formal first aid training.

### **The War Years and Beyond (1939-1946)**

During WWII, Camp Kanawana operated every summer despite disruptions. In 1941, **Chief Howie Langille** (promoted from Senior Director since 1935) led the camp's 48th season with 68 staff and over 30 evacuees from Britain and continental Europe, representing children sent to Canada under evacuation schemes after the fall of France. Lifesaving instruction and non-swimmer conversion were documented achievements.

Health crises tested camp leadership: scarlet fever quarantine in 1941, mumps outbreak in 1944, and polio cases (6 campers) in 1946. **R.H. Hanagan** served as Montreal YMCA camp director during the mid-1940s, managing detailed season reports. By this era, fathers who had attended as boys were sending their own sons—evidence of multi-generational tradition.

### **Post-War Expansion (1947-1967)**

In 1947, the **Lumbermen and Voyageur Games** were introduced (inspired by YMCA Camp Pine Crest in Ontario), quickly becoming Kanawana's signature competitive event dividing the entire camp into two teams for multi-day competition. As of 2025, the L&V Games have run for 78 editions (one year skipped, likely 2020).

The **Voyageurs de la Verendrye programme** was introduced in the 1950s, taking older campers on extended canoe-camping expeditions. By 1950, Kanawana ran six canoe trips per week to Lake Archambault, the Ottawa River, and the Pembina River. In 1956-1959, the YMCA searched for and selected La Vérendrye Park as a base camp for ages 15+, with six trips departing the first summer.

The **Counsellor-in-Training (CIT) programme** was introduced in the 1960s, formalizing the leadership pipeline from camper to staff. By 1962, the YMCA was planning broader citizenship training programs across city branches and Sir George Williams University. **A. Ross Seaman** directed the camp from the late 1950s through early 1960s, overseeing the section renaming and La Vérendrye expansion.

### **The 1967 Centennial and Transition to Coeducation**

In 1967, Canada's Centennial year, **Camp Kanawana sent six boys and two counsellors** on the CCA Centenary Journey, paddling 150 miles from Deep River, Ontario to Britannia Beach near Ottawa (August 2-9, 1967). The trip leader, **Yves St. Pierre**, wrote the required historical essay. This marked the end of Kanawana's boys-only era (1894-1967).

By 1968-1969, the camp transitioned to coeducation through a formal **Co-ed Camping Pilot Project (1967-1970)** documented in Concordia archives. The YMCA Quebec website states "1968" as when girls were first welcomed; McMorris confirms the first girls admitted were ages 10-12, entering the youngest section. The "Boys' Camping Committee" persisted through 1968-1969, suggesting phased transition. By 1972, the first **all-female Voyageur canoe trip** departed, demonstrating substantive integration within three years of formal coeducation.

### **Modernization (1970s-1990s)**

During the 1970s, the camp underwent professional rebranding from "camping" to "outdoor education," with institutional restructuring reflected in new committee and branch governance. In 1971, Kanawana hosted **"Noosphere,"** an international development education simulation for approximately 100 young Canadians ages 16-19. Y camp counsellors received formal three-day leader training courses in 1972.

The section structure that emerged from coeducation reassigned the 1959 section names (introduced when the camp was still boys-only) along gender lines: Pathfinders and Pioneers serve girls and non-binary campers; Coureurs des Bois and Woodsmen serve boys and non-binary campers; Senior Kanawanians and Voyageurs are co-ed groups.

**Bruce Netherwood** served as Director from 1988-1994, overseeing the camp's **centennial celebrations in 1994**, marking 100 years since Billy Ball's first organized trip in 1894. A documentary film, *Kamp Kanawana: The Experience that Lasts a Lifetime* (1993, directed by Cathy Reeves), was produced for the centennial. A commemorative poster, alumni newsletter (*The Look Out*, Vol. 1, No. 3), and local newspaper coverage (including French-language press in *Le Devoir*) documented the milestone.

### **Year-Round Programming and Contemporary Era (1996-Present)**

Beginning in 1996, Kanawana offered year-round residential programming. In 1997, the **Kanawana-Be programme** launched for ages 5-6, lowering entry age. In 1998, the **Adventurer programme** introduced a one-month intensive canoe camping trip.

**Sean Day** served as Director and Regional Director of Camps for Quebec YMCAs (2004-2023), navigating COVID-19 challenges (overnight camp closed summers 2021-2022). In **2022, the Mountaineer programme** launched—an all-gender tent providing a safe environment for transgender, non-binary, and gender non-conforming campers, representing a conceptual evolution from binary coeducation.

The camp operates as a technology-free facility with no cellphones or Wi-Fi. Pre-COVID enrollment reached approximately 900 campers per year across regular, leadership, and expedition streams (12-48 days). Current programming includes water trampolining, paddle boarding, archery, rope courses, rock climbing, theatre, music, arts and crafts, nature exploration, dance, and survival techniques. In **2024, a new "Kanawana Adventure" day camp** program launched at the site—the first day camp there. Environmental stewardship is woven through all programming.

### **Physical Site**

The 550-acre Saint-Sauveur site (673 Saint-Elmire Street, ~90km north of Montreal) sits in the Laurentian Mountains on traditional Anishinaabe (Algonquin) territory. The property features three private lakes (Kanawana, Wilson—formerly called Lac Desjardins—and Round Lake) connected by a dam. In **2018, Desjardins donated $1 million** for renovations including a new Desjardins Pavilion. Modern environmental infrastructure includes 16 Clivus Multrum composting units and 32 dry toilets. The **superintendent's house burned** in a total loss fire on May 16, 2023, with no injuries.

### **Institutional Context**

Camp Kanawana was established by the **YMCA of Montreal**, founded November 25, 1851—the first YMCA in North America. The YMCA organized Sir George Williams College in 1926 (chartered as a university in 1948), which merged with Loyola College in 1974 to form Concordia University. The Concordia University Archives now hold the most comprehensive Camp Kanawana historical records (YMCA of Montreal fonds, P0145). In January 2009, the organization rebranded as **YMCAs of Quebec** (Les YMCA du Québec). Camp Kanawana remains the organization's sole residential camp.
