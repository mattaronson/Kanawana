### New Findings (Contextual -- Not Kanawana-Specific)

**1. William Shatner as Ghost Storyteller at Camp B'nai Brith (Laurentians, ~1948-49)**

The most interesting parallel discovery. Around 1948-49, an 18-year-old William Shatner worked as a volunteer counsellor at **Camp B'nai Brith** in **Sainte-Agathe-des-Monts** -- about 30 km from Kanawana in the same Laurentian mountains. He earned the respect of underprivileged teenage boys specifically through **acting out ghost stories**, going bunk to bunk each night. He credits this experience with pushing him toward acting. Sources: *Globe and Mail* (August 2025), *Maclean's*. This confirms that ghost storytelling was an active tradition at Laurentian summer camps in the late 1940s -- contemporaneous with Kanawana's operation. However, this is about a different camp and cannot be attributed to Kanawana without evidence of cross-pollination.

**2. YMCA Camp Director on "Legends and Lores" Trend**

Jon McLaren, executive director of YMCA Camp Minikani (Wisconsin), described a broader YMCA camp trend of rebranding ghost stories as "legends and lores" -- not calling them scary stories, but using them as storytelling pedagogy. This provides useful context for how YMCA camps (including Kanawana) may have evolved their approach to campfire storytelling. Source: *Slate* (2019, already cited).

**3. Additional Quebec Folklore Candidates for Campfire Repertoire**

The article already covers La Chasse-Galerie, Le Loup-Garou, and Le Beau Danseur. This round confirmed two additional Quebec legends that are strong candidates for Kanawana campfire storytelling but for which no direct Kanawana connection was found:
- **La Dame Blanche** (White Lady of Montmorency Falls): Ghost of a bride whose fiance was killed at the Battle of Beauport (1759)
- **La Corriveau** (Marie-Josephte Corriveau, 1761): Murderess whose body was displayed in a gibbet; one of Quebec's most enduring ghost stories

**4. Loup-Garou Additional Detail**

The Quebec version is distinctive: transformation results from religious transgression (missing Mass for 7 consecutive Sundays), not from being bitten. The *Quebec Gazette* reported a werewolf attack on July 21, 1766, at St. Rock near Cap Mouraska -- one of the earliest documented "sightings" in North American journalism.

**5. Reader's Digest Kanawana Alumni Story**

Charles "Smillie" Buckland attended Camp Kanawana 1944-1951. His memoir describes being the "first recipient of the coveted Green Triangle." No ghost story or legend content in his account, but confirms camp culture details of the era (train from Montreal Central Station, bus up gravel road to camp).
