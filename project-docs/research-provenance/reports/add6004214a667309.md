### Summary of Substantive Findings

1. **The transition date remains unknown.** No web search produced a specific date for when Kanawana switched from "Each for all and all for each" to "Non Nobis Solum." The existing KB data (from the McMorris thesis) establishes a window: the English motto is documented in 1922 and 1927; the Latin motto is first attested in the 1993 video. The transition occurred somewhere in that 66-year window.

2. **The 1935 "History of Kamp Kanawana" document** exists on Internet Archive but could not be fetched. This is a critical source -- if it still uses "Each for all and all for each," that narrows the window to post-1935. If it uses "Non Nobis Solum," the transition happened very early.

3. **Lower Canada College connection is the strongest lead.** LCC founder Charles Sanderson Fosbery adopted "Non Nobis Solum" from his family crest when he founded LCC in 1909. The LCC school song "Non Nobis Solum" was first sung in 1936. Both institutions served anglophone Montreal boys. Stuart McLean (attended LCC, then worked at Kanawana from 1969) is the only documented individual bridge, but the social overlap between LCC families and YMCA camp families in anglophone Montreal was likely much broader.

4. **"Each for all and all for each" was a shared YMCA tradition**, not unique to Kanawana. Camp Becket (Massachusetts, est. 1903) and Camp Hayo-Went-Ha (Michigan, est. 1904) both used/use the same motto. Kanawana's adoption of the Latin motto was therefore an act of differentiation.

5. **"Non Nobis Solum" is unique to Kanawana within the YMCA system worldwide.** No other YMCA camp or branch uses this motto. It is shared with schools (LCC, Massanutten Academy, Walnut Hill, Pennthorpe, Washburn, Willamette), military units, and heraldic families, but not other YMCAs.

6. **The 1941 CFCF radio broadcast** and the **1980 Ka-News newsletter** on Internet Archive are potentially important sources for narrowing the motto transition window, but could not be fetched.

7. **No search (17 total queries across multiple strategies) produced the specific transition date or a documented reason for the change.** This remains a Critical open question. The most promising unexplored sources are: the 1935 history document, the 1941 radio broadcast, the 1980 Ka-News, and the full McMorris thesis (which may contain more detail than the fact extracted from it).
