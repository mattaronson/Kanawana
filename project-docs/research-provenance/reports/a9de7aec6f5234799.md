### 1. "Oldest Camp in Canada" -- Popular Misconception (CONFIRMED FALSE)

Camp Kanawana is sometimes described as "the oldest camp in Canada," but this is inaccurate. The documented ranking is:

| Camp | Founded | Location | Notes |
|------|---------|----------|-------|
| **Big Cove YMCA Camp** | **1889** | Merigomish Harbour, NS | Oldest residential camp in Canada |
| Camp Keewaydin | 1893 | Ontario | Some sources say tied with Kanawana |
| **Camp Jubilee/Kanawana** | **1894** | Lake St. Joseph, then Saint-Sauveur, QC | Quebec's oldest; Canada's second-oldest |

The 1993 YMCA promotional film (*Kamp Kanawana: The Experience that Lasts a Lifetime*) gets it right: "the oldest camp in Quebec and second oldest in Canada." The YMCA's current website also uses the accurate claim. The "oldest in Canada" version appears to circulate in informal camp lore.

**Key source**: Wikipedia "Big Cove YMCA Camp"; Ottertooth.com oldest camps list; Concordia Archives film description.

### 2. The Meaning of "Kanawana" -- Unresolved Mystery

This is the most fascinating finding. The KB already contains three critical facts from the McMorris thesis:

- **f_0248**: A 1951 camp history document says "Kanawana" means **"lots to eat"** in an Indigenous language.
- **f_0249**: Ralph Dawson's 1933 history says the meaning was **not learned until years after the name was adopted**.
- **f_0250**: The specific Indigenous language has **never been identified** in any source.

My extensive searching across Algonquin dictionaries, Algonquian word lists, Native Languages of the Americas databases, and linguistic resources turned up **nothing**. The word "Kanawana" does not appear in any standard Indigenous language dictionary available online.

**Possible connections**:
- **"Kanawha"** (West Virginia river): Generally means "white rocks" in Shawnee/Delaware, with some sources suggesting "new water" or "water way." The Kanawha Indian band was part of the Algonquin family. This is the closest documented toponym.
- **"Kanawa"** (Cariban/Arawakan): Means "dugout canoe" -- the root of the English word "canoe." Different language family entirely (South American), so likely coincidental.

The naming may also reflect early 20th century camps adopting pseudo-Indigenous names without rigorous linguistic basis, a practice McMorris examines in her "playing Indian" thesis chapter.

**Highest-priority sources to resolve this**: Ralph Dawson's 1933 history; R.L. Charlton's 1943 "Early Days" account; McMorris thesis full text (all in Concordia Archives).

### 4. The Cropsey Legend -- Camp Ghost Story Archetype

The **Cropsey** (also "Cropsy," "Cropsey Maniac") legend is the most widespread camp ghost story in northeastern North America:

- **Origin**: New York area, multiple versions involving a deranged figure with an axe or hook hand who stalks campers
- **Common variants**: (1) Camp caretaker burned in a prank, returns for revenge; (2) Father whose child died in a camp fire; (3) Escaped asylum patient; (4) Ghost of a boy who died at camp
- **Real-life convergence**: On Staten Island, the legend merged with the actual crimes of **Andre Rand**, a former Willowbrook State School janitor convicted of kidnapping children (1970s-80s)
- **Pop culture**: 1981 film *The Burning* (produced by Harvey and Bob Weinstein); 2009 documentary *Cropsey*
- **Camp function**: Used by counselors to reinforce safety rules ("stay out of the woods," "don't wander alone")
- **Variants at other camps**: Camp IHC has "Cropsey," Camp Minikani has "Mud Lake Monster" and "Grandpappy"

**"Chopsy" as Kanawana variant**: The name "Chopsy" (suggesting "chopping") follows the same pattern as other Cropsey variants where the name mutates across camps while retaining the core narrative (threatening figure + bladed weapon + camp setting). Oral history is needed to document the specific Kanawana version.

### 5. Camp Founding Mythology vs. Documented History

The standard narrative -- "Billy Ball took boys to Lake St. Joseph in 1894" -- is the official YMCA version but oversimplifies the founding. Key complications already in the KB:

- An 1893 exploration journal documents the "first Montreal YMCA venture to explore the country round about St. Agathe with the view of securing a lake" (Concordia Archives, Series 12A)
- The Permanent Camp Committee minutes begin in 1895
- The relationship between D.A. Budge (YMCA Secretary General), "Billy Ball," and the Cushing family remains poorly documented
- The camp was originally "Camp Jubilee" (named for the YMCA's 50th anniversary in 1894), then moved to the Saint-Sauveur site in 1910 and became "Kamp Kanawana"

### 6. "Playing Indian" -- Colonial Appropriation at Kanawana

The cached sources reveal extensive Indigenous-themed programming with no authentic Indigenous connection:

- **1922**: Kanawana became "a chartered tribe in the Woodcraft League of America" (brochure); Council Ring built by senior campers
- **1923**: "Real Indian dancing at Council Ring" (Gas Bag); Council Ring used for closing ceremonies
- **1927**: Totem pole added; teepee built from old canvas
- **1935**: "Council of the Tribes of Kanawana" held (1935 history)
- **Maps**: "Indian Grave" marking and Indian characters depicted on camp maps

This was part of a continent-wide movement. Ernest Thompson Seton created the Indian Council Ring at Taylor Statten's camp in 1922, and the practice spread to camps across Canada. The McMorris thesis (2023) devotes an entire chapter to "playing Indian" at Kanawana, examining its intersection with colonialism, religion, and national identity construction.

### 7. Quebec Folklore Context

Quebec folklore traditions that would have been part of Kanawana's campfire culture:

- **La Chasse-Galerie** (The Flying Canoe): Lumberjacks make a devil's pact to fly home in a canoe over the Laurentians on New Year's Eve. Especially resonant given Kanawana's canoe culture and Laurentian setting.
- **Le Loup-Garou**: A person who misses Easter confession for seven years transforms into a beast at night.
- **Le Beau Danseur**: The devil appears at a dance as a handsome stranger (200+ versions across Quebec).
- **Ti-Jean**: French-Canadian folk hero, clever trickster.
- **The Dungarvon Whooper** (New Brunswick): A murdered camp cook whose cries echo through the woods.
