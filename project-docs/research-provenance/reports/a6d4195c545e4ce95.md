# McMorris Thesis: Answers to Wiki Open Questions

## founding-1894.md

**Q1: When was the name 'Kanawana' adopted?**

McMorris states the camp has been "known as Kamp Kanawana since 1910" (Introduction, p.3). The 1910 date coincides with the move from Lac St. Joseph to Saint-Sauveur. The naming may not have been immediate -- Ralph Dawson's history says the meaning "was not learned until years after the name was adopted." The name was adopted circa 1910.

**Q2: Who was camp director between John Roy (1901) and Philip Paterson (1923)?**

McMorris provides: Harold Cross was camp director "in the 1920s" (Introduction, p.7); in 1925, Cross as director introduced "Kickers and Knockers" council groups; in 1927, Cross noted totem pole building. Between Roy and Cross, the thesis does not name any directors explicitly for the 1901-1920 gap. However, the brochures already document G.D. Brandon (1921) and Lemuel Ereaux (1922).

**Q3: What happened to the Lake St. Joseph islands after the move to Saint-Sauveur?**

NOT DIRECTLY ANSWERED. The thesis mentions the Lake St. Joseph property was 33 acres purchased in 1898 for $1/acre from the provincial government. The camp there continued as Camp Otoreke (renamed from Jubilee 1909) for adult/family use. McMorris does not detail the fate of the islands specifically.

**Q4: What was the Lac St. Joseph camp like physically?**

McMorris describes a "simple" setting with "a half dozen sleeping tents, a large dining marquee, and a few 'primitive boats' rented from local farmers" (Introduction, p.20). Campers took the train to Sainte-Agathe, then a "two-hour drive by horse and springless buckboard" to the site (Cross, p.190). The property was 33 acres purchased from the government in 1898 for $1/acre.

## harold-cross.md

**Q5: What role did Cross play at Kanawana specifically? Was he responsible for 'playing Indian' programming?**

YES. McMorris confirms Cross was camp director in the 1920s. In 1927, he "proudly noted that one of that summer's special projects was building a totem pole for council ring" (Ch.2, p.60). In 1925, he introduced the "Kickers and Knockers" camp council system. His book is confirmed as *One Hundred Years of Service with Youth: The Story of the Montreal Y.M.C.A*, published by Association Press, Montreal, 1951.

**Q6: Was the book published for public distribution?**

McMorris cites it as: "Harold C. Cross, *One Hundred Years of Service with Youth: The Story of the Montreal Y.M.C.A* (Montreal: Association Press, 1951)." The publisher "Association Press" was the YMCA's own publishing arm. This confirms it was a published book (not just a manuscript), though through an in-house YMCA publisher.

## centennial-1967.md

**Q7: What was the itinerary and duration?**

McMorris Ch.3 provides significant detail: six boys and two counsellors paddled 150 miles from August 2-9 (8 days). The route ran from Deep River, Ontario to Britannia Beach on the west side of Ottawa. The trip report was written by Yves St. Pierre, one of the trip leaders. Deep River is described as "the oldest and most well established site of ritual baptism along fur trades" (citing Podruchny). The group was greeted by "a busload of campers singing the Kanawana song." The Ottawa Journal covered the arrival on August 10, 1967.

## myths-and-legends.md

**Q8: Does the McMorris thesis contain information about the meaning of 'Kanawana'?**

YES, extensively. Ch.2, p.76: According to a former camper's account, "Kanawana means in the Indian language 'lots to eat.'" A different account says "this meaning was not learned until years after the name was adopted." McMorris notes: "According to an online dictionary, the word 'Kanawana' does not exist in Kanien'keha (Mohawk)." The closest word is "kanawa" meaning "swamp." McMorris also notes the name "does not sound so different from Kahnawake, the Mohawk reserve near Montreal which was widely known as Caughnawaga through most of this period." Wall suggests many camps "were happy enough if the overall effect was an Indian tone."

## traditions-and-culture.md

**Q9: What are the details of the firelighting ceremony (1936) and fire of friendship (1939)?**

McMorris mentions the "Fire of Friendship" ceremony. In 1942, Kanawana "welcomed the Boys of Tamaracouta" with a "programme that featured fire-lighting, hymns, and prayers." The Fire of Friendship programme included a direction to be used "at the end of a Boy Scout day," suggesting some Boy Scout influence. No detailed description of the specific ceremony format is provided beyond these elements.

**Q10: When did the camp adopt 'Non Nobis Solum'?**

McMorris documents two mottos: In 1927, a staff meeting decided the camp motto was "each for all and all for each." The 1993 promotional video narrator states the camp motto was "non nobis solum" meaning "not for ourselves alone." McMorris notes "This is still the camp motto, as seen on Kanawana's Facebook page." The transition date is not specified but occurred between 1927 and 1993.

## council-ring.md

**Q11: Does the Council Ring still exist?**

NOT DIRECTLY ANSWERED for current state. McMorris confirms: built 1922 by senior campers; totem pole added 1927; rebuilt 1929 to seat 325 people. Location described as "situated between the cabins and the chapel."

**Q12: What was the specific format of the Council of Tribes ceremony?**

YES. McMorris provides detailed pageant scripts. The typical format included: torch parade to council ring, peace pipe ceremony, Indian dance, story on "moral nature, generally pertaining to the North American Indian" (1939 report). The "Tribes of Kanawana in Council" script featured a Big Chief, four Little Chiefs (representing Ojibways, Tuscaroras, Algonquins, Cayugas), named braves who challenged each other to competitions (Indian leg wrestling, neck pulling, crab walking), ending with the Big Chief calling for the Kanawana camp song and a closing prayer/chant ("Wakonda, dhe-dhu, wapdhin atonhe"). The evening concluded with the Big Chief saying "the time has come when we must close our Council Ring and retire back to our wigwams."

## directors-index.md

**Q13: Is 'Greig Macdiarmid' the same as 'W.J.G. MacDiarmid'?**

Almost certainly yes. McMorris refers to "W.J.G. MacDiarmid" as camp director in 1936 and 1937, and quotes his season reports. The 1935 History and Green Triangle refer to "Greig Macdiarmid." These cover overlapping/adjacent years (1935-1938), confirming they are the same person. "Greig" likely maps to the "G" initial in W.J.G.

**Q14: Who was R.H. Hanagan?**

McMorris confirms R.H. Hanagan as camp director. He wrote the 1942 season report ("49 Years of YMCA Camping"), managed the 1946 polio outbreak (writing a letter to parents Aug 19, 1946), and was listed as "Montreal YMCA camp director."

**Q15: Who was A. Ross Seaman?**

McMorris mentions A. Ross Seaman as camp director who wrote the 1959, 1960, and 1962 season reports, and the 1964 camping letter. He also wrote the Aug 1, 1967 letter to parents about the Centennial canoe trip. His tenure spans at least 1959-1967.

**Q20: When exactly did L&V start?**

McMorris cites a 1988 Kanawana Camping Task Force document stating Lumberman-Voyageur Week was created in 1947. A L&V mural was in the dining hall by 1950.

## section-names.md

**Q21: Did the Bantam name fall out of use at the 1959 renaming?**

YES. McMorris Ch.3: "In 1959, instead of Bantam, Junior, Intermediate, and Senior, campers were now Pioneers (8-9 year olds), Woodsmen (10-11), Coureurs de Bois (12-13), and Pathfinders (13-16). These section titles are still in use today."

## coeducation-gender.md

**Q22: When did girls first attend?**

McMorris Conclusion: "In 1968, Kanawana invited girls between the ages of 10 and 12 to come to camp for the first time with the boys." Confirmed by YMCA Annual Report footnote. Note: this is 1968, not 1967 or 1969 as some sources suggest.

## wartime-kanawana.md

**Q23: Details on health scares?**

1941: scarlet fever, camp quarantined for a month. 1944: mumps during 6th and 7th week. 1946: six cases of polio; Dr. Cushing (pediatrician) visited; camp did not close, only cancelled trips and visits; three boys discharged from hospital by end of summer.

## canoe-trips.md

**Q24: What routes did earliest trips follow?**

1925: trips introduced for extra $3.50. 1936: first trips north to Lake Archambault. 1937: eight trips to Archambault. By 1945, intermediate trips went to Lachute (3 days), seniors to Ile Perrot/Grenville (7-8 days) or Ottawa (10 days). 1950: six trips per week on Lake Archambault and Ottawa/Pembina rivers. 1959: Rouge, North rivers, Lake Kiamika, Taureau. 1959: La Verendrye base camp established on 25-acre lease at Lac Landron. 1964: 20+ trips from La Verendrye.
