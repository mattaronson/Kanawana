### EXTRACTED FACTS

#### 1. Which Indigenous Nation(s) Traditionally Occupied the Laurentians

**The Weskarini Algonquin** (also known as Wawackeciriniwak, "people of the deer-clan"; La Petite Nation; Little Nation; Ouescharini) were the primary Indigenous people of the Laurentians region where Camp Kanawana is located.

- They were cousins of the Kichesipirini (Kichespirinis), both branches of the Lower Algonquin, sharing the same language group but speaking different dialects.
- Source: [Morin Heights Historical Association](https://morinheightshistory.org/the-indigenous-people-of-the-laurentians/); [Wikipedia: Weskarini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weskarini_Algonquin_First_Nation)

**Pre-Weskarini context -- the St. Lawrence Iroquoians**: Before the Algonquin period, the St. Lawrence Iroquoians (a distinct Iroquoian-speaking people, not the Five Nations Iroquois) occupied territories along the St. Lawrence including the village of Hochelaga (Montreal). They mysteriously disappeared between Jacques Cartier's visit in 1535-1541 and Champlain's arrival in 1603, likely due to disease, warfare, and climate change. Their disappearance left a vacuum that the Algonquin peoples filled in the lower Ottawa and northern St. Lawrence regions.
- Source: [Wikipedia: St. Lawrence Iroquoians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Lawrence_Iroquoians); [Globe and Mail](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/canada/article-carbon-dating-archeology-montreal-st-lawrence-iroquoians/)

#### 2. Historical Territory Boundaries

**The Weskarini lived along four river systems:**
1. **Riviere Lievre** (Hare River)
2. **Riviere Petite-Nation** (named after the Weskarini themselves -- "La Petite Nation")
3. **Riviere Rouge** (Rouge River)
4. **Riviere du Nord** (North River) -- **this is the watershed where Camp Kanawana is located**

- Source: [Morin Heights Historical Association](https://morinheightshistory.org/the-indigenous-people-of-the-laurentians/); search result summaries from multiple sources

**Their territory was on the north side of the Ottawa River**, extending inland (northward) along these tributaries into the Laurentian mountains. Their principal summer encampment was at the mouth of the Petite-Nation River at what is now Montebello, which was probably a permanent camp.

**Broader Algonquin band territories along the Ottawa River** (from east to west):
- **Weskarini** (Petite Nation) -- Lievre, Petite-Nation, Rouge, and North rivers
- **Kichesipirini** ("Big River people") -- the largest and most powerful, centered on Morrison Island in the Ottawa River
- **Matouweskarini** ("Madawaska people") -- along the Madawaska River
- **Onontchataronon** ("people of Iroquet") -- along the South Nation River
- **Kinounchepirini** -- on the Ottawa below Allumette Island
- **Ottagoutowuemin** -- on the Ottawa above Allumette Island
- Source: [Wikipedia: Weskarini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weskarini_Algonquin_First_Nation); [Kronoskaf](https://www.kronoskaf.com/syw/index.php?title=Algonquin_People); [Ottawa River Heritage PDF](https://ottawariver.org/pdf/05-ch2-3.pdf)

#### 4. Seasonal Movement Patterns

The Weskarini were semi-nomadic, following a seasonal cycle:

- **Summer (before 1600)**: Gathered "in large numbers on the Ottawa River" at communal encampments, particularly at the mouth of the Petite-Nation River at Montebello. Activities included fishing, socializing, trade, and ceremonies.
- **Autumn**: "Returned upriver on the tributaries to spend the winter in small family groups along the shores of their lakes and the valleys of the rivers." This means they would have traveled up the Riviere du Nord (among other tributaries) into the interior of the Laurentians.
- **Winter**: Small family groups dispersed across the landscape to hunt terrestrial mammals (deer, elk, beaver, bear). The Weskarini name itself -- Wawackeciriniwak, "people of the deer-clan" -- reflects the centrality of deer hunting.
- **Spring**: Maple syrup tapping; goose hunting in March.
- **Specific seasonal calendar**: October-November: trout and whitefish spawning (prime fishing); March: goose hunting; June-August: berry gathering; September: corn hulling.
- Source: [Morin Heights Historical Association](https://morinheightshistory.org/the-indigenous-people-of-the-laurentians/); [Wikipedia: Weskarini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weskarini_Algonquin_First_Nation)

**Key implication for Camp Kanawana**: The pattern of winter dispersal into small family camps along lakes and rivers in the Laurentian interior means the area around Lac Kanawana (on the Riviere du Nord watershed) would likely have been used as winter hunting grounds by Weskarini family groups.

#### 5. Relationships Between Different Algonquin Bands

- The Weskarini and Kichesipirini were **cousins** -- branches of the Lower Algonquin with different dialects.
- The Weskarini had **good trade relations with the Wyandot (Huron)** before and during the early colonial period.
- Chief **Iroquet**, leader of the Weskarini at European contact (1609-1613), had close ties to the Huron leader Ochasteguin and the Arendahuronon people. He usually spent winters in Wendat (Huron) territory.
- The Weskarini were also connected to the **Onontchataronon** (sometimes called "people of Iroquet" after the Weskarini chief).
- By the early 1640s, under Iroquois pressure, the Weskarini were **compelled to seek refuge with the Kichesipirini**, whose territory had not yet been attacked.
- Source: [Wikipedia: Weskarini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weskarini_Algonquin_First_Nation); [Kronoskaf](https://www.kronoskaf.com/syw/index.php?title=Algonquin_People)

#### 6. Colonial-Era Contact and Displacement History

**First Contact (1609-1613)**:
- In 1609, the Algonquin and Innu invited Champlain to ally with them and the Wendat against the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois). Champlain agreed and participated in the original 1609 raid.
- In 1613, when Champlain made his first voyage up the Ottawa River, the chief of the Weskarini was **Iroquet**.
- Champlain arranged for **Etienne Brule**, an 18-year-old in his entourage, to spend the winter with Iroquet's people.
- The Weskarini traded at the mouths of the St. Maurice, Dumoine, and Gatineau rivers.
- Many Weskarini intermarried with French settlers.

**Beaver Wars and Displacement (1640s-1650s)**:
- The Iroquois (particularly Mohawk), seeking control of fur trade routes, launched devastating attacks on Algonquin and Huron peoples.
- By the early 1640s, the Weskarini were forced to seek refuge with the Kichesipirini.
- Many Weskarini moved to **Trois-Rivieres** to seek help from the Jesuits, who offered protection only to those who converted to Catholicism.
- Haudenosaunee war parties "roamed the length of the Ottawa River during the 1650s and 1660s, making travel extremely dangerous."

**The 1653 Massacre at Petit Lac Nominingue**:
- During a major Iroquois offensive in 1652-1653, the main band of Weskarini fled north from the Ottawa River into their ancestral interior territory.
- They were cornered and **massacred on the shores of Petit Lac Nominingue**, at the source of the Petite-Nation River, between the upper valleys of the Petite-Nation and Rouge rivers, near their sacred Mont Tremblant.
- The massacre was "without mercy."
- Some survived because they were elsewhere (e.g., at Trois-Rivieres), such as **Marie Anaraoui Mite8ameg8k8e**.
- Source: [Morin Heights Historical Association](https://morinheightshistory.org/the-indigenous-people-of-the-laurentians/); [grandquebec.com](https://grandquebec.com/outaouais/petite-nation/)

**Post-Massacre Dispersal**:
- Many survivors and descendants settled at **Kitigan-Zibi-Anishinabeg reserve at Maniwaki**.
- Others spent their summers at the **mission of La Montagne (Kanesatake/Oka)**, though they still returned to their hunting areas in the Ottawa River valley at other times of year.
- The Sulpician mission at Kanesatake (Oka) housed Algonquin, Nipissing, and Mohawk peoples. By 1840 it had approximately 1,000 inhabitants.
- Source: [Wikipedia: Weskarini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weskarini_Algonquin_First_Nation); [Familypedia](https://familypedia.fandom.com/wiki/Weskarini_Algonquin_First_Nation)

**The Great Peace of Montreal (1701)**:
- Signed August 4, 1701, between New France and 39 First Nations, ending the Beaver Wars.
- Algonquins and Nipissings were among the 1,300 representatives present.
- Source: [Wikipedia: Great Peace of Montreal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Peace_of_Montreal)

#### 7. Sacred Sites in the Region

**Mont Tremblant** (approximately 80 km north of Camp Kanawana):
- Algonquin name: **Manitonga Soutana** ("mountain of the spirits") or **Manitou Ewitchi Saga** ("mountain of the great god")
- The Weskarini believed the Great Spirit **Glitche Manitou** caused the mountain to shake when nature was disturbed (hence the French translation "Mont Tremblant" -- trembling mountain).
- It was "the highest summit in the region, an important place of passage where ceremonies were held."
- Source: [Tremblant.ca](https://www.tremblant.ca/mountain-village/mountain-info); [Wikipedia: Mont Tremblant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mont_Tremblant)

**Nominingue (Onamaning)**:
- The name comes from an Algonquin word meaning "vermilion" or "red ochre" -- "Onamani Sakaigan" would be translated as "Vermilion Lake."
- Red ochre was culturally and spiritually significant to Algonquin peoples, used for ritual and ceremonial purposes.
- Source: [comeexplorecanada.com](https://www.comeexplorecanada.com/quebec/nominingue); [QAHN](https://qahn.org/article/nominingue-its-history-and-heritage)

#### 8. Archaeological Evidence

- **No specific archaeological sites at or near Lac Kanawana/Saint-Sauveur** were found in the search results.
- The **Laurentian Archaic** material culture tradition (lasting approximately 4,000 years) is found throughout the St. Lawrence Valley. Stone points known as "Otter Creek" confirm early inhabitants were part of this broader culture.
- A **15th-century dugout canoe** was found in a lake in the Laurentians in the mid-1980s by amateur divers -- one of only about ten surviving prehistoric Indigenous dugout canoes in Quebec.
- **Leamy Lake Park** (in the Ottawa River drainage basin) contains the largest complex of pre-contact archaeological sites in the watershed, used continuously for more than 6,000 years.
- **Morrison Island** (Kichesipirini territory) reveals a 1,000-year-old culture that manufactured copper tools and weapons.
- Common Algonquin artifacts include scrapers, arrowheads, knives, axes/celts, pottery.
- Around **300,000 artifacts** (arrowheads, pots, pipe bowls, tools for making canoes) have been catalogued from ancient Algonquin collections.
- Source: [NCC](https://ncc-ccn.gc.ca/blog/five-artifacts-that-reveal-a-lot); [Archeo-Quebec](https://www.archeoquebec.com/en/archaeology-quebec/thematic-files/prehistoric-quebec)

#### 9. Current Status and Unceded Territory

- **Algonquin territory has never been dealt with by a land-sharing Treaty**, and Algonquin title continues to exist. The land where Camp Kanawana sits is unceded Algonquin territory.
- Nine Algonquin communities exist in Quebec today: **Kitigan Zibi, Barriere Lake, Kitcisakik, Lac Simon, Abitibiwinni, Long Point, Timiskaming, Kebaowek, and Wolf Lake**.
- **Kitigan Zibi Anishinabeg** (near Maniwaki) filed major land claims in 2025 seeking legal recognition of Aboriginal title over eight areas of unceded territory.
- Many modern **Weskarini descendants** (some identifying as Metis, others as Weskarini) work to rebuild knowledge of the Algonquin language and shared cultural identity. They have concerns about ongoing development of sacred Manitonga Soutana (Mont Tremblant), destruction of land and water in unceded territory, and appropriation of the Weskarini name.
- Source: [CBC News](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/ottawa/kitigan-zibi-anishinabeg-land-title-case-9.6962873); [Canada's History](https://www.canadashistory.ca/explore/politics-law/algonquin-territory)

### SUMMARY OF KEY FACTS FOR THE KB

The land where Camp Kanawana sits -- on Lac Kanawana (formerly Lac Brule), in the Riviere du Nord watershed near Saint-Sauveur-des-Monts -- is **confirmed unceded Weskarini Algonquin territory**. The Weskarini occupied four river systems including the Riviere du Nord. Their seasonal pattern of summer gatherings on the Ottawa River followed by autumn dispersal into small family winter hunting camps along interior lakes and rivers means the Kanawana area was likely used for winter hunting. The Weskarini were devastated by the 1653 Iroquois massacre at Petit Lac Nominingue, approximately 100 km north, and survivors dispersed to Kitigan Zibi (Maniwaki) and Kanesatake (Oka). This territory remains unceded and has never been subject to a land-sharing treaty.
