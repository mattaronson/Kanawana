## 1. Ernest Thompson Seton, "Woodcraft Indians," YMCA Camps, Canada

**Key findings:**

Ernest Thompson Seton (1860-1946) was born in England, raised in Toronto, Canada (from age 6), and became the foundational figure linking woodcraft, Indigenous-inspired programming, and organized camping across North America.

- **1901-1902:** Seton founded the Woodcraft Indians at his estate in Cos Cob, Connecticut, after inviting local boys who had been vandalizing his property to a weekend of nature stories and outdoor skills. He published articles in *Ladies' Home Journal* on woodcraft and "Indian-style" camping.
- **The Birch Bark Roll of the Woodcraft Indians** (1906) and **The Book of Woodcraft and Indian Lore** (1912) became foundational texts, containing sections on "Woodland Songs, Dances and Ceremonies," campfire stories, "Some Indian Ways," and general scouting indoors and outdoors.
- **YMCA connection:** The Laws of the Seton Indians were published in *Association Boys*, a YMCA publication. Almost all Woodcraft Indian "tribes" operated within the YMCA infrastructure. Edgar M. Robinson, Boys' Work Secretary of the International Committee of the YMCA, was a key link -- he helped merge the Woodcraft Indians into the nascent Boy Scouts of America in 1910.
- **Canadian roots:** Seton's admiration for First Nations was strengthened by time spent with nehiyawak (Cree) in Manitoba in the 1880s. In 2022, Parks Canada reviewed Seton's National Historic Person designation due to "colonial assumptions, outdated terminology," noting his organization "contributed to a legacy of stereotyping Indigenous peoples."
- **Camps following Seton:** "Camps following the Woodcraft Program in the United States and Canada were also founded by friends and students of Seton."

## 2. "Council Ring" Summer Camp Tradition -- Origin and History

**Key findings:**

The Council Ring is one of the most enduring and iconic camp traditions, with multiple cultural roots:

- **Ancient precedents:** The circular gathering design traces to Viking village councils (stone circles for public gatherings) and Indigenous North American traditions.
- **Jens Jensen (landscape architecture):** Jensen, a Danish-American landscape architect in Chicago (early-mid 1900s), made the Council Ring his signature design element, evoking both his Viking Danish heritage and Native American egalitarianism.
- **Ernest Thompson Seton (camp tradition):** Seton formalized the Council Ring as a camp ceremony. At the 1910 Silver Bay YMCA camp, Seton led programming that included council fire ceremonies.
- **Taylor Statten and Camp Ahmek (1922):** The critical Canadian link. Taylor Statten, who became Boys' Work Secretary for the Toronto YMCA in 1905, founded Camp Ahmek in Algonquin Park in 1921. **In 1922, at Statten's request, Seton spent the entire summer at Canoe Lake creating Ahmek's own "Indian Lore" program featuring the Indian Council Ring.** The Council Ring was described as "participatory theatre" where the Chief (Statten), counsellors, and campers dressed in "traditional Indian clothing, complete with headdresses and painted faces, to tell stories, play games, and conduct 'traditional' dances around the fire."
- **Glen Bernard Camp (1924-1933):** At this private girls' camp in Muskoka, Ontario, girls composed stories and poems for the weekly Council Ring, published in *The Scroll*.
- **Enduring tradition:** YMCA Camp Coniston has held vespers at a ring nightly since 1911. Camp Ondessonk has held Council Ring ceremonies since 1960.

## 4. "Playing Indian" -- Summer Camp History, Canada

**Key findings -- a rich scholarly literature:**

- **Sharon Wall, "Totem Poles, Teepees, and Token Traditions: 'Playing Indian' at Ontario Summer Camps, 1920-1955"** (*Canadian Historical Review* 86:3, September 2005, pp. 513-544). This foundational article argues that camp "Indian" programming "had little to do with honouring or even understanding Aboriginal peoples and more to do with seeking a balm for the non-Native experience of modernity." It was "a racialized form of antimodernism."
- **Sharon Wall, *The Nurture of Nature: Childhood, Antimodernism, and Ontario Summer Camps, 1920-55*** (UBC Press, 2009/2010). Won the 2010 Clio Prize (Ontario). The book examines how camps "went native," appropriating Indigenous culture because "Indians" were considered "as close to nature as possible." Wall documents the hypocrisy of camps "lamenting the loss of the Indian way of life" while "legitimizing and marketing the authenticity of their particular parcel of nature because of the extolled proximity of their campgrounds to land formerly occupied by indigenous peoples."
- **G. Stanley Hall's recapitulation theory:** Psychologist Hall proposed that children's bio-psychological development "recapitulated the ancient history of mankind." Youth organizations designed programs based on this theory, "positioning tribal societies as primitive and childlike relics of the past."
- **Timing:** The emergence of summer camps coincided with the residential school system in Canada. "During the time when campers were making incredible memories through Indian lore programs and at the Indian Council Ring, it was illegal for Indigenous peoples to practice their own traditions and ceremonies."
- **Georgetown University thesis:** "Fire and Feathers: The Appropriation of Native American Culture in Summer Camp Traditions" argues appropriation persists because "the label 'tradition' has been applied to Council Fire and consequently masks the appropriation."
- **Post-TRC (2015):** Canadian camps have begun reflecting on these origins, though a Canadian Camping Association survey found "no consensus on how to address them."

## 5. Totem Pole -- Summer Camp Tradition, YMCA

**Key findings:**

## 6. YMCA Camp Honor System / Badges, 1920s History

**Key findings:**

- **The Ragger Society (1914):** The most significant YMCA camp honour/recognition system. Founded at Camp Loma Mar, California, by Thomas Caldwell, Boys' Secretary of the Oakland YMCA. Caldwell rejected athletic awards because a boy with disabilities (Charles Von Konnigsburg) would be unable to win. Instead, he awarded blue kerchiefs ("Rags") for moral character, good behavior, and positive attitude. The first Rags cost 10 cents each from Williamson's County Store in Pescadero.
- **Ceremony:** Reflected the legend of King Arthur and the Knights of the Round Table (not initially religious). By 1916, the hymn "I Would Be True" became the Raggers' Creed, introducing the religious dimension. In 1917, the Red Rag ceremony was written, incorporating "a challenge in addition to a recognition."
- **The Rag's design:** Blends the YMCA triangle, the square ("four-square life"), the circle ("circle of friendship"), and the cross (Christianity).
- **Spread:** Spread through Northern California by 1918-1919, then to Southern California (1920), and eventually nationwide. "Several hundred thousand youngsters in YMCA camps have been led blindfolded to Raggers' Point."
- **Camp Dudley (1886):** The oldest YMCA camp took "young honor YMCA members camping" as its initial rationale.

## 9. Summer Camp Conversion/Commitments, YMCA, Christian, Muscular Christianity

**Key findings:**

- **Muscular Christianity (1840s-1920s):** The YMCA was founded on these principles -- "a Christian commitment to health and manliness." The heyday was 1880-1920.
- **Canadian context:** A thesis on the Edmonton YMCA documents how "utilizing Muscular Christianity to inspire men to participate, the greater YMCA from 1880 through 1920 transformed itself from an evangelical organization to a social service provider."
- **Character building:** David I. Macleod, *Building Character in the American Boy: The Boy Scouts, YMCA and Their Forerunners, 1870-1920* (University of Wisconsin Press, 1983) is a key scholarly reference.
- **Camp as conversion tool:** The Ragger ceremony evolved to include "a challenge in addition to a recognition" -- essentially a commitment to Christian values. "There are boys and girls -- adults now -- who will tell you that these ceremonies were the most profound Christian experiences of their lives."
- **Decline of explicit evangelism:** In the 1920s, "widespread discontent with many of the ideals that had flourished during World War I, including muscular Christianity" emerged. The YMCA "became interdenominational and more concerned with promoting morality and good citizenship than a distinctive interpretation of Christianity."
- **Bruce Kidd** has noted the lasting Canadian legacy: efforts to address "youth obesity, drugs, disillusionment and violence still inspire the YMCA and various other corporate foundations to reinvest in sports and cultural languages that echo the muscular Christian intervention."
- **Kanawana thesis:** "An Experience That Lasts a Lifetime: Building Modernity, Man, and Nation at the YMCA of Montreal's Kamp Kanawana, 1894-1967" (Concordia University) explicitly examines how Kanawana built "modernity, man, and nation."

## 10. YMCA Camp Vespers / Devotions -- History and Tradition

**Key findings:**

- **Morning Watch:** Originated in 1812 at Cambridge University, where students committed to spend their first minutes in Bible study and prayer (the "Cambridge Seven"). Formalized for the YMCA by **John R. Mott** in 1893, when the International Committee of the YMCA printed the first edition of *The Morning Watch*. Mott described it: "The true object should be -- to meet God, to hear His voice, to receive guidance and strength."
- **Vespers:** A long-standing evening tradition. At YMCA Camp Coniston, vespers has occurred nightly since 1911 -- "a time for reflection about your day, a time to be calm, and a time of vulnerability where counselors share a life lesson or story."
- **Camp Harrison:** "Morning Watch before breakfast and Vespers after the evening program highlight our Christian theme and bring the camp community together to honor God and Country."
- **Camp Cheerio:** "From starting each morning with Flag Raising to ending each night with Vespers, the daily schedule has remained largely unchanged for over 60 summers."
- **Cabin devotions:** "A YMCA devotion is a story, Bible verse, or parable that reinforces the YMCA character traits of respect, responsibility, honesty, caring, and faith in God."

## 11. "Camp Fire Ceremony" -- Summer Camp History and Tradition

**Key findings:**

- **Frederick Gunn (1861):** The first organized camp included "singing patriotic songs by the fire."
- **Camp Fire Girls (1910):** Founded by Luther Halsey Gulick (who had founded the YMCA Training School's physical education department) and his wife Charlotte. The organization was "heavily influenced by American Indian lore and culture." Gulick was known by his "Indian name" Timanous; Charlotte was Hiiteni (Arapaho). Charlotte developed the "Wohelo" watchword (Work, Health, Love) and modeled "the buckskin ceremonial gown and beaded headband."
- **Scouting Campfire Ash Ceremony:** Traced to Baden-Powell at Brownsea Island (1907). Ashes are mixed from campfire to campfire, with lists going back to 1907.
- **Opening/closing campfires:** Many camps hold these as bookend rituals. The pinecone ceremony (throwing one pine cone in the fire, keeping one as a memento) and torch-passing/Grand March are common.
- **Council Fire as appropriation:** The Georgetown thesis "Fire and Feathers" demonstrates that "the label 'tradition' has been applied to Council Fire and consequently masks the appropriation as simply part of the tradition."
- **ACA centennial recognition:** "Through wars, recessions, triumphs, and feats of accomplishment, the camp experience has remained at its core essentially the same."

## 12. Hedley Dimock -- Camping Research, YMCA

## Synthesis: Camp Kanawana Specifically

**Direct findings on Kanawana:**

1. **Founding (1894):** "Billy" Ball of the YMCA of Montreal took a group of boys to camp on an island in Lake St. Joseph, near Sainte-Agathe, Quebec. The concept was "to take youth away from the hustle and bustle of everyday life and to teach them about group dynamics, team building, and leadership." Initially called Camp Jubilee, with 20 campers.
2. **Move to Saint-Sauveur:** The YMCA acquired a 550-acre site with 3 lakes in the Laurentians. 85 campers registered for the first session.
3. **Traditions:** The camp's competitive games "were inspired by Pinecrest Camp and quickly became an annual Kanawana tradition" (celebrating their 78th edition in 2025). Camp life includes "sitting around a campfire, singing call-and-response songs in the forest."
4. **Motto:** "Non nobis solum" -- not for ourselves alone.
5. **Archival records at Concordia:** Include "Notes re Early Days of YMCA Camps at Lake St. Joseph and Kanawana" by R.L. Charlton (1943), "History of Kamp Kanawana" by Ralph Dawson (1933), "Historical sketches -- Lake St. Joseph" by W.E. Cushing (1943). A 1923 brochure and a 1941 radio broadcast (Station CFCF) are on the Internet Archive.
6. **Thesis:** "An Experience That Lasts a Lifetime: Building Modernity, Man, and Nation at the YMCA of Montreal's Kamp Kanawana, 1894-1967" (Concordia University) -- the most directly relevant scholarly work.
7. **Hedley Dimock connection:** Dimock worked for the Montreal YMCA and Sir George Williams University. His foundational camp research was conducted at Camp Ahmek (1923), where Seton had just created the Indian Lore/Council Ring program (1922). Dimock's influence at the Montreal YMCA would have directly shaped Kanawana's programming philosophy.

## Key Follow-Up Threads Identified

1. **The Concordia thesis** "An Experience That Lasts a Lifetime" is the single most important source for Camp Kanawana specifically. It should be accessed in full through Concordia's repository.
2. **Concordia YMCA of Montreal fonds** (P0145/12B) contains the primary archival material on Kanawana's programs, including director's reports that would document specific traditions (Council Ring, Indian lore, badges, etc.).
3. **Internet Archive items** -- the 1923 brochure, 1935 history, and 1941 radio broadcast may contain details about specific camp traditions.
4. **The Seton-Statten-Dimock-Montreal chain** is the most likely pathway through which Woodcraft/Indian Lore traditions reached Kanawana: Seton influenced Statten (Toronto YMCA -> Camp Ahmek); Dimock researched at Ahmek, then moved to Montreal YMCA/Sir George Williams, directly influencing Kanawana's institutional context.
5. **The Y-Indian Guides program** (1926), co-created by Joe Friday of the Temagami First Nation and Harold Keltner of the St. Louis YMCA, represents a parallel YMCA "playing Indian" tradition. The book *Inappropriation* (Hillmer & Bean) is the key scholarly treatment.
6. **The Ragger program** spread primarily in the western/southern US and may not have reached Canadian YMCA camps, which may have had their own honour systems -- this requires archival investigation.
7. **Sharon Wall's scholarship** (article 2005, book 2009) is the essential Canadian academic framework for understanding "playing Indian" at Ontario camps; equivalent Quebec-focused scholarship may exist in French-language sources.
