## Overview

Camp Oolahwan was a YWCA summer camp for girls founded in 1917 by Mary Susanna Edgar (1889–1973) on Lake Walfred in Sainte-Marguerite-du-Lac-Masson, approximately 100 km north of Montreal in the Laurentian mountains.^1 ^2 ^11 It operated until its final camp season in 2004, making it one of the longest sustained initiatives in YWCA Montreal history.^1 ^11

The YWCA describes it as the first summer camp for girls in Canada, though Camp Northway in Ontario (founded 1906) predates it; the YWCA claim likely refers to the first YWCA-operated girls' camp.^1 ^3 The YWCA Montreal 150th anniversary timeline (2025) dates the camp to 1915, while all other YWCA sources use 1917. Edgar's directorship ran from 1915 to 1919, suggesting a possible precursor or trial season in 1915 before formal establishment on Lake Walfred in 1917.^1 ^12

The property comprised 345 acres with a private lake (Lake Walfred), at coordinates 46.0501°N, 74.0659°W, elevation 369 metres.^11 ^13

## Founding and Direction

Mary Susanna Edgar (born May 23, 1889, Sundridge, Ontario) graduated from the YWCA National Training School in New York City in 1915 and served as YWCA Girls' Work Secretary in Montreal from 1915 to 1919, directing Camp Oolahwan during that period.^3 ^4 Edgar later founded Glen Bernard Camp (1922, with 38 initial campers) and served as first vice-president of the Canadian Camping Association (1936).^3 She authored *Wood-Fire and Candle-Light* (1945) and won first prize in the ACA hymn competition in 1925 for "God Who Touchest Earth with Beauty" (published 1926).^3 She died September 17, 1973 in Toronto. Archival collections are held at Trent University (fonds 82-010) and Queen's University.^4

Ferna G. Halliday, a Queen's University graduate, served as Secretary of YWCA Montreal from 1918 to 1921 and was responsible for girls at Camp Oolahwan during that period.^5 Halliday went on to co-found Camp Ouareau (1922) and Camp Oconto in Tichborne, Ontario (1924, directed until 1949), and was a founding member of the Ontario Camping Association in 1933.^5 Camp Oconto's original dining hall is named "Halliday Hall" in her honour, and the traditions she brought from YWCA work — candlelight ceremonies, classical music, poetry, and tableaux vivants — may have originated at Oolahwan.^5

## Programming

Camp Oolahwan's programming focused on developing healthy minds and bodies through activities designed to encourage self-esteem, build lasting relationships, and promote leadership.^1 Activities included sports, learning new skills, nature appreciation, and campfire singing — including "Land of the Silver Birch" and other folk songs.^1 ^11 The camp's philosophy, rooted in the YWCA's belief since 1915 that young women must acquire self-confidence and the ability to "laugh and have fun," emphasized healthy life habits and empowerment.^1

## Connection to Kanawana

Camp Oolahwan's connection to Camp Kanawana runs through the YMCA/YWCA Montreal institutional network. Both camps were operated by branches of the same parent organization. Direct inter-camp contact is documented: YWCA Camp Oolahwan sent visitors to Kanawana, and in 1946, Kanawana campers were covered in cocoa powder to greet Oolahwan visitors as "Indians."^6

## Closure and Aftermath

The last camp season was summer 2004, attributed to "rising costs and changing times."^11 The YWCA website dates the camp's operation as 1917–2005, likely reflecting the property disposal date rather than the final season.^1 ^11 YWCA Camp Hochelaga in Vermont is described as "the last residential YWCA summer camp," placing Oolahwan's closure within a broader YWCA trend of exiting residential camp operations.^9

The 345-acre property at 54 Chemin d'Entrelacs was sold in 2005 for $1.47 million to Louis Pratt in partnership with Louis Nault.^9 ^11 They opened Érablière Ste-Marguerite (a sugar shack operation using the original 1917 camp building, with over $1.5 million invested in maple syrup equipment, opening February 2019) and Aventure Oolahwan (outdoor activities) on the site.^9 ^14 A luxury vacation cottage development called Club Lac Walfred, built by Habitations Germat / Timber Block Homes, opened on the broader property circa 2015.^10 Surviving traces of Camp Oolahwan include a hiking trail, bird feeder, volleyball court, and the main building with cafeteria. The Association du Lac Walfred adopted an owl logo in memory of Camp Oolahwan's own owl logo.^10

Upon closure, the YWCA launched "Summer Space Day Camp," an urban day camp for girls ages 10–13 sharing Oolahwan's objectives. Programming included dance, circus, theatre, music, yoga, and kickboxing, with workshops focused on countering violence and early sexualization. Summer Space was discontinued circa June 2020.^1 ^15

## Centennial Reunion

In 2017, a centennial reunion was held at Camp Ouareau in Notre-Dame-de-la-Merci, organized by a committee of former campers and counselors.^7 The exact dates of the reunion are disputed: the YWCA gives September 8–10, while the West Island Blog gives August 25–27 (see conflicts.json, c_002).^7 ^8 CBC Radio's "All in a Weekend" aired an 11-minute segment about the anniversary.^16 A Twitter/X account (@campoolahwan, "Camp Oolahwan 100") was created for the occasion.

## Name Etymology

The name "Oolahwan" may derive from the Lakota word *olówaŋ* meaning "song" or "to sing." This is phonetically and semantically consistent, though notably Lakota is not an indigenous language of the Laurentian region (Algonquin territory). The derivation remains speculative.
