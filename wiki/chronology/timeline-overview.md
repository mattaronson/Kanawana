# Timeline Overview: Camp Kanawana Decade by Decade

*Status: draft | Sources: 0 (hub page — synthesizes existing wiki articles; no new sources)*
*Last Updated: 2026-07-11*

## Overview

This is a navigation hub, not a new research article: it draws together what the rest of the wiki already documents, decade by decade, so a reader can see the camp's whole span at a glance and jump to the fuller treatment. Every claim below is already cited in the linked articles — this page adds no new facts. Where a decade has no dedicated article yet, the section below is necessarily thin; that thinness is itself informative; see Open Questions.

## The 1890s–1910s: Founding

Camp Jubilee was founded in 1894 by Billy Ball at Lake Saint-Joseph, in the Township of Howard (now Saint-Adolphe-d'Howard) — Quebec's first residential summer camp and Canada's second-oldest, after Big Cove YMCA Camp (1889). The camp moved to its present Saint-Sauveur site around 1910, adopting the name "Kanawana." Full detail, including the still-unresolved 1902–1920 directorial gap, is in [[chronology/founding-1894|Founding of Camp Kanawana (1894)]]; the original Lake Saint-Joseph site continued independently as [[places/camp-otoreke|Camp Otoreke]].

## The 1920s–1930s: Growth Between the Wars

No dedicated article yet covers this period as a whole (see Workstream C4 in the operator-approved wiki-expansion plan), but it is substantially documented across [[people/directors-index|Directors and Staff of Camp Kanawana]] (the "Staff by Era" section covers 1921–1938 in detail) and [[context/canadian-camping-movement|The Canadian Camping Movement]]. The camp grew from a small tented operation into a sophisticated physical plant — the Council Ring, totem pole, and Woodcraft League charter all date to this stretch — under a succession of directors (Brandon, Ereaux, Paterson, Cross, McEwen, Dawson, Macdiarmid) whose individual tenures are tabulated in the Directors index. Ralph Dawson's 1933 "History of Kamp Kanawana" is the camp's own first historical self-account.

## The 1940s: Wartime

Fully covered in [[chronology/wartime-kanawana|Wartime Kanawana]]: the 1941 season under Howie Langille (48th season, 68 staff, 30+ British evacuees), R.H. Hanagan's 1942 and 1946 directorships, and the 1946 polio outbreak that the camp weathered on medical advice rather than closing.

## The 1950s: The Post-War Gap

The thinnest-documented decade in the camp's history. Nelson McEwen departed in 1947; A. Ross Seaman's directorship is confirmed only from 1959. No director is documented for the intervening decade, and this remains an open question tracked in [[people/directors-index|Directors and Staff of Camp Kanawana]]'s Gap Periods section. What programming continued during these years is not established beyond general institutional continuity.

## The 1960s: Centennial and Coeducation

A. Ross Seaman's directorship (1959–1967) brought the 1959 section renaming and the La Vérendrye canoe-trip expansion. Canada's 1967 Centennial is covered fully in [[chronology/centennial-1967|The 1967 Centennial and Kanawana]] (the CCA Centenary Journey canoe trip to Ottawa). The Co-ed Camping Pilot Project began in 1967, leading to full coeducation by 1968–1969 — see [[context/coeducation-gender|Coeducation and Gender at Kanawana]].

## The 1970s: Consolidating Coeducation

No dedicated article yet. Documented facts: the first all-female Voyageur canoe trip (1972, three years after formal coeducation), Derek Walsh's directorship and Stuart McLean's assistant-directorship (1974–1975), and Dave Twynam's directorship (1979–1980) — see [[people/directors-index|Directors and Staff of Camp Kanawana]] and [[context/coeducation-gender|Coeducation and Gender]]. The SGW/Concordia institutional pipeline (Dimock, Seaman, later Kass) is covered in [[context/sgw-concordia-connection|Sir George Williams / Concordia University and Camp Kanawana]], though its individual milestones mostly predate this decade (1963, 1972).

## The 1980s: Between Centennials

No dedicated article yet, and this decade is one of the thinnest in the directorial record after the 1950s: Nancy Sawyer (assistant, 1980), Peter Goddard (~1981–1985, dates uncertain), and Jay Netherwood (1986–1987) are the only documented directors. See [[people/directors-index|Directors and Staff of Camp Kanawana]]'s Gap Periods section for the specifics of what remains unconfirmed.

## The 1990s: A Second Centennial

Bruce Netherwood's directorship ran through the camp's own Centennial year, 1994 — see [[chronology/centennial-1994|The 1994 Centennial]] for the Labor Day alumni reunion that marked it, and for the separate, easily-confused 1889–1989 YMCA-wide camping centenary that preceded it by five years. In 1995, the directorship split into the still-current two-tier structure (a year-round Executive Director and a three-season on-site Director) — see [[people/directors-index|Directors and Staff of Camp Kanawana]]'s "The Two-Tier Era" section.

## The 2000s–2020s: The Two-Tier Era

No single dedicated article yet narrates this quarter-century as continuous history — it is currently only tabulated, director by director, in [[people/directors-index|Directors and Staff of Camp Kanawana]]'s Two-Tier Era tables. Within it: a major environmental "green shift" (2006–2012, detailed in [[context/environmental-history|Environmental Education and Stewardship at Kanawana]]); Sean Day's long Executive Directorship (2005–2023); Kate Taylor's on-site directorship through the COVID-19 pandemic (2013–2023); the Mountaineer all-gender program (2022); Marie-Pierre Lacasse's succession as Executive Director (2023–) and the launch of Kanawana's first-ever day camp (2024).
