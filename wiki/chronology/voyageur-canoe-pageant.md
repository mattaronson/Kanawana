# The Centennial Voyageur Canoe Pageant (1967)

*Status: E1-reviewed | Sources: 12*
*Last Updated: 2026-03-16*

## Overview

The Centennial Voyageur Canoe Pageant was a competitive canoe race held from May 24 to September 4, 1967, as part of Canada's centennial celebrations.^1 ^2 Ten teams of 10 men — representing eight provinces plus Yukon and Northwest Territories (Prince Edward Island and Newfoundland did not participate) — paddled 3,283 miles (5,283 km) with 60 miles of portaging across 70 portages over 104 days, following the historic fur trade route from Rocky Mountain House, Alberta, to Expo 67 in Montreal.^1 ^2 As of 2012, it holds the Guinness World Record for the longest canoe race in history.^3

The event was organized by the federal Centennial Commission and was distinct from the CCA Centenary Journey, a separate youth camping canoe trip organized by the Canadian Camping Association the same year.^4

## Origins

The idea originated with **Gene Rheaume** of Flin Flon, Manitoba, who in 1963 proposed a two-man canoe race from Edmonton to Montreal.^5 **Norm Tyson**, also of Flin Flon, drafted the formal proposal sent to the Centennial Commission in 1964.^5 **Arnie Charbonneau**, the Commission's project officer, transformed the concept into a voyageur canoe pageant with larger crews, and organized the first planning meeting at the Fort Garry Hotel in Winnipeg on May 14, 1965.^5 **Eric W. Morse**, Canadian wilderness canoeist and historian, helped plan the route drawing on his book *Canoe Routes of the Voyageurs*.^6 **Bill Mason**, who would later become famous as an NFB canoe filmmaker, attended the 1965 planning meeting.^5

## The Canoes

Twelve prototype canoes were built by the **Chestnut Canoe Company** of Fredericton, New Brunswick, in 1966 — 25 feet long, 51 inches wide, 19 inches deep, approximately 235 pounds in traditional wood-and-canvas construction.^5 When these proved too fragile for race conditions, the 10 race canoes were built by **Moïse Cadorette** of St-Jean-de-Piles (near Shawinigan), Quebec, in fiberglass, weighing approximately 400 pounds.^5

Each canoe was named after an explorer associated with its province or territory:^1

## The Race

Teams paddled in 6-man shifts, with each team carrying 10 men total.^1 The route followed the North Saskatchewan River from Rocky Mountain House, through the prairies and Lake Winnipeg, across Ontario via the Great Lakes, down the Ottawa River (reaching Ottawa on August 29), and finally the St. Lawrence River to Expo 67.^1 ^2 The longest portage was 19 miles, from Delta to Portage la Prairie, Manitoba.^5

**Judy LaMarsh**, Secretary of State, started the race at Rocky Mountain House on May 24 and received the paddlers at Expo 67 on September 4, where they presented her with a bale of beaver furs and a buffalo robe fashioned into a cape.^1 ^7 **John Fisher**, the Centennial Commissioner, greeted the canoeists in Ottawa on August 29, calling them "the harbingers of the centennial."^1

The route concluded with paddling the Lachine Rapids, where the Yukon team was swamped and had to be rescued.^1

On August 21, the voyageurs were joined at North Bay, Ontario, by 24 young Jesuits in four canoes retracing 17th-century missionary routes.^1

## Results

| Place | Team | Time |
|-------|------|------|
| 1st | Manitoba | 507 hours, 21 minutes, 51 seconds |
| 2nd | British Columbia | 509 hours, 41 minutes |
| 3rd | Alberta | 511 hours, 33 minutes |

Manitoba also won several sprint races, including a 4,500-metre sprint on the final day.^1 ^8 The full Manitoba team was inducted into the Manitoba Sports Hall of Fame in 2010.^8

### Prizes

Every man who completed the trip received $1,000 and an Expo 67 commemorative watch. First-place team members received an additional $1,500 each; second and third place, an additional $500 each.^5

## Notable Participants

**Don Starkell** was a member of the winning Manitoba team. He later became famous for paddling from Winnipeg to Belém, Brazil (19,603 km) with his son Dana — the longest canoe journey ever recorded — and attempted a kayak traverse of the Northwest Passage in 1990, losing fingertips and toes to frostbite. He was inducted individually into the Manitoba Sports Hall of Fame in 2006.^1 ^9

Team Manitoba's captain **Norm Crerar** and **Gib McEachern** (both 27) had won the Flin Flon Trout Festival Canoe Derby three times as amateurs and six times as professionals. Nearly the entire team was from the Flin Flon area.^8

Nova Scotia was the only team not sponsored by its provincial government; none of their paddlers had extensive canoe experience before the pageant.^10

## Surviving Canoes

Several of the race canoes survive in museums and private hands:^5

- *Pierre Radisson* (Manitoba): Fort Dauphin Museum, refurbished
- *David Thompson* (Alberta): roadside shelter at Rocky Mountain House (installed on 25th anniversary)
- *Henry Kelsey* (Saskatchewan): Western Development Museum, Saskatoon
- *Samuel de Champlain* (New Brunswick): Woodsmen's Museum, Boiestown, Miramichi River
- *William McGillivray* (Ontario): with a paddler's family near Thunder Bay
- *Robert Campbell* and *Alexander Mackenzie* (Yukon/NWT): in service at a BC middle school's big canoe program
- Three canoes have been lost or destroyed

## The CCA Centenary Journey

The **Canadian Camping Association Centenary Journey** was a separate centennial canoe project organized by the CCA and the Ontario Camping Association (OCA), conceived as an educational and commemorative expedition rather than a race.^4 ^11

A cross-Canada canoe route was laid out, with **campers in every province** paddling a section in relay fashion. Planning began as early as April 23, 1964.^11 **Bruce Hodgins** (1931–2019), a Trent University professor and leader of Camp Wanapitei, was "extremely active" on the organizing committee, and his expertise in Canadian history and canoe routes was described as "a key to the success of the project."^11

Confirmed participants include campers from the **Regina YMCA Camp** (Saskatchewan section) and **Camp Deka** (British Columbia). A National Campfire was held on August 9, 1967, as part of the program.^11 A book, *A Log of Canada's Centenary Journey*, was published in 1971.^11

The CCA fonds at Trent University Archives hold extensive records: national committee minutes (1964–67), chairman's reports, health and safety criteria, maps, and instructions for writing trip reports. The OCA fonds hold personnel data sheets, trip logs, photographs, and the original CCA proposal from 1964. A rolled map of the full Centenary route exists in the Trent University Map Collection.^11

### Connection to Kanawana

The CCA fonds include materials on "agency liaison with the Quebec section" of the CCA, indicating Quebec camps participated in the Journey.^11 Six boys and two counsellors from Camp Kanawana paddled the 150-mile Montreal-to-Ottawa section; a busload of campers greeted them singing upon their arrival.^4 The detailed participant lists remain in the archival records at Trent University and have not been digitized.

## Academic Study

Misao Dean published "The Centennial Voyageur Canoe Pageant as Historical Re-enactment" in the *Journal of Canadian Studies* 40:3 (Fall 2006), pp. 43–67, analyzing the event's nationalist symbolism.^12 The analysis was later incorporated into her book *Inheriting a Canoe Paddle* (University of Toronto Press, 2013).

## Film and Media

Glenn Fallis, an Ontario team member, created an hour-long documentary using still photos and short videos, which he has presented publicly.^5 CBC Archives holds video of both the launch at Rocky Mountain House and the finish at Expo 67.^2 CBC Radio's Doc Project produced "Paddle of the Century," a radio documentary about the event.^2
