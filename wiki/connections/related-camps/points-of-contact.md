# Points of Contact: Kanawana and the Other YMCA Camps

*Status: draft | Sources: 14*
*Last Updated: 2026-09-05*

## Overview

Kanawana has never been a camp on its own. For its whole existence it has been one site in a national
YMCA system that moved people, programmes and paperwork between its camps, and one member of a
provincial and national camping association that did the same across ownership lines. This article
collects the **documented** points of contact — people who worked at Kanawana and at another camp,
traditions that demonstrably travelled, and the institutional occasions on which the camps acted
together — and separates them from the resemblances that look like contact and are not.

Two of its findings are negative and worth stating first. **The route by which the L&V Games reached
Kanawana in 1947 is undocumented on every surface searched.** And **Big Cove appears not to have had a
Lumbermen-and-Voyageurs competition at all**, despite sharing the word "Voyageur" with Kanawana's.

## People who worked at Kanawana and elsewhere

These are the hard connections: the same individual, documented at two camps.

**T. Duncan Patton — Montreal to Winnipeg, 1894.** Patton led the first Montreal Boys' Camp, at Jones'
Island in 1891, and sat on the fall-1892 committee that planned Camp Jubilee. In 1894 he left to become
**third General Secretary of the Winnipeg YMCA** — the association that owns Camp Stephens — and an
island on the Lake of the Woods was named after him.^1 His memoir is the reason this project holds any
date at all for camping at that site.

**Nelson McEwen — Camp Stephens to Kanawana, c.1927.** Hired at the Winnipeg YMCA in 1919, McEwen
**directed Camp Stephens from 1924 to 1927**, was recruited to Montreal as Boys' Work Secretary, and
**directed Kanawana from about 1927 until 1947**.^2 He left for the Saint John YMCA and ended his career
as Winnipeg's first Metropolitan General Secretary. **He is the only person documented as having run
both Kanawana and another camp in contention for Canada's oldest.**

**Ross Bannerman — Saint John, Pascobac, Camp Stephens, and a Kanawana report.** McEwen recruited
Bannerman at Saint John; Bannerman went on to direct Camp Pascobac (1951) and **Camp Stephens (1956)**,
and later authored the **1969 Report on Kamp Kanawana**.^2 The chain runs Winnipeg → Montreal → Saint
John → Winnipeg again → back to Montreal on paper.

**A. Ross Seaman — Kanawana to the Maritimes, 1967.** Kanawana's director was the resource person for
the **first Atlantic Provinces Regional Camp Directors' and Administrators' Seminar** at Moncton,
17-19 November 1967, teaching "Methods of Measurement in Camping" and "Methods of Evaluation" to
eighteen delegates from Newfoundland to Halifax.^3 The report of it was written by **Tom Creighton,
Director of YMCA Big Cove Camp**.^4

**Charles Plewman — Golden Lake to Camp Stephens.** Plewman, the founder of *Canadian Camping* and the
figure whose memoir dates the magazine, directed **Camp On-Da-Da-Waks at Golden Lake from 1913 to
1917** and then **Camp Stephens** on the Lake of the Woods.^5 He is not a Kanawana figure, but he links
the Ottawa and Winnipeg camps to each other and to the national association Kanawana belonged to.

## Institutional occasions

**The 1989 relay campfire — the most concrete contact on record.** The YMCA of Montreal's own 1989
annual report describes the **Centenary of YMCA Camping in Canada (1889-1989)**, an anniversary of Big
Cove. **Ashes from a fire lit at Big Cove were distributed to every YMCA camp in the country**, and
Kanawana and twenty-eight others each burned a campfire for one hour of a twenty-nine-hour national
relay, comparing songs, legends and customs, ending in a time capsule of camper signatures and campfire
ashes buried at the Canadian Camping Association congress that October.^6

**Kanawana literally burned Big Cove's fire.** It is also the occasion on which Kanawana's own parent
put **1889 and Big Cove** in print as the origin of YMCA camping in Canada — a concession discussed in
[[history/oldest-camp-question|Is Kanawana the Oldest Camp in Canada?]].

**The national peer group, 1953.** Stan Symington, National Boys' Work Secretary of the National Council
of YMCAs of Canada, listed the eastern camps he visited in 1953 with their directors: **Naskapie**
(Quebec City), **Pascobac** (Saint John), **Wapomeo** (Yarmouth), **Barachois** (Sydney),
**Magaguadavic** (Fredericton), **Y's Acres** (Moncton), **Sherbrooke** (Eastern Townships), **Holland
Cove** (Charlottetown) and **Big Cove** (Halifax), under Clare Buckley.^7 This is the institutional
world Kanawana sat inside, and the list is a roster of a YMCA camping system that has largely vanished.

**The associations.** Kanawana's directors held provincial and national office continuously enough that
the camp was rarely more than one step from any other member camp: Roy D. Locke was president of the
Quebec Camping Association by 1951; A. Ross Seaman was QCA president to September 1967 and the CCA's
vice-president for Quebec from December 1966; Derek Walsh chaired the English Section's publicity in
1975 and presided over its fortieth anniversary in 1977.

## The L&V Games: what is known, and the gap in the middle

**What is established.** The Lumbermen-and-Voyageurs competition is **not Kanawana's invention**. YMCA
Camp Pine Crest in Muskoka has run **"The Pine Crest Games" — a two-day all-camp contest between the
Voyageurs and the Lumbermen — every summer since 1940**, seven years before Kanawana's version began.^8
Pine Crest uses the same two symbols Kanawana uses, the paddle and the axe.^9 Kanawana's
Lumberman-Voyageur Week was created in **1947**, and the YMCA's own count of editions confirms that
start.^10

**And it is not unique to those two camps.** YMCA Hayo-Went-Ha Camps in Michigan, founded 1904, use
"Woodsmen" and "Voyageurs" as programme group names, and the wider **"colour war"** genre is documented
by the historian **Leslie Paris** as beginning with "Red and Gray Week" at Schroon Lake Camp in the
Adirondacks in **1916**, becoming an American camp staple through the 1920s.^11 Kanawana's L&V is best
understood as **a Canadian YMCA fur-trade-themed variant of a North American camp form**, not as a
local invention or a two-camp secret.

**What is not established, on any surface searched: how it travelled.** Neither the YMCA of Greater
Toronto's account of the Pine Crest Games nor any other source consulted says who created the Games, why
the teams carry those names, or whether the tradition was ever deliberately passed to another camp.^8
**The route from Pine Crest to Kanawana in 1947 is undocumented** [f_4759].

**One coincidence of dates, recorded as a lead and not as an answer.** Kanawana's L&V began in the
summer of 1947, and **Roy D. Locke "became Camp Chief during the summer of '47"** in that same season.
No source consulted connects Locke to Pine Crest or to Toronto, and no document names whoever introduced
the competition [f_4760]. A second date sits nearby without explaining anything: **W. E. Ted Yard took
over Camp Pine Crest in 1946**, the year before.

**Where the answer probably is.** Camp Pine Crest published a centennial history, ***Lumbermen &
Voyageurs: The YMCA Pine Crest Story***.^12 It is the single most likely place for the origin of the
Games and any account of their spread, and **this project has not read it**.

## Resemblances that are not contact

**Big Cove is the important case.** Its 1987 tribute records that "**Our Voyageur program dates back to
this time** [the late 1940s], challenging older youth to develop expert outtripping, canoeing,
axemanship and woodcraft skills,"^4 and the camp has a site feature called the Voyageur Trail. It is
easy — and this project nearly did it — to read that as a Lumbermen-and-Voyageurs tradition shared with
Kanawana, whose own L&V began in 1947.

**It is not one.** Big Cove's Voyageur programme is an **advanced outtripping track**, a rank rather
than a team. Its programme sections are Little Big Cove, Juniors, Seniors and Leadership (L1 and L2);
the tradition it names is **"the Keeper of the Cove"**; and there is **no Voyageur section, no Lumbermen
team and no colour-war competition** on the record.^13 The shared word is fur-trade vocabulary common to
Canadian camping generally, and the coincidence of decade — both in the late 1940s — is a coincidence
until a document says otherwise [f_4761].

This distinction matters beyond Big Cove. "Voyageur" appears across Canadian camping as a name for
senior trippers, for canoe pageants, for whole camps. Finding the word at two camps establishes that
both drew on the same national idiom; it does not establish that either learned it from the other.

## How many YMCA camps are there, and where?

**In 1989 there were about twenty-nine.** The centenary relay ran twenty-nine hours precisely because
Kanawana and twenty-eight other YMCA camps each took one [f_4762].

**In 2026, the overnight camps this project can name are six**, and this is a floor rather than a
roster — YMCA Canada's own national camps page names no individual camp at all:

| Camp | Location | Operated by |
|---|---|---|
| **Camp Kanawana** | Saint-Sauveur, Quebec | YMCAs of Quebec |
| **Big Cove** | Sutherlands River, Nova Scotia | YMCA of Greater Halifax/Dartmouth |
| **YM-YWCA Camp Stephens** | Lake of the Woods, near Kenora, Ontario | YMCA-YWCA of Winnipeg |
| **YMCA Camp Pine Crest** | Torrance, Muskoka, Ontario | YMCA of Greater Toronto |
| **YMCA Wanakita** | Haliburton, Ontario | YMCA of Hamilton-Burlington-Brantford |
| **YMCA Camp Elphinstone** | Gibsons, British Columbia | YMCA BC |

YMCA Camp Kanannaq near Prince George runs day camps only.^14 Three provinces have no YMCA overnight
camp in this list at all, and the whole of the Prairies is represented only by a Winnipeg association
running a camp in Ontario.

**The system appears to have contracted sharply, but that is an observation and not a measurement.**
Symington's 1953 tour of eastern Canada alone named nine YMCA camps, of which **only Big Cove is
identifiable here as still operating** [f_4763]. Comparing a 1953 travel itinerary, a 1989 event
participation count and a 2026 web search is comparing three different instruments, and the apparent
decline should be tested against the YMCA's own records before it is asserted.

## Open Questions

1. [Critical] **How did the L&V Games reach Kanawana in 1947?** Read *Lumbermen & Voyageurs: The YMCA
   Pine Crest Story*; check whether Roy D. Locke had any Toronto or Pine Crest posting before summer
   1947; look for correspondence between the two camps in Concordia P0145/12B.
2. [Important] **Who created the Pine Crest Games in 1940, and why those team names?** Unanswered by the
   camp's own public account.
3. [Important] **Did any other Canadian YMCA camp run a Lumbermen-vs-Voyageurs competition?** Only Pine
   Crest and Kanawana are documented; Hayo-Went-Ha's Woodsmen/Voyageurs is a programme-group naming, not
   a games tradition.
4. [Important] **What is the actual number of YMCA camps in Canada, now and over time?** The 1989 figure
   of twenty-nine is solid; 2026 is a floor of six overnight camps assembled from separate association
   websites. YMCA Canada would have the real series.
5. [Nice-to-have] What became of the nine camps on Symington's 1953 list — Naskapie, Pascobac, Wapomeo,
   Barachois, Magaguadavic, Y's Acres, Sherbrooke, Holland Cove?
6. [Nice-to-have] Is there a record of what Kanawana did at its hour of the 1989 relay campfire, or of
   the time capsule buried at the CCA congress that October?
