# Camp Weredale and Its Relationship to Kanawana

*Status: E1-reviewed | Sources: 14*
*Last Updated: 2026-09-07 (the Sir George Williams side, 1968-1971, and the home's size and age range stated by a contemporary)*

## Overview

Camp Weredale is a youth residential camp at 1300 chemin Weredale, Saint-Hippolyte, Quebec, on Lac l'Achigan in the Laurentians, approximately 55 miles north of Montreal.^1 ^2 Founded in 1934, it is one of several Montreal-area camps whose history intersects with Camp Kanawana's, though — contrary to some assumptions — Camp Weredale was never a YMCA property. Its relationship to Kanawana centers on a two-site operating arrangement pursued from the late 1970s into the early 1980s — including a signed lease, not merely a proposal (see Relationship to Camp Kanawana, below) — and on shared communities of Montreal youth workers.

## Origins: The Boys' Home of Montreal

In 1870, Charles Alexander (1816–1905), a Scottish immigrant who had made his fortune as a confectioner in Montreal, founded the Boys' Home of Montreal on Mountain Street.^3 The institution housed homeless and at-risk anglophone boys aged nine to eighteen, providing cheap board, evening classes, and manual training. Alexander built the original building at his own expense and served as president from 1870 until his death, during which time the Home sheltered more than 1,000 boys.^3

By 1930, larger quarters were needed. A campaign conducted by the Rotary Club of Montreal raised $289,000, and a new building was constructed on Weredale Park in Westmount.^1 The institution — a four-story brick building located below St. Catherine Street off Atwater — became known as Weredale House.^1

## Pre-Camp Activities

Before the formal establishment of Camp Weredale, Weredale House boys attended a shared camp in Sainte-Marguerite from the 1920s.^1 An even earlier boys' camp, "Camp Boy," had been sponsored in 1918. The following year it was renamed "Camp Lewis" in memory of Major John Lewis, a friend of the boys killed in the First World War.^1

**And the Montreal YMCA was using Camp Lewis in 1921.** Its annual report for the year ending 30
April 1922 lists, under Eastern District, "many agencies have been used for our Boys' Work, including
Churches, the Macdonald Playground, Fletcher's Field, tennis courts, Maisonneuve Protestant School,
and **Camp Lewis**."^10 That is thirteen years before Camp Weredale existed and sixty before the
1979-1982 relationship described below, and it is between the two parent institutions rather than
between the two camps. What the use was, the report does not say — boys sent, programme run, staff
shared, or a partner agency simply listed. The paragraph around it concerns the Eastern District's
work in the foreign-speaking districts: Boy Scout work with Italian and Syrian boys, employed boys of
foreign parentage, and co-operation with the Juvenile Court. That points toward boys placed rather
than programme run, but it is inference from adjacency and nothing more. No other cached annual
report in this project names Camp Lewis.

**Camp Lewis was an agency in its own right, which is why the YMCA could use it without owning it.**
*Canadian Camping*'s survey of Quebec in June 1949, counting forty-five registered permanent camps in
the province: "**Camp Lewis, under the direction of Mr. Wylie, offers good camping to five hundred
boys for two weeks or longer each year**."^11 Five hundred boys and a named director is a substantial
operation, not a facility of somebody else's.

Fifteen years later it is still listed that way. A Montreal YMCA news release of 1964 names the
organizations taking part in a Rotary-initiated inter-agency youth project — Rosemount Boys Club, the
YM-YWHA, Holy Cross and Dawson Boys Clubs, St. Andrews Youth Centre, four YMCA branches, the Montreal
Girls Association, the Lion's and East-End Boys & Girls Clubs, Unity Boys Club, Tyndale House, **Camp
Lewis**, the **Negro Community Centre**, and the Boy Scouts — with **Ross Seaman** of the
Metropolitan Office on its staff, the same man who directed Kanawana.^11 Camp Lewis sits in that list
as a peer, one line above the Negro Community Centre.

So the 1921 sentence above reads as cooperation between agencies rather than as a YMCA camp: the
association's Eastern District boys' work drew on another organization's camp, in a city where that
was ordinary enough to be listed beside churches, a playground and a school. What kind of use it was
— boys sent, programme run, staff shared — and whether it lasted one year or many are still open, and
the Weredale House fonds at Library and Archives Canada, whose Camp Lewis records run 1919 to 1966,
is where to ask.

## Establishment and Facilities

In 1934, the Rotary Club of Westmount purchased 260 acres with a mile of shoreline on Lac l'Achigan and donated the property to Weredale House.^1 ^2 Camp Weredale was established as a summer home for the boys and staff.^2 At its peak, the camp featured approximately two dozen buildings, including a dining hall, recreation hall, craft shops, hospital, shower rooms, sleeping cabins, playground, open-air chapel, Indian Council ring, rifle range, tree house, and campsites.^1

The camp suffered a fire in 1965 and was rebuilt.^1

## Institutional Transitions

**Two glimpses of Weredale House in the years just before that merger, from university newsletters.** Loyola College's paper *The Happening*, 11 March 1974, lists among the on-going projects of Loyola's Volunteer Bureau "**tutoring boys from the Weredale Home**" — beside the John Birk Association, the Benedict Labre House, the Lethbridge Rehabilitation Centre, the Montreal Association for the Blind and the Griffith McConnel Home, which is a useful indication of the company it kept in the city's social-agency landscape.^12 And Concordia's *FYI* of 7 October 1976 carries the Chancellor's Centraide appeal, which names "**Weredale House**" as one of five examples standing for "some 100 different social agencies in the Montreal region," alongside the Red Cross, the Montreal Diet Dispensary, University Settlement and the Y.W.C.A.^12 A chancellor picking five names out of a hundred to mean something to faculty and staff picked this one, a year before the merger.

*Two things not to read into those.* The 1974 item is **Loyola's** bureau, not Sir George Williams'; the two colleges merged into Concordia that same year, so it sits on the other half of the merger from the SGW–YMCA material gathered elsewhere in this wiki. And neither document touches open question 4 below, which asks about connections between Weredale House and the **Montreal YMCA**. These are university connections, on a different axis.

**And by the late 1970s the YMCA describes Camp Weredale as a French camp.** Nothing else in this wiki mentions language in connection with it. "The Montreal Fact '75-'79," the Association's four-year review of the linguistic policy it adopted on 15 September 1975, sets out what each of its camps was for: "**Camp Weredale, on Lake l'Achigan, has a specific purpose: to be a French camp also able to receive English-speaking campers.**"^13 That is the inverse of what this camp's own history would predict — a Westmount boys' home, an anglophone Rotary donation, a Montreal Protestant institutional lineage — and it says the Association had given the site a francophone mandate with English service as the secondary provision. The same paragraph assigns [[site/camp-otoreke|Camp Otoreke]] French-speaking low-income families. See [[traditions/french-language-camping|French-Language Camping]] for the policy behind it.

*A caution on what this does and does not say.* It is the Association describing its own camps in a promotional review, not an operating record, and it gives no year for when the mandate began, no enrolment figures, and no account of how a French camp was staffed. It also sits four years after the two-site planning with Kanawana that this article records for 1977–1980, and nothing here connects the two.

**And the Sir George Williams half of the same relationship, five years earlier.** The caution attached to those two glimpses — that the 1974 item is Loyola's bureau and sits on the other side of the merger — can now be answered from the other side.^14 *The Georgian* of **11 December 1968** ran a notice for a collection of books, records and Fine Art students' work, taken in bins on the mezzanine and fifth floor and auctioned the following day, whose "proceeds will go to **start art classes for the Weredale Boys Orphonage**." *The Paper* of **13 January 1969** lists the bodies the Sir George Williams volunteer bureau co-ordinated requests for — "the Unity Club, East End Boys Club as well as hospitals, individuals and **Weredale Home**" — the requests being "usually… for **tutoring** of children and young people," which is the same service Loyola's bureau was supplying five years later. And *The Paper* of **25 January 1971** puts "**Weredale House**" and "**the Y.M.C.A.**" in a single list of the agencies that bureau worked with. The student-volunteer relationship ran on both campuses and predates the merger that made Concordia; it remains a university connection, and still does not bear on open question 4 below.

**The only contemporary description of the home itself that this project holds.** *The Paper* of **7 December 1970** introduced Radio Sir George's Christmas project with a sentence written for students who did not know the place: "**Weredale is a home for orphaned boys and boys from broken homes between the ages of 9 to 18**," and "there are **over 200 boys at the home**."^14 The drive asked for wrapped gifts marked with a boy's age, and ran a second collection of used sports equipment "for **Weredale's sport's program**" — badminton rackets, ping pong sets, hockey and football equipment, floor hockey sticks, nets, skates, toboggans. A benefit with Danny Farmar and his Blues Band in the Alumni Auditorium, at 75¢, funded the party of 11 December, and the Evening Students' Association voted it $100, a sum that surfaces again as the line "Charity (Weredale House) 100.00" in the association's published budget of February 1971. Two things are worth taking from this. The **size and age range** are stated plainly, by a contemporary, in a year Camp Weredale was operating — this article otherwise describes the institution through its buildings, its mergers and its fonds. And the home ran **a sports programme of its own** in the city, which is the winter counterpart of a camp whose purpose was outdoor recreation for the same boys. The issue also reports a professor's telephone being tapped under the War Measures Act, which places all of this a few weeks after the October Crisis.

In 1977, Weredale House merged with Summerhill Homes, the Girls' Cottage School, and Allencroft Reception Centres to form Youth Horizons (now part of the Batshaw Youth and Family Centres network).^1 Camp Weredale survived this transition and continues to operate today under the Weredale Foundation as an independent co-ed camp for children aged 6–17, welcoming four cohorts of approximately 85 campers each from late June through mid-August.^5

## Relationship to Camp Kanawana

In 1977, the YMCA of Montreal produced situation reports profiling Kamp Kanawana, Camp Otoreke, and Camp Weredale together, and by approximately 1980 had developed plans for a proposed two-site operation combining Kanawana and Weredale.^4 Notably, the planning window coincided exactly with the dissolution of Weredale's parent institution: Weredale House closed in 1977, the same year it merged into Youth Horizons.^1 The two-site discussions thus took place while Weredale's governance was in flux — a plausible context for the plan's origin (a camp suddenly without its founding institution), though this remains inference.^7 Concordia University Archives sub-series P0145/12A (Committees) holds the relevant planning documents (1977–1983), while sub-series P0145/12F is dedicated to Camp Weredale records.^4

A direct fetch of Concordia's static finding-aid page for sub-series 12F (not the bot-walled AtoM catalog) documents Box HA1894 holding an actual **signed 1979 lease agreement between the Weredale Foundation and the Montreal YMCA**, plus continuing joint files — "Weredale Foundation/YMCA" (1980-1981), "Comité Weredale" (1981-1982), a day-camp program proposal (1980), and a "YMCA staff cottage rental" (1981) — extending at least three years past the original 1977-1980 planning window.^8 This indicates a genuine, multi-year operational relationship, not a plan that died on paper (see Revision History). What the arrangement actually looked like in practice, and why it eventually ended (rather than continuing to the present), remains undocumented online and is the article's most significant open question going forward.

The camps also shared overlapping communities of Montreal youth workers. Frank Edward Crabbe (born 1946, died February 16, 1966), an ex-Weredale Boy and Camp Lewis counsellor c.1964–65, enlisted in the U.S. Marine Corps on January 7, 1965, and was killed in the Vietnam War.^6 Beyond the 1979-1982 institutional relationship documented above, no specific shared staff, programming, or organizational practice between the two camps during 1934-1977 has been found: a full-text search of the complete McMorris 2023 thesis (covering 1894-1967) contains zero occurrences of "Weredale," and a direct re-check of the Crabbe memorial blog post confirms it makes no mention of Kanawana at all — his Weredale/Camp Lewis connection and any Kanawana connection remain two separate, undocumented-as-linked facts.
