# The Association's Own Statistics, and the One Camp in Ten

*Status: draft | Sources: 7*
*Last Updated: 2026-09-07 (the YMCA's own national camp count, and the same number printed twice)*

## Overview

Every national figure this wiki quotes about Canadian camping comes from the Canadian Camping
Association's own statistics, and **all of them count member camps only**. In November 1982 the
association's executive director was asked what share of Canadian camps belonged and answered
"depending on the area of the country, probably 10 to 12" per cent. So the association's numbers
describe a self-selected, better-resourced tenth of the field, not Canadian camping.

That multiplier has to travel with every figure taken from here, and it has a direct consequence
for Kanawana: **its membership of the Quebec association is not a neutral administrative fact but a
marker of a particular kind of camp**, and any comparison between Kanawana and "Canadian camps"
drawn from CCA data is a comparison with the accredited tenth.

The series itself is short and imperfect, and its imperfections are stated by the people who
compiled it. The association had no reliable numbers at all until 1976 and said so in print. The
1980-81 provincial table is extrapolated across a 45 per cent non-response rate and its column
headings are illegible in the scanned copy. The labelled table that followed rests on returns from
eight provincial associations in one year and six in the next. **These are partial aggregates of
provincial returns, not a census**, and this article says so in each case rather than once.

What the series is good for is a denominator. Quebec had on the order of **125 organized camps in
1980-81** in a Canadian field of about 574 member camps, and Kanawana was one of them; the Quebec
rows for 1982 and 1983 are read in
[[connections/institutional-lineage/quebec-camping-association|The Quebec Camping Association]].

## The first national statistics, 1976

**The first national statistics, 1976.** The association had never had reliable numbers, and said so plainly when it published its first annual statistical questionnaire in March 1977: it needed figures "in order to appear to be an organization with a strong foundation, and a knowledgeable spokesman for camping," and some directors had refused to take part, "feeling that we were invading their privacy, or that the figures would be used to embarrass them." The editor's answer to them is worth preserving: "**if we are unable to provide reasonable figures for the Government, we will then be legislated into answering certain questions… with no option to be coy or evasive!**"^1

The projected national figures for the 1976 season: **68,000 camper spaces**; **260,000 campers attended member camps**; **32,000 staff, of whom 22,000 were paid and 10,000 were volunteers, and 1,600 came from outside Canada**; **food costs of $8,000,000** excluding salaries, fuel and kitchen supplies, and **$14,000,000 of other costs** excluding food and salaries. Quebec was one of four associations whose members responded at over 50%.^1 These are the only national figures the run produces, and they give the denominator against which Kanawana's own numbers can be read — a camp registering about 190 children a season was one part in roughly fourteen hundred of Canadian organized camping. The 1,600 foreign staff also give the scale of the visa waiver described above: an accreditation benefit that touched about one camp employee in twenty.

## A second set, five years later, by province

**And a second set, five years later, broken down by province.** In October 1981 the association printed a table of figures for **1980-81** province by province, without commentary, beneath a special report by **Rosaire Corbin, directeur général of the Association des Camps du Québec**.^2 Three cautions have to travel with any number taken from it. First, Corbin says the figures "**ont la valeur objective de savants calculs d'extrapolation effectués afin de remédier aux 45% des non-répondants**" — **they are extrapolations covering a 45% non-response rate**. Second, **the column headings are set in rotated type and are illegible in the scanned copy**; the numbers are clean but the labels are not, and a clean copy should be obtained before they are named. Third, what follows is therefore given as figures with a reading attached, not as a labelled table.

Nationally the row totals are **574** in the first column, **313,953** in the second, **37,690** in the third and **228** in the fourth. The second and third sit squarely in line with the association's own 1976 projections of 260,000 campers and 32,000 staff, which makes campers and staff the natural reading of those two columns and implies growth of roughly a fifth over five years; the first column reads as the number of camps; the fourth is not identifiable. **Quebec's row is 125 · 63,243 · 5,694 · 40**, second only to Ontario's 197 · 91,281 · 13,339 · 60.

For this project the useful figure is the denominator, and it is the best one the run supplies for the 1980s: **Quebec had on the order of 125 organized camps in 1980-81, in a Canadian field of about 574, and Kanawana was one of them.**

## The number that reframes all of them: about one camp in ten belonged

**And the number that reframes all of them: about one camp in ten belonged.** In November 1982 the association's own Executive Director, **Marjorie Booth**, was asked what percentage of Canadian camps were members and answered "**depending on the area of the country, probably 10 to 12**"; her interviewer restated it as "roughly then, only 10% of camps operating in this country are members of their provincial association", and she let it stand.^3

**Every figure in this article counts member camps, so this is the multiplier that has to travel with them.** If 574 member camps were a tenth of the field, Canada had on the order of five thousand camps operating, and the association's statistics describe a self-selected, better-resourced minority rather than Canadian camping as a whole. For this project the consequence is direct: **Kanawana's membership of the Quebec association is not a neutral administrative fact but a marker of a particular kind of camp**, and any comparison between Kanawana and "Canadian camps" drawn from CCA data is a comparison with the accredited tenth.

Booth's account of who stayed out explains the shape of that tenth: "there are a number of small organizations or church-affiliated camping programmes in this country that **don't have the financial resources — at least in their minds — to meet some of the recommended practices and so they choose not to belong**"; and, separately, "**if camp directors don't know we exist, they can't join the association**… Nationally, we're not well known."^3 She also states the rule that applied to Kanawana: in **Ontario and Quebec**, "**in order to become a member camp, you must meet the standards or recommended practices and be visited by a consultant or your peers**" — so in Quebec, membership and accreditation were a single gate, which is why the two words are used interchangeably about Kanawana in this wiki. The alternative she floats, and which some provinces used, was a two-tier membership: "one that's an accredited member and one that is a member which has not met the standards or desirable practices", letting any camp belong "as long as they pay the fee and subscribe to the goals and objectives of that association."

**Where Quebec sat in the national picture, in her words.** "In the prairies and Atlantic provinces, most of the camps are organization-run, agency-run or religiously based. **There is a huge difference in Ontario, Quebec, and to some degree British Columbia**, where many camps operate for longer periods of time — two or four weeks for a session. As well, most of the camps are non-denominational and are able to attract a clientele who are able to pay more to go to camp than you would find in other parts of the country."^3 And on staff: elsewhere "campers don't stay on as campers until they're 19… they're almost forced to [leadership] earlier because they are needed to be leaders; **this is so foreign to camps in Quebec and Ontario, for example, where you'll often have 16 and 17 year-old campers who return year after year.**" Long sessions, higher fees, non-denominational, older campers returning: that is the category Kanawana belonged to, described by the person whose job it was to know.

*(The issue this comes from was scanned 180 degrees rotated and machine-read upside down; the text was recovered with a purpose-built decoder that is lossy. The quotations above are reconstructions — close, but to be verified against a clean copy before the exact wording or the "10 to 12" figure is relied on. The substance is not in doubt.)*

## A labelled national table for the same two years

**A labelled national table for the same two years.** Nine months later the association printed its national statistics with the headings intact, compiled by Marjorie Booth in March 1982.^4 For **1980** and **1981** respectively: accredited member camps **590** and **533**, with 44 non-accredited member camps in 1981; day camps **126** and **125**; resident camps **453** and **468**; camper beds **64,276** and **74,762**; camper/days **3,962,848** and **4,046,388**; volunteer staff **18,405** and **12,247**; paid staff **19,282** and **20,539**; staff/days **1,125,676** and **1,151,255**; staff who were students **18,787** and **16,784**; food costs **$11,454,000** and **$12,607,000**; salaries **$17,279,000** and **$19,448,000**; foreign campers **9,790** and **9,130**; foreign counsellors **1,076** and **1,268**.

Booth's own caveat is as important as the numbers: "**the 1980 data was based on a response from eight provincial associations; the 1981 figures represent provincial totals for six associations**", and she notes that some resident camps are double-counted as extended-season camps and that the rise in camper/days is "probably due to the longer summer vacation period (10 weeks) for students". **These are partial aggregates of provincial returns, not a census**, and any figure taken from them carries that.

The labelled table also decodes most of the illegible one. Its 1980 volunteer and paid staff sum to **37,687**, three away from the provincial table's third-column total of 37,690, which identifies **column 3 as total staff**; the camp counts of 590 and 577 bracket the provincial table's 574, identifying **column 1 as camps**; and column 2's 313,953 is consistent with a head count of campers rather than camper/days. **Column 4 remains unidentified** and is queued as `p_324`. Nothing in this article labels those columns. Corbin's argument for collecting the numbers at all is the frankest statement in the run of what the associations did not know about their own members — that when a government or a university asked "qui représentez-vous?, combien d'enfants accueillez-vous dans vos camps? combien de personnes engagez-vous?", the answers had to be prefaced with "à peu près", and "la solidité de l'argumentation en est automatiquement affaiblie."^2

## Two provinces confirm the membership figure from the ground

**Two provinces confirm the membership figure from the ground.** Booth's estimate that the association spoke for "probably 10 to 12" percent of Canadian camps is corroborated, independently and candidly, in two of the provincial reports bound into the association's 1982 Annual Report. Nova Scotia: staff "visited all the camps to carry the message of the Association… Interestingly enough, and maybe not so, but **some of the Directors operating camps had never heard of the Association!**" Saskatchewan: "**We speak for about twenty camps but there are probably more than one hundred other camps out there** who may or may not know we exist to speak for them." Every CCA statistic used in this wiki describes a member sample of roughly a tenth of the field, sampled at about half again.^5

## A second denominator: the YMCA counted its own camps

Everything above counts **Canadian Camping Association member camps**, which were about one in ten of the field. The YMCA kept its own national count, and it is a different denominator worth having beside this one. The *National Council Bulletin* printed the count twice. Reporting on **1964**: "**There were 48 Canadian 'Y' residence campsites and more than 70 day campsites used by more than 24,500 campers (up more than three thousand from last year)**," in a feature whose other figures are also 1964's — 27 million attendances, 66 gyms, some sixty pools.^7 So **1964: 48 residence campsites, 70-plus day campsites, 24,500 campers**, and **1963: about 21,500 campers**. A year later the same feature reads "**We camped: 24,500 of us, at 48 'Y' residence campsites and more than 70 day campsites**," beside 15,800 swimming awards earned in 62 pools and a membership of nearly 50,000 boys under 18.^6

*The camp numbers are identical across the two years and the swimming numbers are not.* That is either a genuinely flat two years or a figure carried over, and **nothing here decides which** — so 48 and 24,500 are firm for 1964 and provisional for 1965. It is worth saying because a series built by taking each year's feature at face value would record a suspiciously stable camp system on the strength of one number printed twice.

**Why this is worth more than a stray number.** The question of how many YMCA camps Canada had, and whether that number fell, has been stuck on the fact that the available figures came from different kinds of instrument. This one is the *same* instrument as the **twenty-nine YMCA camps** that each burned an hour of the 1989 centenary relay campfire: both are counts published by the national body. So there is now a national series of two comparable points, **48 residence campsites in 1965** and **twenty-nine camps in 1989**. The modern figure of six is not a third point in that series — it is the number of overnight YMCA camps this project could *name* from separate association websites, which is a different thing entirely.

*Two cautions belong with it.* "Residence campsites" counts **sites**, not camps or associations, and a single camp can hold more than one site — Kanawana's own Lac Becscies annex is exactly that case. And the 1989 relay counted the camps that **took part**, which is a floor for the number existing rather than the number itself.
