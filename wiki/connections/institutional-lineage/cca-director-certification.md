# Certifying the Camp Director

*Status: draft | Sources: 7*
*Last Updated: 2026-09-06 (spun out of The Canadian Camping Movement under p_452)*

## Overview

Between the winter of 1982 and the summer of that year the Canadian Camping Association put a
question to its own membership and got an answer it then acted on for the rest of the decade:
**should a person be required to hold anything at all before being allowed to direct a camp?** Its
editors said the absence of any requirement was a scandal. Two of its members wrote back and
explained why a national scheme would not work in a country where camping meant different things in
different provinces. The association dropped certification and set about writing down what a camp
director ought to know instead — a body of literature, a staff-manual specification, and the first
Canadian textbook of camp administration, which came out of Montreal.

That last fact is why this belongs in Kanawana's wiki at all. **The movement's textbook was written
by a McGill professor who held his national membership through Kanawana's own provincial
association**, and the specification for a camp staff manual describes, item by item, a document
Kanawana would have produced every year and which nobody has yet looked for.

The debate sits inside a wider argument about what the associations regulated and what they left
alone, which is set out in
[[connections/institutional-lineage/canadian-camping-movement|The Canadian Camping Movement]];
the accreditation regime Kanawana actually passed through is in
[[connections/institutional-lineage/quebec-camping-association|The Quebec Camping Association]].

## The debate, and the literature that came instead

**Nobody had to be qualified to direct a camp, and the association's own editors called that a scandal.** The Winter 1982 editorial, printed under a policy note distinguishing the editorial committee's views from the association's, is the plainest statement in the run of what a camp director was and was not required to be: "Doctors are licensed to practise their profession. Teachers are certified before entering a classroom. Nurses are registered before being allowed to care for patients. **Yet, just about anyone is allowed to direct a camp and care for other people's children. This is not right! There are no national standards as to the professional qualifications of a camp director**; some provinces have age, education or experience requirements but does this ensure that camp directors are capable and qualified…? Certainly not."^1 The scheme proposed was a specified curriculum — needs of children, needs of staff, hiring and development of staff, health and safety, camp administration and people management, camp philosophy, food services — **plus an internship in an organized camp**; and the argument for acting was the threat of being regulated from outside: "**this situation opens up the very real possibility — in fairness to the public — of the imposition of regulations on us by the government. The responsibility rests with us — the time is now!**"

That is the direct context for reading any Kanawana director's credentials in this period. The provincial requirements set out in [[connections/institutional-lineage/quebec-camping-association|The Quebec Camping Association]] — the ACQ's own age and experience rules — were the only bar that existed; **there was no national qualification, no register and no disciplinary mechanism**, and the association's editors were saying so in print in 1982. The same issue also carries the counter-argument, from a teacher writing on wilderness leadership: "**certification courses simply legalize errors in judgment**."^1

**The membership answered, and the answer explains why it never happened.** Two letters printed the following spring set out the profession's own reasoning.^2 **Ron Johnstone** of Camp Tawingo argued against on two grounds. The first was that a national scheme would misread the country: "**We in Ontario seem to have a tendency to think that all camping in Canada is the same… in reality it isn't! There are whole degrees of difference in sophistication in camping in Canada.** I have no difficulty at all in seeing the Camping Associations as educational and inspirational organizations but I have some real trouble when we start into the business of **regulating the industry** and that is what a program of National Standards or Certification will do." The second was that the public-protection argument was hollow because the business was not profitable enough to attract bad actors: "**all that wheeler-dealers have to do is look at the balance sheets of most Camps in Canada and they couldn't run fast enough to stay out of the business.**" He conceded the competence problem in a phrase worth keeping — "**I know some of our numbers who claim ten years experience in camping but in reality have had one year ten times**" — but concluded that education, not certification, was the movement's instrument: "now is not the time and I do not believe that we will ever see that time being ripe!"

**Lorne Bowering**, President of the British Columbia association, was qualifiedly in favour but doubted the machinery: "**The American Camping Association Certification system seems cumbersome to me — too many levels and a rat's nest to administer.** I have always thought **certification of Directors is at least as important as the Facilities/Program standards approach**… the problem is usually the 'How' to do, rather than the 'Why'. **Camping people are diverse and what may be a high standard for one may be a very low standard for another.**"

So the **ACA had a director-certification system in 1982 and the CCA did not**, and the reason was a settled position rather than an oversight: the Canadian associations understood themselves as educational and inspirational bodies, not regulators of an industry. For this project that is the answer to what Kanawana's directors were required to hold. Provincial accreditation covered the camp; the ACQ's rules covered the director's age and experience; **nothing national covered the director at all.**

**Having declined to certify, the association set about defining the body of knowledge instead.** Its Education Committee, chaired by Dorothy Walter with Janet Adamson, John Wilkinson and Stan Talesnick — and observed by Carolea Butters as chairman of the Ontario Task Force on Professional Development — was mandated to "examine, make recommendations for and publicize a variety of educational opportunities for the professional development of camp directors in Canada", including possibly "the preparation of a **self-directed learning curriculum for use anywhere in Canada**".^3 Eight research steps were designed, three completed by the spring of 1982, "and **all of this research is to come from within the Canadian Camping community**"; the results were to be presented at a **Pre-Congress Educational Workshop in January 1983** to determine "the **basic core curriculum required for professional development of camp directors**". Ontario had put the same question to its own Directors' Weekend in the autumn of 1981, led by Nelson Wieters, and reported that "**no decisions were reached**".^3 Whether the national curriculum was ever completed is not known to this project, and the workshop papers would be in the CCA fonds at Trent.

**What a camp staff manual contained — the specification, 1981.** In February 1981 the association printed a full definition of the document it had been recommending for years, and the contents list is the most useful single page in the run for anyone looking for a camp's own account of itself.^4 A staff manual is "a booklet prepared by the camp for the purpose of informing its future staff about the camp and their role in it", and "most staff manuals contain" seven parts.

Part II, **Facts About Camp**, is the part that matters here. Under *Locale*: a directional map, "a sketch of site showing trails, program areas, buildings", a description of the setting's special features and "**special problems which site gives rise to**". Under *Traditions, Philosophy*: statements interpreting them, a statement relating the camp's objectives to the camping movement, "**a brief historical sketch of the camp**", and "**explanation of role that camping plays in the total program of the sponsoring organization, where such exists**" — which for Kanawana means the YMCA of Montreal. Then *Procedures and Regulations* (including "**for inter-camp relationships (e.g. canoe tripping)**"), *Organizational Structure*, and *Personnel Policies* — "sickness allowance; **employment and dismissal procedures**; salary scales and payment procedures; travel to and from camp, charges for laundry."

Part IV is **Sample Forms Used At Camp**: "**test sheets (swimming, canoeing, camp crafts, etc.); tripping reports, what to take, hints for safety; parent reports, reports to section heads, etc.; counsellor evaluation**." Part V is **Job Analyses** for programme director, section head, specialist, activity counsellor, cabin counsellor and the CIT programme. And the production advice is specific enough to recognise an object by: "**bind in a stiff loose-leaf cover**… use different coloured pages for different sections (compulsory material, reference material, basis for pre-camp training)", closing with "**the tone of the Manual sets the tone of the future counsellor**."

**A Kanawana staff manual, if one survives, would therefore contain the camp's own historical sketch, a site map with its problems named, its dismissal procedures, its salary scales, its blank forms and its job descriptions, in a loose-leaf binder with coloured section dividers.** That is a specific object to ask for, and this project already holds one document of the family — the "Kamp Kanawana History" presented at a staff training course in 1951 (see [[traditions/cit-lit-program|The CIT/LIT Program]]), which is precisely the "brief historical sketch" this specification prescribes.

**A Montreal professor writes the movement's textbook, 1981.** The first comprehensive Canadian text on camp administration was published in Toronto in 1981 by **Robert E. Wilkinson, Ph.D., Professor in the Department of Physical Education at McGill University** — described by his own publisher as a "**Member of Canadian Camping Association through the Association des Camps du Québec**", and by his reviewer as being of **Camp Nominingue**, F. M. Van Wagner's camp.^5 *Camps, Their Planning and Management* runs to 291 pages, was sold through the CCA's own publications service at $19.95, and "**includes national code of ethics for Canadian Camping Association**".

Eanswythe Flynn's review places it: "**Not since Dimock and Hendry's *Camping and Character* (1929) has a Canadian textbook of this size and depth been so all embracing**" — which puts a McGill physical education professor's 1981 book in a line that runs back to the study conducted at Taylor Statten's camp (see [[people/hedley-dimock|Hedley Dimock]]). What matters for this project is what she says it contains: "scores of charts, sketches, plans and **specimens of every kind of business form any camp records would require**", with fifty-two sketches and charts drawn by Mrs Wilkinson.

One caution about the edition, which matters to anyone trying to obtain it. The May 1981 review and the June 1981 publication notice both give the book as C.V. Mosby, Toronto, 1981; but two months earlier, in April 1981, a contributor's reference list cited "**Wilkinson, Robert E., *Camps, Their Planning and Management*, C.V. Mosby Co., St. Louis, Missouri, 1978**", with footnotes to specific pages.^6 Either an earlier American edition exists or the contributor cited from proof. **The book should be asked for under both dates**: a 1978 edition's specimen business forms would date from the decade in which Kanawana's own surviving record is thinnest, rather than from after it. (A second, smaller trap in the same notice: it says the book was "reviewed in April *Canadian Camping*". The review is in the May issue.)

**Wilkinson is not the only Quebec name on the association's own list.** The CCA's 1981 publications catalogue marks Canadian titles with an asterisk, and there are only about fifteen of them among more than a hundred entries — the association's reading was overwhelmingly American, and it was the sole Canadian distributor for the American Camping Association. Three of that small Canadian list are Quebec-authored: Wilkinson's textbook; **Derek Walsh's *How to Promote Your Camp* / *Comment promouvir votre camp vacances*, CCA/ACC, 1981**, listed under his name in both the English and French series pages (see [[people/derek-walsh|Derek Walsh]]); and "***Outdoor Education — Guidelines for Camps* — W. Mingie, 17p, CCA, revised 1981**", by **Walter Mingie**, recorded in this magazine twenty years earlier as director of canoe trips and the intermediate section at **Camp Nominingue**.^7 So the national body was drawing a disproportionate share of its own published writing from the province Kanawana was accredited in, at exactly the period when Quebec's English-language section had disappeared from its printed list of provincial associations.

**A book with specimens of every camp business form, written by a member of Kanawana's own provincial association and published in the year the camp's record is thinnest, is a direct aid to reading whatever Kanawana papers survive** — and a second route, after the **Ontario forms kit of 1980**, to knowing what the blanks looked like. (The forms kit stayed with the parent article, which prints the nine forms by name.) It is also the fourth **McGill** connection the parent records, after Arthur S. Lamb, the McGill camping school of 1937-40 and Blackstock's own B.Sc. — a run of four that is easier to see now that these paragraphs sit apart from it.

## Open Questions

1. [Important] **Was the national core curriculum for camp directors ever completed?** Eight research
   steps were designed and three were done by the spring of 1982, with results due at the
   Pre-Congress Educational Workshop of January 1983. This project holds nothing after that. The
   workshop papers would be in the Canadian Camping Association fonds at Trent University; see
   `project-docs/trent-access-plan.md`.
2. [Important] **Does a Kanawana staff manual survive, and does it follow this specification?** The
   1981 contents list gives seven parts and names what each contains. This project holds one document
   of the family — the "Kamp Kanawana History" read at a 1951 staff training course — which is
   exactly the "brief historical sketch" the specification prescribes. `p_399` asks Concordia for the
   manual by name.
3. [Nice-to-have] **Did Kanawana own a copy of Wilkinson's textbook, and did anyone from the camp
   take his Congress session on camper safety and the law?** He taught it at Toronto in January 1983
   and every session was recorded and sold on cassette; see `p_325`.
4. [Nice-to-have] **Who was Robert E. Wilkinson, beyond the two lines his publisher printed?** A
   McGill professor of physical education, a member through the Association des Camps du Québec, and
   of Camp Nominingue. No biography of him has been sought.
