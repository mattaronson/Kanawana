# The Movement's Archive Turn, 1985-1987

*Status: draft | Sources: 8*
*Last Updated: 2026-09-06 (spun out of The Canadian Camping Movement under p_452)*

## Overview

In its last three years of publication the Canadian Camping Association turned its magazine and its
committees toward its own past. It mailed every member camp in the country a kit of camp-history
activities for **International Youth Year** in 1985, began reprinting historical addresses each
quarter, invited every camp to stage its own history for the association's fiftieth anniversary in
1986, told its provincial associations in February 1987 to record oral history, and printed the
plainest statement of archive practice in the whole run: **"today's snapshots, camp brochures,
crests, cards and programs are tomorrow's priceless archives. Every camp should be actively
archiving."**

**Why this matters to Kanawana more than most articles here.** Twice in two years every member camp
in Canada was asked, in writing, to produce an account of its own history — once in May 1985 and
again for the anniversary summer of 1986. Kanawana was a member camp both years. If it did either
thing, the document exists and this project has never looked for it. That is the single most
actionable consequence of anything in this article, and it is what
[[history/between-centennials|the years between the centennials]] are thinnest on.

The rest of the association's last years is at
[[connections/institutional-lineage/canadian-camping-movement|The Canadian Camping Movement]] and
[[connections/institutional-lineage/cca-national-office|The National Office and the Federal Money]].

## The turn

**24 July 1985: the one day every member camp in Canada was asked to keep — and what arrived in the post.** The International Youth Year National Celebration Event was fixed for **Wednesday 24 July 1985**, with Ian Fowler of Moncton as national co-ordinator. The association undertook to "provide **each member camp with a kit of program material** suitable for the day's event," to be "**directly mailed to each camp in early May**," and the contents were credited by author: **"Historical Roots Activities — Dr. Bruno Morawetz, Camp Ponacka"**; "Environmental Activities — Mike Walters, Camp Chief Hector"; "Fitness Activities — Fitness Canada"; "Peace Activities — Canadian Peace Congress"; and an "IYY Campfire Program — Jack Pearse, Jane McCutcheon, John Jorgenson, Camp Tawingo." Camps were asked "at least [to] use the special IYY campfire program giving the CCA salute to youth one common denominator."^1

For this project the first of those is the significant one. **A named set of camp-history activities, written by the association's own archives chairman, was posted to every member camp in Canada in early May 1985** — Kanawana among them, since every ACQ member camp was an accredited member camp — for use on a fixed date eleven weeks later, in a period for which no Kanawana season report survives. It is a specific document with an author and a delivery window rather than a general exhortation, and a copy should be sought in the CCA fonds at Trent. The Quebec member of the task force that commissioned it was **Peter Goddard of Montreal**; that Kanawana's own director was therefore party to sending a history kit to his own camp follows from two documented facts and is stated here as an inference, not as a record.

The theme article that accompanied it, Bob Stewart's "Tracing Your Roots," is a genealogy primer for campers, and its advice on the limits of what can be done at camp is unexpectedly close to this project's own method: gather family stories, "but treat them with a bit of care. **Time has a way of adding to old family stories**… Sometimes they can be very interesting without being very true."^1

**The magazine begins reprinting its own past.** From the Summer 1985 issue a feature called "Camping… A Retrospective" reprints, each quarter, "a previously spoken or published work on some aspect of camping in Canada," so that readers might "examine to what degree the atmosphere, thrusts and initiatives of bygone days are relevant today." The first is **Barry Lowes's address to the Ontario Camping Association's annual banquet at the Inn-on-the-Park on 19 March 1966** — an attack on the movement's complacency ("we talk a better game than we play… the claims have been mostly shadows without substance"), a warning against letting the OCA's newly appointed first executive secretary, **Stan Wild**, carry work the volunteers should, and a rejection of the profit/non-profit distinction as "anathema… A camp is a camp, and a child is a child." Its most striking passage, written months before Canada's centennial year: "**Canada stands in danger of coming apart at the seams, after 100 years**… If the 3½ millions of dollars that have been poured down the drain on the Bilingual and Bicultural Commission had been spent instead to exchange young people, getting them into homes and camps across Canada, to live together for a time. **What a bridge of understanding such exchanges could form!**"^2 That is very nearly the inter-cultural camping exchange the Secretary of State went on to fund, and which this project has traced through the 1967 Centenary Journey.

**"An Adventure into the Past", 1986 — every camp in Canada invited to stage its own history.** For the second consecutive summer the national association asked its member camps to put their own past into the programme, and this time it printed the invitation in both languages: "**CELEBRATE CCA's 50th BIRTHDAY WITH AN ADVENTURE INTO THE PAST — 1936-1986**… All across the country camps will participate by including one or all of the following activities in their programs: **historical journeys; re-enactment of early camping lifestyles; ceremonies and theme events.** Each participating camp will be recognized with **a wall certificate** and those camps pre-registering will receive **cards for each of their campers**," with registration forms distributed through the provincial associations.^3

Taken with the International Youth Year "historical roots" kit mailed to every camp in May 1985, **that is two successive national prompts, in 1985 and 1986, for camps to research and perform their own histories** — and the second leaves objects behind. A dated wall certificate is the kind of thing that hangs in a dining hall and survives in photographs; a camper's card is the kind of thing that survives in a drawer. Whether Kanawana registered is not documented. The notice is also one more statement of the 1936 dating, against the c.1947 dating its own past president used in 1967.

**What the ACQ did with the summer of 1986.** Its own bilingual report: Employment and Immigration Canada funded **a three-person team that spent the summer reclassifying all of the association's archives and camp dossiers** — the summer, that is, that determined the present arrangement of the ACQ's own records, and a thing worth knowing before asking what it holds on Kanawana. **Three accreditation consultants — Leo Venditti, Yves Legault and Louis Jean — visited 76 camps**, four of them new members. **Jean-Marc Amyot** of the Régie des loteries du Québec's audio-visual service shot **400 slides** at Quebec camps for a bilingual slide show. A pamphlet produced with the ministry promoting outdoor-education classes was printed in **50,000 French and 8,000 English copies** and mailed in September to every school principal and pedagogical consultant in the province, with a second on "Employment in a Summer Camp" planned.^8

**Provincial archives, not only Trent.** Bruno Morawetz's archives report records that the provincial associations had been asked to send newsletters, minutes and notices to Trent so that they "would constitute a permanent record of camping in Canada during 1986," and that the archivist **Bernadine Dodge** had received "several of the brown envelopes commonly known as the '**Brown Bag**'." He adds that "**some Provincial Associations have their own archives** where they collect printed material about their member camps," and hopes all will establish archives committees.^4 With the ACQ's own summer-1986 archive reclassification (recorded just above), that is two independent reasons to ask the Quebec association what it holds rather than only Trent.

**A letter to the provinces, February 1987 — including an instruction to record oral history.** Bruno Morawetz reported that "in February of this year a letter was sent to **all Provincial Archives Chairmen, or to the Provincial President** concerning the collection of material for C.C.A./A.C.C. Archives at Trent University," and urged member camps "to collect as much written material as possible and **have it lodged in your own provincial archives**." He then added the instruction that matters most here: "**One of the ways of collecting archival material is to interview persons who are playing important roles in camping — these too can be lodged and catalogued in an archival collection.**"^5 With the ACQ's own archive reclassification of summer 1986 and Morawetz's note that some provincial associations already held their own archives, that is a third documented prompt inside eighteen months for Quebec camping records — and Quebec camping voices — to be gathered somewhere other than Trent.

**Mary Northway died on 26 February 1987, and the archives this project relies on are largely her doing.** Jocelyn Palm's memorial records that Northway "established the OCA Research Committee and also **engineered the collection and organization of the OCA and CCA Archives now at Trent University**," and that her gift funded the **Neathern Trust**, the endowment the association announced in its fiftieth-anniversary season and which is recorded at [[connections/institutional-lineage/canadian-camping-movement|The Canadian Camping Movement]]. She had attended Northway Lodge, Camp Wapomeo and Glen Bernard, was at Glen Bernard "from 1922 until 1939" as camper and staff member, founded **Windy Pine** with Flora Morrison and deeded it to Trent in 1983, lectured at the Institute of Child Study, founded the Brora Centre on retirement, and took an honorary doctorate from Trent in 1979.^6 Palm adds a precise archive lead: "**In 1981, I interviewed Dr. Northway in order that the tape could be added to the CCA Archives. I have just listened to that tape**" — so a dated Palm-Northway interview tape was in the Trent fonds and playable in 1987.

The clearest statement of the movement's own archive practice comes from an unplanned source: the previous winter's article on the Camp Oconto fire brought in "**a goldmine of material which might otherwise have been lost… from one person who worked on staff at one small camp in Ontario for two years in 1936 and 1937.**" The magazine drew the lesson:^7

> "We must recognize that **today's snapshots, camp brochures, crests, cards and programs are tomorrow's priceless archives. Every camp should be actively archiving. Every camping association should be actively archiving as well. Without gathering our histories together, in a safe organized way we are all losers.**"

And it set out the routing, which matters for anyone looking for Quebec material now: the magazine would publish what it could "**before forwarding it to the Canadian Camping Association Archives at Trent University or to the appropriate provincial camping association archives in the camp's home province**." **That is the third statement in three consecutive issues that the provincial associations held archives of their own** — so material sent to the magazine from a Quebec camp in these years may have gone to the ACQ, not to Trent.

**A documented gap in the run itself.** The same issue's editorial note explains why several provinces are silent in it: "**the postal system blocked**" a number of provincial reports and committee items, and a telephone failure blocked the Personality Profile of Lorne Brown.^7 An absence in the autumn 1987 issue is therefore evidence about Canada Post, not about the associations.

## Open Questions

1. [Critical] **Did Kanawana produce a history for either of these calls?** The International Youth
   Year kit went to every member camp in **May 1985** with a named set of camp-history activities; the
   fiftieth-anniversary invitation, "An Adventure into the Past," went out for the summer of **1986**.
   Ask Concordia's YMCA of Montreal fonds and the camp itself. Nothing here says Kanawana answered
   either, and nothing says it did not.
2. [Important] **What did the provincial associations send in after the February 1987 letter?** It
   asked for newsletters, minutes and photographs, and it instructed them to record oral history. If
   the Quebec association answered, its deposit is the nearest thing to a provincial camp archive for
   Kanawana's own province.
3. [Important] **Where did the Quebec material go?** The magazine set out a routing for what it could
   not print. Whether anything from Quebec travelled it is unknown; the Canadian Camping Association
   fonds at Trent is the place to ask, see `project-docs/trent-access-plan.md`.
4. [Nice-to-have] **Is the Camp Oconto fire material still held?** One camp's fire produced "a goldmine
   of material which might otherwise have been lost" from a single former staff member of 1936-37. It
   is the movement's own worked example of what an appeal to alumni can recover, which is the method
   this project would use on Kanawana.
