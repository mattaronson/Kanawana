# The Canadian Camping Movement

*Status: E1-reviewed | Sources: 18*
*Last Updated: 2026-09-05*

## Summary

Camp Kanawana, founded in 1894 as Camp Jubilee by the YMCA of Montreal, holds a significant position in the Canadian organized camping movement. It is **Quebec's first residential summer camp and among the oldest in Canada** — long described here as "Canada's second-oldest, after Big Cove YMCA Camp in Nova Scotia (1889)", a ranking this article no longer asserts.^1 ^2 **Three camps now claim a start before 1894** and the evidence for each is set out below; see also conflict `c_031`. Every camp in contention for the earliest start is a YMCA institution, underscoring the YMCA's foundational role in Canadian camping. The organized camping movement grew from antimodernist impulses in the late nineteenth century, with the YMCA as its institutional engine, and was later formalized through the Ontario Camping Association (1933) and the Canadian Camping Association (1936).

## Origins: The YMCA and Early Camps (1851-1920)

The Montreal YMCA, founded in 1851 as the first YMCA in North America, was among the earliest organizations to use camping as a tool for youth development.^3 The YMCA's national camping infrastructure was shaped by Edgar Munroe Robinson (1867–1951), appointed Boys' Work Secretary of the YMCA International Committee in 1900, who developed camping programs, father-son events, and edited *Association Boys* (1902–1912).^13 Robinson also co-founded the Boy Scouts of America in 1910.^13 By the 1880s and 1890s, YMCAs across North America were sponsoring camps to improve the health and character of urban youth in response to industrialization and urbanization.

## Taylor Statten and the Professionalization of Camping (1906-1936)

Taylor Statten (1882-1956) was the central figure in the professionalization of Canadian camping.^5 After serving in the Boer War, he joined the YMCA, taking on Boys' Work responsibilities by 1906 (including directing Camp Couchiching) and rising to national Boys' Work Secretary by 1912. In 1914, he established the Canadian Standards Efficiency Training (CSET) program, and by 1918 was running the TUXIS and Trail Rangers programs — enrolling over 30,000 boys across Canada by 1928.^6

In 1921, Statten founded Camp Ahmek on Canoe Lake in Algonquin Park, named after the Ojibwa word for "Great Beaver."^5 In 1924, his wife Ethel opened Camp Wapomeo for girls on the same lake. Pierre Trudeau attended Camp Ahmek as a camper.^5

The founding of the Ontario Camping Association marked the transition from informal to organized camping. Statten served as first chairman, alongside founding members A.L. Cochrane, Mary Edgar, Mary Hamilton, Ferna Halliday, and Ethel Statten.^7 Three sources give three different dates for this founding (1930, 1933, 1938); this KB reads them as three distinct milestones rather than a single disputed date — the OCA organized informally in 1930, was formally chartered/branded in 1933, and Statten's own first full presidency began in 1938 (editorial reconciliation, see Revision History and [[connections/institutional-lineage/taylor-statten|Taylor Statten]] for the full reasoning).

In 1936, the Canadian Camping Association was founded at the Central Y.M.C.A. in Toronto on May 20, with Statten as first president, Mary Edgar as first vice-president, Dr. George S. Patterson as secretary, and Mary C. Donaldson as treasurer.^8 Nelson McEwen, who directed Camp Kanawana as Boys' Work Secretary of the Montreal YMCA from c. 1927 to 1947, co-founded the CCA with Statten, serving as Secretary. McEwen's involvement is documented in Camp Stephens Alumni blog sources, which state McEwen "founded the Canadian Camping Association in 1946" with Statten; since the CCA was formally founded in 1936, McEwen's role may relate to a 1946 reorganization or national assembly.^8n Doug McEwen, possibly Nelson's son, later served as CCA President from 1967 to 1969.^8o

**A founder says the meeting was in Ottawa, not Toronto — an open conflict.** Charles F. Plewman, founder of Camp Kilcoo, past president of the Ontario association and the man who produced the association's first bulletin, was interviewed by Jay Haddad on **15 August 1976** at his Haliburton rest home; the excerpts were printed after his death on 28 December 1981.^8ar Asked how the national body began, he said: "**We did know there were camps in Quebec, so we invited them to a meeting and we all met in Ottawa and decided to have a Canadian Camping Association — and Taylor Statten was its first President.**" That contradicts the account above, which places the founding at the Central Y.M.C.A. in Toronto on 20 May 1936. Both are documented, and they disagree about what happened rather than about how much weight to give a memory, so **the disagreement is recorded and not resolved here — see conflict `c_029`.** It is a separate question from the competing 1936, 1946 and 1949 dates set out in this section: Plewman agrees with 1936 and differs only on the city. The reconciliations worth testing are an organising meeting at Ottawa followed by a constituting meeting in Toronto; a memory at 86 of a meeting forty years past; or a Toronto claim resting on the association's later Toronto address. **The association's own minutes are in the CCA/ACC archives at Trent University**, whose archivist the association named in print in the same issue.^8ar

**And a second witness, at Ottawa, in 1947.** **May Brown** — then director of the YWCA's Camp Oolahwan in Montreal, later President of the CCA from 1965 to 1967 — was asked in a 1976 interview for her first recollections of the association and answered: "**I attended one of the first meetings on the CCA in Ottawa in the spring of 1947; I was really there as a Director of Camp Oolahwan which is the YWCA camp in Montreal.**"^8at She met Mary Edgar there for the first time, and was introduced to British Columbia's delegate by C. R. Blackstock.

She says "one of the first meetings", not the founding, and 1947 is eleven years after 1936, so this does not settle the disagreement either. **What it does is make a pattern out of what were separate anomalies.** This article now carries four items pointing at Ottawa and at the second half of the 1940s: Plewman's recollection of meeting there to decide on a national association; the Camp Stephens claim that Nelson McEwen "founded the Canadian Camping Association in 1946" with Statten, explained above as possibly "a 1946 reorganization or national assembly"; the Newfoundland association's statement that the CCA "was incorporated by Letters Patent at Ottawa November 5, 1949"; and May Brown at one of its first meetings at Ottawa in the spring of 1947.

**A hypothesis worth testing, offered as that and not as a finding:** that a body founded in 1936 out of the Ontario association's circle was constituted as a genuinely national, incorporated association at Ottawa across 1946-49, and that the participants' memories, the Letters Patent and the competing founding dates are all describing parts of one two-stage process rather than contradicting one another. It would reconcile almost every rival claim recorded in this section. It is not established, and nothing in this article is written as though it were; the minutes at Trent are the document that would decide it. See conflict `c_029`.

**The association itself acted on 1936.** In the autumn of 1983 its archives chairman reported that "**Cliff Labbett, Immediate Past President, is currently canvassing all the CCA Past Presidents for ideas to celebrate CCA's 50th Anniversary in 1986.**"^8az A fiftieth anniversary in 1986 places the founding in 1936, and this is the association's own arithmetic, settled by its immediate past president and its archives chairman in the same year a professional archivist took charge of its fonds. A year later the association stated the year outright rather than by inference, in both languages, in the front matter of its own sales catalogue: "**The Canadian Camping Association was founded in 1936 as a non-profit organization**" / "**L'Association des camps du Canada, créée en 1936**."^8bd It does not touch the Ottawa-or-Toronto question — Plewman and the archival holdings agree on 1936 and disagree only about the city — but of the three rival founding *dates* recorded above, **1936 is the one the association planned a birthday around**, which is the strongest available evidence for the first stage of the two-stage reading.

**Two Montreal facts from the same interview.** May Brown was a McGill graduate, and her account places two figures of this history on that campus in the mid-1940s. Of the founder of the Quebec Camping Association: "**Mr. Van Wagner used to lead our outdoor education excursions at McGill**; he was just marvelous!… he could take any group of students out and it wouldn't matter what the weather was like." And of C. R. Blackstock: "Blackie at that time had his own camp (Mazinaw) but **he was up at McGill improving his academic qualifications** and I had just graduated from McGill and we became good friends."^8at With Arthur S. Lamb, the McGill camping school of 1937-40 and Robert Wilkinson's 1981 textbook, that makes McGill a recurring institutional thread through Canadian camping, and one that sits in Kanawana's own city.

Plewman's account of the founding is also the fullest first-hand statement of the movement's origins this project holds. Canada was an American Camping Association unit before it was anything of its own — "about 1930 I was asked to sit in with a group — there were about 4 or 5 people there; they were meeting and **went down to the States once a year to the American Camping Association Convention. We were a part of one of the A.C.A. units**, along with many other units from the northern states" — and Statten's dual standing made the separation possible, since "both Americans and Canadians took to him as he was their (A.C.A.) President too." The argument at the founding was over scope: "**The issue here — because we were all private camp directors — was whether we were concerned with private camps or the broader scope to include agency camps. We had broader visions and wanted to include both.**" That decision is why an agency camp like Kanawana could belong to the association at all — and Plewman adds that "agencies in the first 10 or 15 years didn't play too much of a role… nothing like today", which places the agency camps' arrival in the association's second generation rather than at its founding.^8ar

**And a third date, which is documentary rather than remembered.** The Newfoundland & Labrador Camping Association's entry in the CCA's January 1976 national directory opens: "The Newfoundland & Labrador Camping Association is a Provincial Section of the Canadian Camping Association (**which was incorporated by Letters Patent at Ottawa November 5, 1949**)."^8y

That is not a recollection but a corporate record, and it fits Plewman's own qualification exactly. Plewman dated the Château Laurier meeting to 1946 and added that "for the moment it had no corporate existence"; the corporate existence began on **5 November 1949**. So the competing dates need not compete: organising activity from 1936-37 (which is what the Concordia holdings record, and which is when F. M. Van Wagner describes himself as a "promoter and charter member"), a founding meeting at the Château Laurier in 1946, and incorporation by Letters Patent at Ottawa in November 1949 — three stages of one process rather than three claims about one event. The 1949 date also sits alongside the QCA English Section's own Federal Charter of 1949, which suggests the two incorporations were part of the same round of formalisation.^8v ^8x

**A founder's own account, and what it does to the 1936-vs-1946 question.** Charles F. Plewman
— a founding member of the OCA, its president from the autumn of 1944, and owner-director of
Kilcoo Camp — published a history of the movement's magazine in 1971 in which he dates the
CCA's formation himself: **"Before very long, I think it was in 1946, the Canadian Camping
Association was formed at a meeting held in the Chateau Laurier in Ottawa. It grew out of the
feeling that the time had come to have a national camping association."** He adds that "while
it was called the Canadian Camping Association, in reality, nearly all of its members were
representatives from the Ontario and Quebec Associations. To the best of my knowledge no other
Province had a camping association," and that at that point **"for the moment it had no
corporate existence"** — which fits the incorporation by Letters Patent on 4 November 1949
recorded above.^8t

This is a second independent source for 1946, alongside the Camp Stephens material on Nelson
McEwen, and it is a stronger one: Plewman was present, names a place, and hedges only the year.
It does not overturn the 1936 founding at the Central Y.M.C.A. — he was recalling twenty-five
years later, and 1936 is documented — but it makes the "1946 reorganization" reading
substantially more likely than a simple error, and it supplies the missing venue. **The
question is now what happened at the Château Laurier in 1946**, and the CCA's own minutes, in
the OCA archives deposited at Trent in 1970, are where to look.^8t

Plewman's account also dates the association's early membership plainly: in 1946 it was
effectively **Ontario and Quebec only**, which is consistent with the Quebec Section's 1937
founding and with no other province organising until British Columbia in 1939.^8t Statten also served as president of the American Camp Association in 1942, the only Canadian to hold both offices.^8b The CCA's stated objective was to "further the interests and welfare of children, youths and adults through camping as an educative, recreative, and character developing experience."^8c Its constitution was formally incorporated by Letters Patent on November 4, 1949.^8c

The Quebec Camping Association (QCA) was formed in 1937 as a chartered unit of the CCA.^8d Concordia Archives hold correspondence regarding the founding of the CCA Quebec Section (1936–1937), a QCA constitution from 1937, and executive/members meeting records from 1937 to 1942.^8e A camping school affiliated with the QCA was held at McGill University from 1937 to 1940, representing one of the earliest formal training programs for camp leaders in Canada.^8f The relationship between the QCA and the Association des camps du Québec (ACQ) — of which Camp YMCA Kanawana is an accredited member today — is not one of simple succession, and an earlier version of this article described the ACQ as the QCA's "modern successor." The Canadian Camping Association's own announcement of the two bodies' merger, made by **Bob Lazanik** at the CCA annual meeting in Winnipeg on 28 February 1969 and printed in French in the magazine, gives a different sequence:^8s

- **L'A.C.Q. was founded in 1958** as a separate francophone association — twenty-one years after the QCA — and **joined the QCA in 1961**, the QCA thereafter being known as *la section anglaise*.^8s
- From 1961 a **Conseil Provincial** made up of representatives of both sections coordinated their activities and represented Quebec's camps to the provincial government.^8s
- The question of a workable affiliation formula was **first raised at the CCA's 1963 annual meeting at Banff**, and took "quelque six années d'études, de discussions, d'évaluation et de recherches" to settle.^8s
- The French section's affiliation to the CCA was announced in **February 1969**, placing the Conseil Provincial "au niveau national" so that Quebec's camps had "une voix les représentant tous." The president of the French section was **M. Lionel Perras, p.s.s.**^8s

So the ACQ and the QCA existed as parallel sections of one provincial structure for at least eight years, and the ACQ was never simply the QCA renamed. When the ACQ became the province's single association is not established by any source yet consulted, and should not be inferred from the 1969 merger alone.

A related datum on the two sections' convergence: *Le Guide du moniteur*, Mary L. Northway and Barry Lowes's counsellor handbook, was published in French translation in 1969 — "for the first time in the history of camping, an established book for Camp directors and staff, originally written in English, has been translated into French" — with a preface by **Mme Nicolas Demetelin**, then QCA president, and a foreword by Father Lionel Perras, sold through the QCA at 2233 Belgrave Avenue for $2.95.^8s

## Women in the Camping Movement

Women were pioneers in their own right:

- **Mary Susannah Edgar** (1889-1973) founded Camp Oolahwan, a YWCA girls' camp at Lake Walfred, Quebec, in 1917 — one of the earliest girls' camps in Canada, though Camp Northway (1906) predates it as Canada's oldest all-girls camp. Edgar later opened Glen Bernard Camp (1922) and served as first CCA vice-president (1936).^9
- **Mary Hamilton** founded Camp Tanamakoon in Algonquin Park (1925) and authored *The Call of Algonquin: A Biography of a Summer Camp*. She was a founding OCA member.^7
- **Ferna Halliday** co-founded Camp Ouareau (1922) with Mabel Jamieson and was also an OCA founding member.^7

## Kanawana's Place in the Movement

**Alta R. Kahn, and the QCA's own chronology.** The fullest account the run gives of the Quebec association's internal history is the tribute read when the QCA English Section made **Mrs. Alta R. Kahn its Honorary President on 23 May 1973 — the first in the Association's history**.^8u It supplies dates the Concordia holdings do not.

Kahn "began her camping career with the Quebec Camping Association in **1946**." In **1958** she chaired the joint QCA and Canadian Camping Association conference and "courageously suggested that the Quebec Camping Association celebrate its **'coming-of-age' party** by holding, for the first time, its Conference in a hotel" — a twenty-first birthday in 1958, which independently corroborates the **1937** founding recorded above from the archival holdings. **From 1959 to 1960 she was Co-Chairman of the QCA Standards Committee, "when inspection of camps and accreditation were in the pioneer stage"** — pushing Quebec camp accreditation back several years before the 1965 round this article previously treated as the earliest documented. She was **President of the English Section from 1960 to 1962** and simultaneously an executive member of the CCA; "a major contributor to the realization of the founding of the **Provincial Board** (co-ordinating body of the English and French Sections), and served as the Board's **second President from 1963 to 1965**." And a corporate fact that should be checkable in Quebec's own registers: "**It was through Mrs. Kahn's efforts that the Quebec Camping Association finally secured its Provincial Charter in 1961.**" By 1973 she was still Chairman of the English Section's Standards Committee, Co-Chairman of the joint QCA-ACQ Provincial Board Standards Committee, and a member of the CCA's National Standards Committee — and the tribute credits her with having "assisted greatly in the establishing of Standards in the French-speaking Camps."

This matters for Kanawana directly. Kanawana was an accredited Quebec camp, and Kahn co-chaired the committee that wrote and applied the standards it was measured against, from the pioneer years of 1959-60 through to 1973. Her career before camping is also given: **the first Jewish teacher to graduate from Macdonald College, in 1908**; a founder of Montreal Hadassah in 1918 and organiser of the first Junior Hadassah chapter in Canada in 1920; Executive Director of the **Young Women's Hebrew Association from 1936 to 1945**; and **Executive Secretary of the Jewish Community Camps (B'nai B'rith, Jewish Laurentian Fresh Air, and Wooden Acres) from 1946 to 1949**, during which she "instituted many new projects, including **the first Pre-Camp Training Programs for Staff**." A Montreal figure whose working life ran from Jewish community camping in the Laurentians into the province's camp accreditation machinery. **She died on Thursday, 7 March 1985**, and the obituary printed then — signed by **Frances Kelly** and **Robert Lazanik** — reworks the 1973 tribute and adds two offices: she was **President of the English Section of the Quebec Camping Association from 1960 to 1962**, and, having been "a major contributor to the realization of the founding of the Provincial Board," she served as **that Board's second President from 1963 to 1965**.^8be The two texts disagree on one point: the 1973 tribute gives her Jewish Community Camps service as **1946 to 1949**, the 1985 obituary as **1946 to 1969**. A single-digit slip is the most economical explanation, but they are two documented sources in disagreement and the question is left open as conflict `c_030` rather than resolved by preference.

**The association's own corporate history, and two incorporations.** The Canadian Camping Association's January 1975 national Directory Issue prints the Quebec association's self-description in both languages: "The QUEBEC CAMPING ASSOCIATION INC. — L'ASSOCIATION DES CAMPS DU QUEBEC INC. is a bilingual non-profit organization **incorporated under Quebec law, January 23rd, 1961**. **The English Section was started in 1937, and was incorporated by Federal Charter in 1949.**"^8v

That gives the exact date of the provincial incorporation Alta Kahn was credited with securing, and adds a step this project had not recorded at all: **a Federal Charter for the English Section in 1949**, twelve years before the provincial one. Two separate incorporations, and the 1937 founding date corroborated a third independent time.

**Quebec re-accredited every camp every year.** In the same issue, John Hasell, chairman of the CCA's National Standards Committee, sets out how often camps are revisited: "with some provinces requiring a visit every two or three years, but **Quebec has developed its programme to the point where every camp is re-accredited annually**."^8v

For Kanawana this changes the shape of a known gap. Kanawana was an accredited Quebec camp; Alta Kahn dates Quebec accreditation to the pioneer years of 1959-60; Madelene Allen of Camp Ouareau describes what a visitation actually covered, down to inspecting septic tank outlets after heavy rain and working from a written marking scheme.^8u ^8w So what is missing is not a report but **a series** — an annual, itemised, externally written assessment of the camp's administration, personnel practices, site, programme, health and safety, running from about 1960. Hasell lists what each visitation examined: written objectives, budgets and financial statements, "properly maintained accounts, records and inventories", insurance, transportation, diet, and staff competence assessed by "at least one experienced camp director".^8v Where those reports were kept is the open question — and the same directory shows the QCA English Section had **an Archives committee chairman, Mr. Joseph A. Hood**, in 1975.^8v

**The government arrives alongside the association, 1976.** The association's own newsletter shows a second regulator appearing in Quebec in the last months of 1976, on both sides of the ledger. In October the Quebec news column reports that "**The High Commission of Leisure and Sports has provided funds to ACQ-QCA so that a full time person may be hired to set up provincial training programmes in camping**" — a provincially funded, full-time camp-training post inside the accrediting body itself.^8z Two months later the same column reports the other side: "**A somewhat scary proposal is now being made by the Department of Labour…Waterfronts in camps may not be deeper than six feet!!!**", together with the formation of "**a very active Day Camp Standards Committee**" in the province.^8z The exclamation marks are the association's, and they register how a body that had written and applied camp standards in Quebec since 1955 received a government department proposing to write one of its own. Quebec was not alone in this — in the same December column New Brunswick, Saskatchewan and Manitoba report government grants conditional on association membership or accreditation, and British Columbia reports being asked to run the in-service training for the Department of Health's own camp inspectors.^8z Whether a six-foot depth rule was ever enacted, and what it would have meant for a camp whose waterfront is a natural lake, is not stated; but by the winter of 1976-77 a Quebec camp such as Kanawana was answerable to a provincial ministry as well as to the association that accredited it.

**February 1977: the scale of the Quebec government's involvement, and a fortieth birthday.** Three months after the Department of Labour proposal, the association's Quebec column reports the other side of the same relationship in figures: "**Support from the Provincial Government is still very evident in this Province, and a close liaison exists when it comes to new legislation. $300,000 is being given to the Association for their spring Training Programme.**"^8aa That is a provincial grant an order of magnitude larger than any other reported in the run — Nova Scotia's recreation grant the same winter was $3,600 — and it was for staff training, the function the association had performed on its own since 1937. **Rosaire Corbin** is named as the ACQ-QCA officer responsible for the training programmes. The province also mounted a promotional campaign, **"Camp Week," 5-11 March 1977**, with "promotional spots on radio and television; advertisements and public service announcements in every newspaper; buttons, posters, badges" — a marketing push on behalf of every accredited camp in Quebec, Kanawana included.^8aa

The same column supplies a fourth independent corroboration of the QCA English Section's founding date: "**Highlight of the spring will no doubt be the 40th Anniversary Banquet of the English section on March 26th.**"^8aa Forty years before March 1977 is 1937 — the year given by the Concordia holdings, by Alta Kahn's 1958 "coming-of-age" conference, and by Van Wagner's tribute. Four sources of three different kinds now agree.

**What membership bought, concretely: foreign counsellors.** The same issue records a change in Canadian immigration practice that applied to Kanawana by virtue of its membership. "**The Employment Visa Certification Waiver has been amended to permit non-immigrants to work as camp counsellors, or in similar leadership roles, (not as kitchen staff or maintenance workers) in camps operated by the Canadian Camping Association. They do not need Canada Manpower certification that residents of Canada are unavailable for such employment.**"^8aa A foreign counsellor needed only a letter from the camp director stating the occupation, location and duration of employment "and particularly, that the employer is a member of the Canadian Camping Association," presented with a passport or birth certificate at the point of entry; provincial associations were told to send their member-camp directories to every port of entry in the province, "explaining that membership in the Provincial Association automatically means membership in the Canadian Camping Association."^8aa Accreditation was therefore not only a standards regime and a condition of government grants but a hiring instrument: from 1977 an accredited camp could recruit international staff without a labour-market test, and an unaccredited one could not.

The arrangement was already being reconsidered a year later, and the mechanics changed with the revised Immigration Act of **10 April 1978**. Duncan R. Campbell, Executive Director of Labour Market Policy at the Ministry of Employment and Immigration, wrote to the CCA's president that "**it was agreed that job offers made to foreign camp counsellors were not to be exempt from Manpower validation in 1978**," but that changing it mid-season "would pose difficulties," so "**the present exemption from Manpower Certification will be retained for the 1978 summer season, with the change likely coming this fall**."^8ae Under the new Act a non-immigrant had to obtain **employment authorization before arriving at a port of entry**: the camp sent a detailed offer of employment establishing "**that the employer is a member in good standing of the Canadian Camping Association**," the prospective counsellor took it to a Canadian embassy or consulate to obtain Employment Authorization Form Imm. 1102, and both documents were presented at the border, with a social insurance number also required.^8ae So the exemption Kanawana's staffing benefited from ran for the seasons of 1977 and 1978 and was expected to end that autumn — a narrow, datable window.

**The first national statistics, 1976.** The association had never had reliable numbers, and said so plainly when it published its first annual statistical questionnaire in March 1977: it needed figures "in order to appear to be an organization with a strong foundation, and a knowledgeable spokesman for camping," and some directors had refused to take part, "feeling that we were invading their privacy, or that the figures would be used to embarrass them." The editor's answer to them is worth preserving: "**if we are unable to provide reasonable figures for the Government, we will then be legislated into answering certain questions… with no option to be coy or evasive!**"^8ab

The projected national figures for the 1976 season: **68,000 camper spaces**; **260,000 campers attended member camps**; **32,000 staff, of whom 22,000 were paid and 10,000 were volunteers, and 1,600 came from outside Canada**; **food costs of $8,000,000** excluding salaries, fuel and kitchen supplies, and **$14,000,000 of other costs** excluding food and salaries. Quebec was one of four associations whose members responded at over 50%.^8ab These are the only national figures the run produces, and they give the denominator against which Kanawana's own numbers can be read — a camp registering about 190 children a season was one part in roughly fourteen hundred of Canadian organized camping. The 1,600 foreign staff also give the scale of the visa waiver described above: an accreditation benefit that touched about one camp employee in twenty.

**And a second set, five years later, broken down by province.** In October 1981 the association printed a table of figures for **1980-81** province by province, without commentary, beneath a special report by **Rosaire Corbin, directeur général of the Association des Camps du Québec**.^8aq Three cautions have to travel with any number taken from it. First, Corbin says the figures "**ont la valeur objective de savants calculs d'extrapolation effectués afin de remédier aux 45% des non-répondants**" — **they are extrapolations covering a 45% non-response rate**. Second, **the column headings are set in rotated type and are illegible in the scanned copy**; the numbers are clean but the labels are not, and a clean copy should be obtained before they are named. Third, what follows is therefore given as figures with a reading attached, not as a labelled table.

Nationally the row totals are **574** in the first column, **313,953** in the second, **37,690** in the third and **228** in the fourth. The second and third sit squarely in line with the association's own 1976 projections of 260,000 campers and 32,000 staff, which makes campers and staff the natural reading of those two columns and implies growth of roughly a fifth over five years; the first column reads as the number of camps; the fourth is not identifiable. **Quebec's row is 125 · 63,243 · 5,694 · 40**, second only to Ontario's 197 · 91,281 · 13,339 · 60.

For this project the useful figure is the denominator, and it is the best one the run supplies for the 1980s: **Quebec had on the order of 125 organized camps in 1980-81, in a Canadian field of about 574, and Kanawana was one of them.**

**And the number that reframes all of them: about one camp in ten belonged.** In November 1982 the association's own Executive Director, **Marjorie Booth**, was asked what percentage of Canadian camps were members and answered "**depending on the area of the country, probably 10 to 12**"; her interviewer restated it as "roughly then, only 10% of camps operating in this country are members of their provincial association", and she let it stand.^8aw

**Every figure in this section counts member camps, so this is the multiplier that has to travel with them.** If 574 member camps were a tenth of the field, Canada had on the order of five thousand camps operating, and the association's statistics describe a self-selected, better-resourced minority rather than Canadian camping as a whole. For this project the consequence is direct: **Kanawana's membership of the Quebec association is not a neutral administrative fact but a marker of a particular kind of camp**, and any comparison between Kanawana and "Canadian camps" drawn from CCA data is a comparison with the accredited tenth.

Booth's account of who stayed out explains the shape of that tenth: "there are a number of small organizations or church-affiliated camping programmes in this country that **don't have the financial resources — at least in their minds — to meet some of the recommended practices and so they choose not to belong**"; and, separately, "**if camp directors don't know we exist, they can't join the association**… Nationally, we're not well known."^8aw She also states the rule that applied to Kanawana: in **Ontario and Quebec**, "**in order to become a member camp, you must meet the standards or recommended practices and be visited by a consultant or your peers**" — so in Quebec, membership and accreditation were a single gate, which is why the two words are used interchangeably about Kanawana in this wiki. The alternative she floats, and which some provinces used, was a two-tier membership: "one that's an accredited member and one that is a member which has not met the standards or desirable practices", letting any camp belong "as long as they pay the fee and subscribe to the goals and objectives of that association."

**Where Quebec sat in the national picture, in her words.** "In the prairies and Atlantic provinces, most of the camps are organization-run, agency-run or religiously based. **There is a huge difference in Ontario, Quebec, and to some degree British Columbia**, where many camps operate for longer periods of time — two or four weeks for a session. As well, most of the camps are non-denominational and are able to attract a clientele who are able to pay more to go to camp than you would find in other parts of the country."^8aw And on staff: elsewhere "campers don't stay on as campers until they're 19… they're almost forced to [leadership] earlier because they are needed to be leaders; **this is so foreign to camps in Quebec and Ontario, for example, where you'll often have 16 and 17 year-old campers who return year after year.**" Long sessions, higher fees, non-denominational, older campers returning: that is the category Kanawana belonged to, described by the person whose job it was to know.

**A labelled national table for the same two years.** Nine months later the association printed its national statistics with the headings intact, compiled by Marjorie Booth in March 1982.^8at For **1980** and **1981** respectively: accredited member camps **590** and **533**, with 44 non-accredited member camps in 1981; day camps **126** and **125**; resident camps **453** and **468**; camper beds **64,276** and **74,762**; camper/days **3,962,848** and **4,046,388**; volunteer staff **18,405** and **12,247**; paid staff **19,282** and **20,539**; staff/days **1,125,676** and **1,151,255**; staff who were students **18,787** and **16,784**; food costs **$11,454,000** and **$12,607,000**; salaries **$17,279,000** and **$19,448,000**; foreign campers **9,790** and **9,130**; foreign counsellors **1,076** and **1,268**.

Booth's own caveat is as important as the numbers: "**the 1980 data was based on a response from eight provincial associations; the 1981 figures represent provincial totals for six associations**", and she notes that some resident camps are double-counted as extended-season camps and that the rise in camper/days is "probably due to the longer summer vacation period (10 weeks) for students". **These are partial aggregates of provincial returns, not a census**, and any figure taken from them carries that.

The labelled table also decodes most of the illegible one. Its 1980 volunteer and paid staff sum to **37,687**, three away from the provincial table's third-column total of 37,690, which identifies **column 3 as total staff**; the camp counts of 590 and 577 bracket the provincial table's 574, identifying **column 1 as camps**; and column 2's 313,953 is consistent with a head count of campers rather than camper/days. **Column 4 remains unidentified** and is queued as `p_324`. Nothing in this article labels those columns. Corbin's argument for collecting the numbers at all is the frankest statement in the run of what the associations did not know about their own members — that when a government or a university asked "qui représentez-vous?, combien d'enfants accueillez-vous dans vos camps? combien de personnes engagez-vous?", the answers had to be prefaced with "à peu près", and "la solidité de l'argumentation en est automatiquement affaiblie."^8aq

**How the CCA became a bilingual association, and who made it one.** The constitutional change is dated to a single meeting by the tribute the association printed when its Quebec vice-president left in December 1977: "**When the C.C.A. Board was struggling with the revision of our constitution at a meeting in Regina in 1971, it was Paul Belanger who convinced us in his delicately kind and inimitable way that we ought to give warm recognition to our two languages in the C.C.A. We became convinced, committed and constituted and, out of the Regina meeting, emerged Le Comité des Camps Français and a new constitution with this stated purpose — 'To promote the development of Camping in Canada with the highest ideals and traditions of both language groups consistent with the national heritage of Canada.'**"^8ac

**Abbé Paul Belanger** founded **Camp-École Keno in 1964**, about sixty miles northwest of Quebec City; sat on the Quebec Camping Association's Provincial Board; was President of the Association des Camps du Québec and the **first President of the CCA's French Language Camping Committee**; was the ACQ's delegate to the Fédération de canot-camping; chaired **the first Canadian Conference on French Language Camping, at the Montmorency Forest in April 1976**; and was Vice-President of the CCA until his order, the Marists, called him to Rome as an Assistant General in late 1977, with responsibility for Quebec, New England and France.^8ac He was honoured at an ACQ-QCA dinner in Quebec City on **24 November 1977**. A French tribute by **Rosaire Poussard** was printed beside the English one, itself an artefact of the bilingualism Belanger had argued for.

For Kanawana the point is structural. The association that accredited it was a single English body until 1971 and a formally bilingual one afterwards, and the Quebec section Kanawana belonged to sat on both sides of that change — the English Section under Derek Walsh's presidency, the French Section under Belanger's, each supplying a vice-president to the joint Provincial Board.^8ac

**A National Camping Day, 14 April 1983.** The first of the three pre-Congress workshops was on promotion, chaired by **Marnie Ellis** of the Quebec association, and Booth described its intended product: "Marnie Ellis and her committee have worked hard to provide a hands-on experience for **two delegates per province** who can then promote camping more knowledgeably back in their own provinces. **The final result of this initiative will be a National Camping Day which will be focusing on the value of the camp counsellor's contribution to the children of this country. This will be happening on April [1st].** Hopefully, we can build on this and watch it become an annual event."^8aw *(The day in that last sentence is a decoding artefact — the issue it comes from was machine-read upside down. The following issue gives the date plainly and twice: "**Thursday, April 14th. This is National Camping Day**", and "National Camping Day was chosen as **April 14** by the Board of CCA at its meeting last May in Saskatoon."^8ax **National Camping Day 1983 was 14 April.**)*

**What it actually was.** The pre-Congress promotion workshop of **25-26 January 1983** had **eighteen delegates** from the nine member provinces, its second day given entirely to National Camping Day, each delegate to carry it home.^8ax The date was chosen for the gap after the workshop and because it fell "still early enough that the publicity would benefit the majority of camps doing their registration"; the long-term aim was "to educate the public about camps in Canada, and to spotlight ourselves enough to get a fund-raising commitment so that CCA can expand its services to its member camps." Campers were asked "**to wear their camp shirts, hats or jackets, or some identification of their camp on this special day**". It had a prehistory: "**National Camping Day is not a new idea. Conceived in the 1960's**… **the B.C. Camping Association spawned the t-shirt day**, and for several years B.C. campers have worn their camp t-shirts on that special day. **The idea was endorsed by all provinces at a Halifax meeting in 1981.**"^8ax A dated national publicity day, run through the Quebec chairman of the national promotion committee, on which every member camp's campers were asked to wear camp clothing — findable in the press, and worth asking the ACQ about. On standards she was explicit about what the parallel workshop was not for: "**we're not trying at all to introduce a National Accreditation programme; that's years away**", though national recognition of recommended practices "would be in our favour to be able to say when asked: 'when you choose a CCA camp, you get assured of…'".

**Where the Action Plan came from.** Booth dates it: "**Fitness Canada funded a meeting of the Board, committee chairmen and task force chairmen from across the country to meet at Camp Wanakita, two years ago**", where the goals and direction for three years were determined.^8aw That identifies the **Leadership Resources Workshop at Camp Wanakita, 16-18 October 1980**, recorded elsewhere in this run as producing "a three to five year plan for the C.C.A.", as the origin of the Action Plan that governed everything the association did from 1981 to 1983. Federal money paid for two staff positions, one full-time and one part-time, and the travel for two Board and two Executive meetings a year; the association's stated target was to be "**50% self-supporting in our core operation by the year 1985-86**", with the publications service as the instrument, "because governments are cutting back everywhere."

**A Montreal professor taught camp law at the Congress.** The first International Camping Congress was held at Toronto's Sheraton Centre, **26-29 January 1983** — over a thousand delegates from Japan, England, New Zealand, the United States, Australia, Norway and Canada, more than ninety sessions, 1,100 at the closing banquet, four years in preparation.^8ax Among the sessions was "**Camper Safety and the Law** — legal responsibility for camper injury, common areas of litigation and appropriate preventative measures", run by "**Dr. Robert E. Wilkinson, from McGill University, Montreal**".^8ax The author of the movement's textbook and a member of the Quebec association through Camp Nominingue (see above) was therefore on the platform of the movement's largest event, teaching liability — which confirms a Montreal presence at the Congress as delivered rather than merely announced. **Every session was recorded and sold on cassette** by Audio Archives of Canada at $9 apiece, with the full session list printed; see `p_325`.

**The physical artefact of membership.** The magazine's advice column describes the certificate system, which is worth knowing because the object would have hung in a camp office: "**there is one for camps accredited by the provincial standards committee and another for member camps who are not accredited. Once the camp receives the member camp certificate, the CCA/ACC forwards the 'year stickers' to all the provincial presidents. These are sent to each camp to keep the certificate up to date.**"^8ax A Kanawana **CCA/ACC member camp certificate carrying year stickers** is therefore a specific, describable thing to ask former staff and the Concordia fonds about, and its stickers would date the camp's membership year by year (see `p_323`).

**A national conference in Quebec, 1974.** One event the run mentions only in a catalogue entry is worth recording, because it is the association's own national programme meeting in the province Kanawana was accredited in. The CCA's booklet *Outdoor Education — Guidelines for Camps* "were developed by a committee **chaired by Walter D. Mingie** at an **Outdoor Education Conference sponsored by the CCA/ACC at La Caleche, Quebec in 1974**".^8av Its subject — the administrative, staffing and site resources a camp needs to let outside groups use it for outdoor education in the off-season — bears directly on what a Quebec camp could do with its buildings outside July and August, and Mingie was of Camp Nominingue. The place name is almost certainly La Calèche; it is not located here, and whether Kanawana sent anyone is unknown. The booklet was **revised in 1981** and was still on sale in 1984-85.^8bd Both the conference and its papers are items for the Trent enquiry.

**And a past president says plainly that it did not work.** Everything above is drawn from the association's own copy, which is uniformly positive about its bilingualism. The one dissenting voice in the run is **John Latimer**, national president from 1971 to 1975 — the years the constitution changed — asked in August 1982 to name the volatile issues of his presidency:

> "**I personally felt uncomfortable about the relationship between French camps and camps in English-speaking Canada. This, unfortunately, is an ongoing concern even today, wrought with political overtones — I know I've made enemies over this issue** but I keep thinking we're forgetting the campers and their collective interests which aren't being served by our politics."^8au

That is his view and is recorded as his view, not as a finding about what happened. But it is the only candid statement of the problem this project has, and it comes from the man who held the chair while the constitution was being rewritten. Read beside the disappearance of the Quebec English Section from the printed lists and the ACQ's emergence as the province's single national voice, it suggests that what those printed lists show as a tidy institutional simplification was experienced from inside as something harder. In the same interview Latimer records that the association never resolved two other questions of his term: it wanted to change its name, because "**we were and still are assumed by the public to be 'that group in charge of trailer camping throughout Canada'**", and never did; and on national standards, "**this issue still isn't resolved — there are no national standards**, and I think it would be very difficult to implement the same standards to all camps across the country."^8au

**Where the Quebec association actually met.** The association's calendar for 1978 records its English-section executive meeting **at the Montreal YMCA** on 13 February, 13 March, 10 April, 8 May and 12 June.^8ad The provincial body that accredited Kanawana, then presided over by Kanawana's own director, held its monthly executive meetings in the building of the institution that owned the camp. The same calendar gives the section's public programme for the year — a general meeting on 28 February titled "**Looking at Camp Thru the Eyes of a Camper**," built around a panel of four campers; the QCA Conference on 17-18 March; a general meeting on 18 April, "**Let's Get Ready for the Summer**"; a Counsellor Conference and a Standards Meeting on 27 May — alongside the French section's own calendar of stages de plein air, soupers-causeries and a séminaire des directeurs de camps on 7-9 April.^8ad Minutes or attendance lists from any of these would place Kanawana's director by name and date.

**Van Wagner made President Emeritus, 1978.** The association's last honour to the man who founded the Quebec Camping Association came in December 1978, when the CCA executive appointed **F. M. Van Wagner President Emeritus** and printed his handwritten reply.^8af The accompanying notice restates his record in the association's own words and adds one thing this article had only by inference: "**He shared in the founding of the C.C.A. in 1936 and is one of its Charter Members. The next year he organized the Quebec Camping Association, English Section, and became its first President.**" It also dates his honorary membership to **1974** and gives the Award of Merit as coming from the ACQ-QCA Provincial Board, with the Centennial Medal in 1967.^8af Taken with Charles Plewman's own testimony — the Kilcoo founder, speaking at 87 on being made an honorary member, said "when the Canadian Camping Association was formed back in 1936, it was an act of faith, pure and simple" — that is a second charter member dating the founding to 1936 in the association's own pages, which bears on the competing 1946 date recorded in the sources below.^8af ^8n

**The Quebec section in the autumn of 1978, at work.** Jack Pearse's account of a presidential visit to Montreal that October records a working afternoon of the kind the QCA's calendar had been advertising: "a panel concerning **staff recruitment, training and supervision** with **Barb Patterson, Ron Braverman and Bob Lazanik**, organized by **Ken Willis, Herb Finkelberg and the English Section of Q.C.A.**", followed by "a superb dinner meeting of A.C.Q.-Q.C.A. **en deux langues**" chaired by Abbé Robert Maisonneuve.^8af Braverman is of Pripstein's Camp (see [[connections/related-camps/quebec-camp-landscape|The Quebec Camping Landscape]]) and Lazanik a QCA past president; Pearse also visited **Tony Shorgan and Yves Beauregard at the Society for Crippled Children** and lunched with Maisonneuve, **Raymond Hade** and **Lucille Cardinal**. The subject of the panel — recruiting, training and supervising staff — is the same subject the Quebec association had scheduled a souper-causerie on in February 1978, and the same one every Kanawana director's report of the period is preoccupied with.

**A CCA conference in Montreal in 1960.** The same issue reprints an address by **Alan F. Klein, Professor of Social Group Work at the University of Pittsburgh**, given "to a C.C.A. Conference in Montreal in 1960."^8af That is a dated national conference held in Montreal during the three years the CCA's national office was there under Van Wagner's presidency, and a further occasion whose records would name Quebec camps and camp people.

**The English Section drops out of the printed list, December 1979.** The association's list of provincial associations in October 1978 carried three Quebec entries — the **Quebec Camping Association, Inc.** under Dr Ken Willis at 2233 Belgrave Avenue, the **Association des Camps du Québec** under René Rousseau, and the joint **ACQ-QCA Provincial Board** under Abbé Robert Maisonneuve.^8aa By December 1979 the same list carries **one**: "**Association des Camps du Québec — M. Raymond Bonneau, President — 1415 est, rue Jarry, Montréal**."^8ai The English Section and the joint Provincial Board are both absent, and the calendar in the same issue lists only ACQ events ("A.C.Q. Semaine des Camps", "A.C.Q. Assemblée Annuelle").

**And no Quebec name on the national executive after May 1981.** The 1981-83 executive installed with President **Jocelyn Palm** at the annual meeting in **Moncton, New Brunswick, 21-24 May 1981** — held with the Spring Board Meeting and the New Brunswick Camping Association conference — was Cliff Labbett as Past President, George Matthews (Eastern), **Jane McCutcheon (Central)** and Gary Luthy (Western) as Vice-Presidents, Jan James as Secretary and George Ross as Treasurer.^8ap Derek Walsh, whose vice-presidency had covered Quebec and Ontario together and who had agreed the previous December "to stay on as the representative of Quebec and Ontario until May, 1981", is succeeded by McCutcheon, who is of London, Ontario (see [[people/derek-walsh|Derek Walsh]]). **From that meeting the region containing Quebec is represented nationally by an Ontario officer, and no Quebec-resident name appears on the executive.** Taken with the disappearance of the English Section from the printed list of provincial associations, that is the second indication in three years of Quebec's English-language camping organisation receding from the national body's visible structure. The new executive's brief was to "implement the **Action Plan adopted by the C.C.A./A.C.C. Board last January**" — a January 1981 national planning document worth asking the association's archive for by that name. It was the association's real operating framework: at the semi-annual Board meeting at **Halifax, 20-22 November 1981**, reports were received from **twenty committee and task force chairmen** on their responsibilities "as outlined in the Action Plan", budgets were approved for 1982-83 and for the 1983 Congress, and **a submission to Fitness Canada requesting $109,617** was authorised, with the answer expected in late March 1982; the 1982 Annual Meeting was set for **Camp Rayner, Saskatchewan, 30 April – 2 May**, and minutes were available to members through the provincial presidents.^8ar In the spring of 1982 the president reported that "**the CCA/ACC has recently been advised that our application for status as a registered Canadian Charitable Institution has been accepted**", a corporate fact checkable against the federal charities register and a second dating point beside the 1949 Letters Patent.^8at

By Winter 1982 the association's printed list of national and provincial offices gives Quebec a single line — "**Association des Camps du Québec, 1415 est rue Jarry, Montréal, Québec H2E 2Z7, Téléphone: (514) 374-4700 ex 447**" — and no English-language body at all.^8ar That is the third such list in four years to carry one Quebec entry where October 1978 carried three.

The national promotion portfolio, meanwhile, stayed in Quebec hands. The Central Region report of the same issue names "**Marnie Ellis, our Public Relations and Promotion Chairman, and an active A.C.Q. member**", chairing a National Workshop on Promotion for the 1983 Congress — the office Derek Walsh had held from 1977, now held by another member of the Quebec association.^8ar So Quebec's institutional presence at the national table survived the disappearance of its English section from the directory; what changed was which Quebec body it came through.

**Quebec held three national posts at once in the spring of 1982.** Jocelyn Palm's President's Perspective lists every committee and task force chairman with their province, and Quebec appears three times: **Marnie Ellis (Qué.)** on Public Relations and Promotions; "**Rosaire Corbin (Qué.) chairs the French Language Committee, and we are constantly seeking his input in order to develop an adequate picture of the French Language members of CCA/ACC**"; and "**Derek Walsh (Qué.) is investigating the realities of publishing a Canadian Camping Magazine**".^8as Corbin was simultaneously the Association des Camps du Québec's *directeur général*, so the same man held the province's chief executive post and the national French-language chairmanship at once — and the national body was, forty-six years after its founding and eleven years after Belanger's constitutional change, still trying to form "an adequate picture" of its own French-language membership.

**The association did not know its own early history, and said so in 1982.** In the autumn of 1982 the CCA printed a boxed appeal over the Trent archivist's name, headed "**CATCH UP WITH YOUR PAST**":

> "Children's camping is older than most of us realize. In time for the coming International Camping Congress and for our own information, **we need to know who opened the first camp in Canada. As well, we would like to co-ordinate a list of camps in Canada which were opened prior to 1900, 1910, and 1920.** Please dig into provincial government departments (education, health, land titles/surveys) as well as those of Church, YWCA, YMCA, Boy Scouts, Girl Guides, CGIT, and private records. **This kind of information has never been researched in detail** — we may be surprised at the number of early camps established in Canada."^8au

Three things follow for this project. **Kanawana dates itself from 1894 and belongs on the "prior to 1900" list**, so if the Quebec association or the YMCA of Montreal answered, an independent statement of the camp's founding date was written down in 1982 and sent to Trent — where the compilation, gathered "in time for the coming International Camping Congress", would sit with the Congress papers. **The list of places to search is a good checklist for Kanawana's own pre-1900 record**: provincial education, health and land-titles or survey departments, alongside the Y's own files. And the admission itself is worth keeping: the national body, at forty-six, was telling its members that the early history of Canadian camping had never been researched in detail. See `p_319`.

**The ACQ's own annual report for 1981-82, in French.** Printed among the nine provincial reports and signed **Claire Menard**, it names the year's two priorities and one document this project should have: "Les activités de l'ACQ ont gravité autour de deux pôles en 1981-82 soit le **Congrès 82** et la promotion. Sous le thème: **Les Vacances, Facteur de Croissance**, le Congrès qui s'est déroulé au mois de mars a rencontré un franc succès sur la qualité de l'organisation et des sessions offertes aux participants. La promotion de l'ACQ a voulu au cours de l'année à affermir sa représentation et l'image des camps du Québec par la publication de **l'Annuaire 82**, par une **Semaine des camps** intensive, par une présence de plus en plus incitante auprès des media et du publique. **Il est à espérer que la fréquentation des camps de vacances se maintiendra et s'accroîtra.**"^8at

Three things follow. **Claire Menard** is a new name for the Quebec association's officers — and she was working outside the province: at the Saskatchewan association's spring conference in Saskatoon on 1 May 1982, "'Parachute Play to Music', a very popular session, was given by **Claire Menard and Michel Charron**, **proving that there is no language division where fun and laughter are concerned**".^8au A second new Quebec name, and evidence that the ACQ's people were teaching at other provinces' conferences rather than only at their own. **The *Annuaire 82* is a dated, published directory of Quebec camps** — Kanawana was an accredited Quebec camp and would be listed in it, which makes it the most directly useful Quebec document this run has named (queued as `p_326`). And the closing sentence is a hope rather than a report: the ACQ was worried about attendance in exactly the years this project's own record of Kanawana's enrolment is thinnest.

**The foreign-counsellor protocol was still running, and one camp was on probation.** Barry Lowes's Immigration Task Force reports that "the protocol worked out with the Federal Ministry of Employment and Immigration, **at our annual meeting in Ottawa with senior officials**, has worked so well that it was continued without change for 1981", and prints a table of **foreign camp counsellors by province for 1980 and 1981, sourced to Canada Manpower & Immigration** under occupational code CCDO 2333-126 — 828 nationally in 1980 and 820 in 1981, distinguishing those admitted with Manpower clearance from those admitted under exemption codes such as Working Holiday Programs and Canadian Crossroads.^8at *(The provincial columns are not legibly OCR'd in the copy read, so no Quebec figure is given here; recovering it is part of `p_324`.)* His warning names a real sanction: "**One camp brought in a staff member under what appeared to be false pretenses. The person and the camp was castigated by the Regional and Federal offices. The camp is on probation for 1982**… **The privilege extended by the Federal Government could be revoked if it is believed that any camp or camps is circumventing the agreed protocol.**" The camp is not named in the source and is not identified here. So the hiring instrument described above for 1977-78 was not only still in force in 1982 but was being policed, with federal statistics kept by province.

**Frances Kelly honoured, and the English Section called "former" by the national body.** At the ACQ Conference at **Laval in March 1982**, where the national executive also met, "**Jocelyn Palm presented Miss Frances Kelly, former secretary of the English Section of the Quebec Camping Association, with a CCA/ACC Certificate of Appreciation. Although Miss Kelly was honoured in Quebec, her long service to the camping movement was recognized by the CCA/ACC Board.**"^8as That is the second national Certificate of Appreciation this project has found going to an officer of the Quebec English Section, both described in the same past tense — Derek Walsh received his in January 1983 as "a Past President of the **former** English Section" (see [[people/derek-walsh|Derek Walsh]]). Two honours to a body the national association was, by then, describing as finished.

Absence from a printed list is weak evidence and is recorded here as that, not as proof of dissolution. But it is consistent with something the project has from another direction: the citation for Derek Walsh's 1983 CCA award thanks him "as a Past President of the **former** English Section – Quebec Camping Association" (see [[people/derek-walsh|Derek Walsh]]). The English Section still appeared with its own president and address in October 1978 and had gone from the list by December 1979 — which brackets the change, if not the date of it, and gives a place to start looking for what happened.

**A Quebec annual meeting announced, then moved.** Ken Willis invited the CCA to hold its 1980 annual meeting in Quebec, and the invitation was minuted at Wolfville in May 1979 and printed in the calendar that October as "**Annual Meeting — P.Q. — May**."^8ah By December the calendar reads "**C.C.A. Board Meeting/Toronto, May 8-9**" and "**C.C.A. Annual Meeting/Toronto, May 9**."^8ai Whatever the reason, the national meeting did not go to Quebec that spring.

**The Quebec association's own staff-training curriculum, dated February 1976.** From February 1980 the national newsletter began serialising extracts from "the **Quebec Camping Association's Director Education Program**" — in French, as "**Formateurs-multiplicateurs**", and dated: "Ce programme… **était préparé par l'Association des Camps du Québec en février 1976**."^8aj It is the only Quebec camp-training document this project has seen in full, and it covers exactly the period of Kanawana's own thinnest records.

The first instalment is on recruitment and selection. Recruitment draws on "**le personnel actuel, les anciens du camp, le milieu scolaire, collégial, universitaire**", specialised faculties — "**récréologie, éducation physique, psychologie**" — the **centre de main-d'œuvre** (the Manpower centre through which Camp Ouareau found its chef), and "**le service offert par l'A.C.Q.**", so the association ran a placement service of its own. Selection is by interview, prepared with a form: "**un formulaire est nécessaire pour obtenir les renseignements utiles… (cf. feuille A.C.Q.)**" — **the Quebec association issued a standard interview sheet to its member camps**, which is a specific document to look for.

The advice itself is more thoughtful than the equivalent English-language material of the period. On the setting: "**le style de l'entrevue détermine le style de leadership d'un camp**." On the questions: they must draw out ideas and feelings rather than yes-or-no answers, and must not be confined to the camp but be "une occasion de se connaître mutuellement" — with four samples given, of which two are "**Qu'est-ce que tu respectes chez les autres?**" and "**Quel genre de musique préfères-tu écouter lorsque tu es joyeux?**" And it closes by turning the assessment on the interviewer: "Avez-vous écouté ou avez-vous trop parlé? Avez-vous laissé le temps au candidat de finir son histoire, ou si vous l'avez interrompu? **Avez-vous vraiment écouté ou si vous étiez impatient d'entendre la réponse que vous désiriez?**"

Kanawana was an accredited member of this association throughout, so this is the training its directors were offered and the form its interviews were expected to follow. (Compare the CCA's own English-language interview rating chart of December 1977, which scores candidates one to ten on appearance, composure, maturity, self-confidence, frankness, leadership potential and experience with children.^8af)

**The standard camp forms, named and priced — the most actionable archival lead in this run.** In March 1980 the Ontario Camping Association advertised a kit of "**Suggested Camp Forms**", "**$3.30 per set**", printed on 8½ × 11 paper, from the same Avenue Road office the CCA shared.^8ak Nine forms were in it:

> **Camper Application Form · Camp Staff Application Form · Staff Health Certificate · Camper's Health Certificate · Staff Contract Letter · Staff Reference Form · Counsellor Evaluation Form · Camper Evaluation Report Form · Accident Report Form**

That list matters more than its length suggests. Every document class this project has been reconstructing piecemeal from the camping press — the counsellor evaluation Barry Lowes described (see [[people/leo-robitaille|Leo J. Robitaille]]), the reference-checking form reviewed in 1979, the "illness and accident record forms" J. Harry Ebbs told directors to keep (see [[site/the-kanawana-site|The Kanawana Site]]) — turns out to have existed as a **purchasable, standardised set**, sold by a member association and explicitly designed to be copied or adapted: "These forms may be copied or you may add or delete anything that may be desirable or undesirable for your own special situation."^8ak **Anyone searching Kanawana's records should ask for these nine documents by these names.**

The **Camper's Health Certificate is reproduced in full** in the same issue, and is a period document in its own right. The parent completes a checklist — chicken pox, asthma, hepatitis, red and german measles, frequent colds, hernia, hay fever, rheumatic fever, fainting spells, heart condition, sinus trouble, severe stomach aches, diabetes, mumps, recent whooping cough, sleep walking, bed wetting — and, for girls, "**Has girl menstruated? Yes/No. If not, has she been told about menstruation? Yes/No**." The physician certifies immunisation against **poliomyelitis, tetanus, pertussis, diphtheria and smallpox**, records allergies to penicillin, horse serum, other drugs, foods, animals and bee sting, and states "any physical or **emotional** abnormalities." The parent grants advance permission "in case of surgical emergency… to the physician selected by the Camp Director, to hospitalize, secure proper treatment for and to order injections, anaesthesia or surgery." A foot section is headed **"FOR CAMP USE ONLY"** with columns for date admitted, discharged, diagnosis, treatment and "admissions as bed patients." That is the shape of the record a Canadian camp kept on each child in 1980.

**Accredited by the province, a member of the nation.** The association stated the distinction plainly in October 1980, printing cut-out logos for camps to paste into their own brochures with a warning beside them: "**Your camp can only be accredited by your Provincial Camping Association and not by the C.C.A. Therefore your camp is a member camp of the C.C.A. and not an accredited member.**"^8al That is the precise form of words this article and its neighbours should use about Kanawana: **accredited by the Quebec association, and a member camp of the Canadian one** — the accreditation being provincial and the membership flowing automatically from it. The same page shows how the national logo actually reached camp stationery: as a sheet of three sizes to be cut out and pasted up. The association had to say it twice: in May 1981 a memo repeated the rule and added that "**it has come to our attention that some camps are still using the wording 'accredited by C.C.A.' on their literature. This is incorrect and should be changed.**"^8ao Any Kanawana printed material of this period claiming accreditation by the national body is therefore using wording the association itself was trying to stamp out, and should not be read as evidence of a second accreditation.

**The Quebec association set age and experience requirements for camp directors.** A December 1980 advertisement for an executive director at **Camp Amy Molson**, a Montreal charity running a residential camp for five- to eight-year-olds, lists among its requirements "formal training in a child related profession; demonstrated competence in working with young children and adolescents; **working knowledge of French and fluency in English; meet age and experience requirements of A.C.Q.**"^8am So by 1980 the Association des Camps du Québec set minimum age and experience standards **for the director's post itself**, not only for camp facilities and programme — a requirement a Montreal camp advertised as a condition of employment. That is one more component of the accreditation regime Kanawana operated under, and one more thing the province's standards documents (`p_314`) would specify.

**Nobody had to be qualified to direct a camp, and the association's own editors called that a scandal.** The Winter 1982 editorial, printed under a policy note distinguishing the editorial committee's views from the association's, is the plainest statement in the run of what a camp director was and was not required to be: "Doctors are licensed to practise their profession. Teachers are certified before entering a classroom. Nurses are registered before being allowed to care for patients. **Yet, just about anyone is allowed to direct a camp and care for other people's children. This is not right! There are no national standards as to the professional qualifications of a camp director**; some provinces have age, education or experience requirements but does this ensure that camp directors are capable and qualified…? Certainly not."^8ar The scheme proposed was a specified curriculum — needs of children, needs of staff, hiring and development of staff, health and safety, camp administration and people management, camp philosophy, food services — **plus an internship in an organized camp**; and the argument for acting was the threat of being regulated from outside: "**this situation opens up the very real possibility — in fairness to the public — of the imposition of regulations on us by the government. The responsibility rests with us — the time is now!**"

That is the direct context for reading any Kanawana director's credentials in this period. The provincial requirements described elsewhere in this article — the ACQ's own age and experience rules — were the only bar that existed; **there was no national qualification, no register and no disciplinary mechanism**, and the association's editors were saying so in print in 1982. The same issue also carries the counter-argument, from a teacher writing on wilderness leadership: "**certification courses simply legalize errors in judgment**."^8ar

**The membership answered, and the answer explains why it never happened.** Two letters printed the following spring set out the profession's own reasoning.^8as **Ron Johnstone** of Camp Tawingo argued against on two grounds. The first was that a national scheme would misread the country: "**We in Ontario seem to have a tendency to think that all camping in Canada is the same… in reality it isn't! There are whole degrees of difference in sophistication in camping in Canada.** I have no difficulty at all in seeing the Camping Associations as educational and inspirational organizations but I have some real trouble when we start into the business of **regulating the industry** and that is what a program of National Standards or Certification will do." The second was that the public-protection argument was hollow because the business was not profitable enough to attract bad actors: "**all that wheeler-dealers have to do is look at the balance sheets of most Camps in Canada and they couldn't run fast enough to stay out of the business.**" He conceded the competence problem in a phrase worth keeping — "**I know some of our numbers who claim ten years experience in camping but in reality have had one year ten times**" — but concluded that education, not certification, was the movement's instrument: "now is not the time and I do not believe that we will ever see that time being ripe!"

**Lorne Bowering**, President of the British Columbia association, was qualifiedly in favour but doubted the machinery: "**The American Camping Association Certification system seems cumbersome to me — too many levels and a rat's nest to administer.** I have always thought **certification of Directors is at least as important as the Facilities/Program standards approach**… the problem is usually the 'How' to do, rather than the 'Why'. **Camping people are diverse and what may be a high standard for one may be a very low standard for another.**"

So the **ACA had a director-certification system in 1982 and the CCA did not**, and the reason was a settled position rather than an oversight: the Canadian associations understood themselves as educational and inspirational bodies, not regulators of an industry. For this project that is the answer to what Kanawana's directors were required to hold. Provincial accreditation covered the camp; the ACQ's rules covered the director's age and experience; **nothing national covered the director at all.**

**Having declined to certify, the association set about defining the body of knowledge instead.** Its Education Committee, chaired by Dorothy Walter with Janet Adamson, John Wilkinson and Stan Talesnick — and observed by Carolea Butters as chairman of the Ontario Task Force on Professional Development — was mandated to "examine, make recommendations for and publicize a variety of educational opportunities for the professional development of camp directors in Canada", including possibly "the preparation of a **self-directed learning curriculum for use anywhere in Canada**".^8at Eight research steps were designed, three completed by the spring of 1982, "and **all of this research is to come from within the Canadian Camping community**"; the results were to be presented at a **Pre-Congress Educational Workshop in January 1983** to determine "the **basic core curriculum required for professional development of camp directors**". Ontario had put the same question to its own Directors' Weekend in the autumn of 1981, led by Nelson Wieters, and reported that "**no decisions were reached**".^8at Whether the national curriculum was ever completed is not known to this project, and the workshop papers would be in the CCA fonds at Trent.

**What a camp staff manual contained — the specification, 1981.** In February 1981 the association printed a full definition of the document it had been recommending for years, and the contents list is the most useful single page in the run for anyone looking for a camp's own account of itself.^8an A staff manual is "a booklet prepared by the camp for the purpose of informing its future staff about the camp and their role in it", and "most staff manuals contain" seven parts.

Part II, **Facts About Camp**, is the part that matters here. Under *Locale*: a directional map, "a sketch of site showing trails, program areas, buildings", a description of the setting's special features and "**special problems which site gives rise to**". Under *Traditions, Philosophy*: statements interpreting them, a statement relating the camp's objectives to the camping movement, "**a brief historical sketch of the camp**", and "**explanation of role that camping plays in the total program of the sponsoring organization, where such exists**" — which for Kanawana means the YMCA of Montreal. Then *Procedures and Regulations* (including "**for inter-camp relationships (e.g. canoe tripping)**"), *Organizational Structure*, and *Personnel Policies* — "sickness allowance; **employment and dismissal procedures**; salary scales and payment procedures; travel to and from camp, charges for laundry."

Part IV is **Sample Forms Used At Camp**: "**test sheets (swimming, canoeing, camp crafts, etc.); tripping reports, what to take, hints for safety; parent reports, reports to section heads, etc.; counsellor evaluation**." Part V is **Job Analyses** for programme director, section head, specialist, activity counsellor, cabin counsellor and the CIT programme. And the production advice is specific enough to recognise an object by: "**bind in a stiff loose-leaf cover**… use different coloured pages for different sections (compulsory material, reference material, basis for pre-camp training)", closing with "**the tone of the Manual sets the tone of the future counsellor**."

**A Kanawana staff manual, if one survives, would therefore contain the camp's own historical sketch, a site map with its problems named, its dismissal procedures, its salary scales, its blank forms and its job descriptions, in a loose-leaf binder with coloured section dividers.** That is a specific object to ask for, and this project already holds one document of the family — the "Kamp Kanawana History" presented at a staff training course in 1951 (see [[traditions/cit-lit-program|The CIT/LIT Program]]), which is precisely the "brief historical sketch" this specification prescribes.

**A Montreal professor writes the movement's textbook, 1981.** The first comprehensive Canadian text on camp administration was published in Toronto in 1981 by **Robert E. Wilkinson, Ph.D., Professor in the Department of Physical Education at McGill University** — described by his own publisher as a "**Member of Canadian Camping Association through the Association des Camps du Québec**", and by his reviewer as being of **Camp Nominingue**, F. M. Van Wagner's camp.^8ao *Camps, Their Planning and Management* runs to 291 pages, was sold through the CCA's own publications service at $19.95, and "**includes national code of ethics for Canadian Camping Association**".

Eanswythe Flynn's review places it: "**Not since Dimock and Hendry's *Camping and Character* (1929) has a Canadian textbook of this size and depth been so all embracing**" — which puts a McGill physical education professor's 1981 book in a line that runs back to the study conducted at Taylor Statten's camp (see [[people/hedley-dimock|Hedley Dimock]]). What matters for this project is what she says it contains: "scores of charts, sketches, plans and **specimens of every kind of business form any camp records would require**", with fifty-two sketches and charts drawn by Mrs Wilkinson.

One caution about the edition, which matters to anyone trying to obtain it. The May 1981 review and the June 1981 publication notice both give the book as C.V. Mosby, Toronto, 1981; but two months earlier, in April 1981, a contributor's reference list cited "**Wilkinson, Robert E., *Camps, Their Planning and Management*, C.V. Mosby Co., St. Louis, Missouri, 1978**", with footnotes to specific pages.^8ap Either an earlier American edition exists or the contributor cited from proof. **The book should be asked for under both dates**: a 1978 edition's specimen business forms would date from the decade in which Kanawana's own surviving record is thinnest, rather than from after it. (A second, smaller trap in the same notice: it says the book was "reviewed in April *Canadian Camping*". The review is in the May issue.)

**Wilkinson is not the only Quebec name on the association's own list.** The CCA's 1981 publications catalogue marks Canadian titles with an asterisk, and there are only about fifteen of them among more than a hundred entries — the association's reading was overwhelmingly American, and it was the sole Canadian distributor for the American Camping Association. Three of that small Canadian list are Quebec-authored: Wilkinson's textbook; **Derek Walsh's *How to Promote Your Camp* / *Comment promouvir votre camp vacances*, CCA/ACC, 1981**, listed under his name in both the English and French series pages (see [[people/derek-walsh|Derek Walsh]]); and "***Outdoor Education — Guidelines for Camps* — W. Mingie, 17p, CCA, revised 1981**", by **Walter Mingie**, recorded in this magazine twenty years earlier as director of canoe trips and the intermediate section at **Camp Nominingue**.^8aq So the national body was drawing a disproportionate share of its own published writing from the province Kanawana was accredited in, at exactly the period when Quebec's English-language section had disappeared from its printed list of provincial associations.

**A book with specimens of every camp business form, written by a member of Kanawana's own provincial association and published in the year the camp's record is thinnest, is a direct aid to reading whatever Kanawana papers survive** — and a second route, after the Ontario forms kit of 1980, to knowing what the blanks looked like. It is also, incidentally, the fourth McGill connection this article records, after Arthur S. Lamb, the McGill camping school of 1937-40, and Blackstock's own B.Sc.

**Abbé Raoul Cloutier, past president.** The Quebec association's 1975 officer list names its past presidents as **M. Lionel Perras, M. Tony Shorgan, Mme N.D. Demetelin, Mme Alta R. Kahn** and **H. Capt. Abbé Raoul Cloutier**, who is also listed as an Honorary Member.^8v Cloutier is the co-founder of Camp-École Trois-Saumons and the man credited with bringing Georges Hébert's *méthode naturelle* to Quebec; the retained military rank of Honorary Captain matches the account of him as a serving officer in France during the war. The founder of hébertisme in Quebec had presided over the province's camping association, and was still an honorary member of the body that accredited Kanawana.

**Who founded the Quebec Camping Association, and who wrote its first standards.** The QCA's 1937 founding has been recorded here from archival holdings without a name attached to it. The name is **F.M. Van Wagner**, and the fullest account of him is the tribute the English Section's executive secretary wrote when the CCA made him an honorary member in 1975:^8x

> "As early as **1937**, as promoter and charter member of the C.C.A., Mr. 'Van' **organized the Quebec Camping Association, English Section**, to improve the camping methods and to raise the standard of the camps throughout the Province of Quebec. **He was also the first President of the Q.C.A.** … **In 1955 he formulated and first introduced a Manual of Standards for children's summer camps in the Province of Quebec.** He was **President of the Canadian Camping Association with Office in Montreal from 1958 to 1961.**"^8x

Three of those facts are new here and one of them matters a great deal to Kanawana. **Quebec camp standards begin with a named document by a named author in 1955** — a *Manual of Standards for children's summer camps in the Province of Quebec*. The sequence is now continuous: the manual in 1955; accreditation practice "in the pioneer stage" under Alta Kahn in 1959-60; provincial incorporation on 23 January 1961; and by 1975 every Quebec camp re-accredited annually.^8u ^8v ^8x Kanawana, as an accredited Quebec camp, was measured against a document whose origin is now datable, and finding that manual is a distinct and tractable search.

The rest of the tribute: born in **Hyde Park, New York**, a farm childhood and a career in physical education; **founded Camp Nominingue in 1925** on Petit Lac Nominingue, which "celebrated its 50th season" in 1974; the **Centennial Medal in 1967**; an **Award of Merit from the QCA's joint Provincial Board in 1968**; **eighty years old and still ski-touring in 1975**, having "probably travelled more ski-miles in the Laurentians than anyone" except Jackrabbit Johannsen.^8x He is the "F. M. Van Wagner, Montreal" unasterisked in the CCA's 1972 past-presidents list, and an honorary member of the QCA English Section in 1975.^8v Whether he was related to **Peter Van Wagner**, who was president of that section in 1972 and chaired its Standards Committee in 1975, is not stated in any source seen here and is not assumed.

**The CCA's national office was in Montreal from 1958 to 1961**, for the length of Van Wagner's presidency.^8x And the association's **annual meeting of October 1974 was held in Montreal** — stated twice in the same issue, in the tributes to Van Wagner and to Charles Plewman.^8x Both are periods and events for which minutes would name Quebec camps and camp people.

Camp Kanawana's relationship to the formal camping organizations is documented in several archival sources. The Ontario Camping Association fonds at Trent University (72-007) holds a "Kanawana (1940)" file in its Camp Brochures and Photographs series, confirming Kanawana had a presence in the OCA network by the early 1940s.^8h In the OCA's Agency Liaison series (fonds 78-006), Kanawana is listed among "Y" camps alongside Wanakita, Wa-sa-ah-bun, Tapatoo, Tapawingo, Pinecrest, and Camp Stephens.^8i The CCA fonds at Trent associates "Kamp Kanawana" with Derek Walsh and the YMCA in Montreal.^8j Concordia Archives sub-series P0145/12A holds "History of Camping" notes dated 1936, the same year the CCA was founded.^8k The same sub-series (P0145/12N03) contains records of the Canadian Camping Association founding (1936–1937), the QCA constitution (1937, 1942), the McGill camping school (1937–1940), and lists of camps, officials, and counsellors (1939–1948).^8l

**The 1982 statistics, fully labelled at last — and every Quebec member camp was accredited.** The 1982 Annual Report, bound into the Summer 1983 issue, prints "CCA/ACC STATISTICS 1982 - SUMMER" broken down by province with every heading legible, which is the first time in this run that the association's provincial tables can be read without guesswork. Quebec returned **129 member camps, of which 129 were accredited and none were not** — the only province in the table where those two numbers are identical (nationally, 500 of 618 were accredited). It reported 117 resident camps and 10 day camps, **15,791 camper-beds**, **52,977 individual campers**, **820,812 camper-days**, **4,618 paid staff against 790 volunteers**, a wage bill of **$5.371 million** and a food bill of **$3.208 million**.^8ay

Three things follow, and all three bear directly on how this wiki should describe Kanawana. **First, in Quebec there was no such thing as an unaccredited member camp**, which confirms in the association's own arithmetic what Marjorie Booth had said in interview about the Ontario and Quebec entry gate: "member of the ACQ" and "accredited by the ACQ" name one status, not two, so Kanawana's membership and its accreditation are the same fact. **Second, the table prints its own response rate** — a "% Return/Province" row giving **Quebec 48%**, against Alberta 75%, Saskatchewan 68%, Nova Scotia 56%, Manitoba 54%, Ontario 51.5%, British Columbia 49%, Newfoundland 24% and New Brunswick 16%. Quebec's figures therefore rest on returns from about half its member camps and are extrapolated from there, which is the caution Rosaire Corbin raised in October 1981 now quantified province by province and printed beside the numbers. **Third, Quebec camping ran on wages.** Nearly six paid staff for every volunteer, where Alberta reported 1,232 paid against 5,000 volunteers and British Columbia 726 against 2,884 — about **$101 of wages and $61 of food per individual camper**. The long-session, higher-fee, non-denominational character Booth ascribed to Quebec and Ontario is visible here as a labour fact.^8ay

**Two provinces confirm the membership figure from the ground.** Booth's estimate that the association spoke for "probably 10 to 12" percent of Canadian camps is corroborated, independently and candidly, in two provincial reports in the same document. Nova Scotia: staff "visited all the camps to carry the message of the Association… Interestingly enough, and maybe not so, but **some of the Directors operating camps had never heard of the Association!**" Saskatchewan: "**We speak for about twenty camps but there are probably more than one hundred other camps out there** who may or may not know we exist to speak for them." Every CCA statistic used in this wiki describes a member sample of roughly a tenth of the field, sampled at about half again.^8ay

**The Quebec accreditation programme Kanawana was subject to was rebuilt in 1982-83.** The ACQ's annual report, again signed **Claire Menard**, states that "le programme d'accréditation de l'Association des camps du Québec a fait l'objet d'un **renouvellement important**. Ce programme est orienté vers une **auto-évaluation des membres**, le respect des **normes universelles** et du **code d'éthique**." So an accreditation file from before that change and one from after are different documents, assessed on different terms. The same report records that **an ACQ market study of camp clientele was carried out that year** and showed a high rate of client satisfaction; that **a *guide d'évaluation des services* was in preparation**; that **la Semaine des camps had by then been running "depuis plus de cinq ans"** — i.e. from about 1977 — through exhibition halls, shopping centres, radio and television and newspaper and magazine articles; and that a colloquium for directors, senior staff and counsellors was set for **13-15 May 1983**. Two named Quebec documents about camp customers and camp service quality, and a five-year run of Quebec press coverage of camping with Kanawana a member camp throughout, are all new leads.^8ay

**The National Standards Workshop concluded that the national body should not accredit camps at all.** Brian Law's report on the workshop of **25-26 January 1983** gives its two objectives as updating "the existing **comparative analysis of provincial standards** for organized camping" — delegates prepared their provincial comparisons in advance, so such an analysis including Quebec's standards exists on paper — and documenting the visitation process. Its conclusion: "**The Workshop has proposed that CCA/ACC should not accredit camps but should promote good camping practices through education of camp directors.** This, of course, has important implications for our future action plan and for provincial camping associations." The task force was extended eighteen months and another workshop set for 1985.^8ay Taken with the certification debate of 1982, the movement settled the same question twice in a year: **no national certification of directors and no national accreditation of camps**. Accreditation stayed provincial, which is why Kanawana's standing was always an ACQ matter and never a CCA one.

**A Quebec vice-president again, and National Camping Day happened.** The 1983-85 executive was elected by acclamation and took office on **6 May 1983**: President **Jocelyn Palm** (Ontario), Eastern Vice-President **George Matthews** (Nova Scotia), Central Vice-President **Louis Jean** (Quebec), Western Vice-President **Terry Burkhalter** (Manitoba). Louis Jean is a new name here and the first Quebec regional vice-president since Derek Walsh, succeeding Jane McCutcheon of Ontario. **He did not last the year, and the reason is on the record.** By the Winter 1984 issue the president reports that "**Louis Jean has joined the staff of the Association des Camps du Québec. As employees are not permitted to serve in Executive roles, Louis has resigned as Central Vice President**… A new vice president has not yet been named."^8ba So Quebec held the seat for under nine months and then vacated it; the association had a standing rule barring employees from executive office; and **Louis Jean becomes a paid officer of the ACQ from late 1983** — a name to look for on Quebec accreditation and membership paper from 1984 onward rather than a volunteer. (That page is printed upside down in the digitized scan and was machine-decoded; the passage is a reconstruction — **corroborated in clean type** by the president's report a quarter later, which says "**Ken [Willis] replaced Louis Jean who resigned after accepting a position with ACQ**," naming his successor as **Ken Willis** — who is **also of Quebec**, writing from Magog in 1985, and who appears earlier in this article as the man who invited the CCA to hold its 1980 annual meeting in the province.^8bg So the seat was vacant for a matter of months and then returned to a Quebecer; Quebec's representation on the national executive was interrupted, not lost.)^8bc **Pauline Rondeau** was confirmed as chairman of the French Language Committee in **March 1983** after the post had been "vacant for several months this year," McCutcheon covering it in the interim; her report records that the Congress provided simultaneous interpretation of all keynote speakers and **three French sessions**, against the eight that had been planned. On the day itself, the results page records that **"Quebec coordinated their annual 'Semaine des camps' or camp week to coincide with April 14th and featured a media blitz"** — so Quebec press coverage of camping in the week of 14 April 1983 is findable — alongside a Senate greeting from **Ray Perrault**, a *Calgary Herald* feature, full-page coverage in the *Kitchener-Waterloo Record*, mailings to **over 600 weekly papers**, a Camp Week proclamation by New Brunswick Premier **Richard Hatfield** and a Camp Counsellor Acknowledgement Day declared in Nova Scotia. The next was set for **21 March 1984**.^8ay

**Who ran the national office, and how the magazine reached the camps.** Jack Pearse's tribute to **Mary Roy** on her departure in 1983 is the only account in this run of the CCA's own office, and it supplies several things this project had been reconstructing piecemeal. She joined "at the tail-end of John Latimer's presidency in the spring of 1975" and served his successors **Ron Johnstone, Jack Pearse, Cliff Labbett and Jocelyn Palm** — a presidential sequence stated in one place, unbroken from 1975 to 1983. Under **Fred Okada's** publications committee she assembled the CCA book catalogues, and she "negotiated a super arrangement with the **American Camping Association** for the distribution of their books throughout Canada — not only to camping folk but to libraries, schools and university courses," which is why American titles dominate the Canadian catalogues and why an ACA book in a Canadian camp library of this period says nothing about that camp's American connections. With **Brian Blackstock** she "designed the format, layout and distributions of the new *Canadian Camping Newsletter*, **beginning in the fall of 1976**," and with **Cliff Labbett**, through **Ken Smith, Director of the Non-Residents Branch of the National Sport and Recreation Centre**, she "arranged for the **nationwide distribution of *Canadian Camping***." She prepared the federal grant submissions and reconciled the statements for every federally funded project, and she pressed provincial associations to exchange newsletters, board minutes and conference reports with the national office — so Quebec association paper of this period may survive in the national office's files, and thence at Trent.^8az

**The second National Camping Day, and why Kanawana was not asked twice.** Marnie Ellis fixed the 1984 event for **21 March, the first day of spring**, a date chosen at the Pre-Congress Workshop on Promotion on Dennis McLelland's argument that it is "one that everyone knows but no other group «has» as their official day," under the theme "**Don't Wait for Summer — Think Camping Now!**" The mechanics changed: the posters went to the **provincial offices in early November 1983** for distribution at pre-Christmas meetings or seasonal mailings, each with a letter to the Director and an inquiry form, and "each province should have a list of all its member camps ready to send out to those filling in the inquiry form." The 1983 national mailing had gone out through Kanawana's office in Montreal; the 1984 one was devolved to the provinces, which means the **ACQ held a ready list of its Quebec member camps in the winter of 1983-84** — a further form of the Quebec camp directory sought elsewhere in this project.^8az

**Quebec sent nobody to the association's national leadership workshop.** Twenty-one provincial and national leaders met at Bolton Camp and Conference Centre on **13-15 October 1983** for a Volunteer Executive Development Workshop, chaired by Terry Burkhalter and facilitated by Arnie Cohen, with Fitness Canada paying the delegates' airfares. The full delegate list is printed: Newfoundland, Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, Ontario, Manitoba, Saskatchewan, Alberta and British Columbia all sent people. **Quebec sent none** — six months after Louis Jean's election to the national executive and around the time he left it for the ACQ's staff.^8ba That absence is a fact; it should not be read, as it might be alone, as Quebec drifting out of national affairs, since the vice-presidency Louis Jean vacated went to another Quebecer.

**The movement wrote its own history at fifty, and endowed its own future in the same season.** In February 1984 the Ontario Camping Association published ***Blue Lake and Rocky Shore***, a 112-page illustrated history written by a committee of its executive members and launched at its annual conference at the Inn On The Park, Toronto, on **23-25 February 1984**; Natural Heritage of Toronto published it and the CCA Bookstore sold it at $8.95. The magazine reviewed it as the first book of its kind — "a book of this calibre, looking at the world of children's camping from the turn of the century, has never before been written" — and as the history of children's camping in Canada as such, not merely of one province's association. **It is the first published history of the movement Kanawana belonged to, appearing ten years before Kanawana's own centennial**, and it is the natural place to look for how the movement described its own origins. On the founding date its review is no help: it says only that the OCA was "founded in the early Thirties'."^8bb

In the same issue the president announced that the association had been awarded **$10,000 by the Neathern Trust**, for projects fostering "the acquisition of appropriate practices in simple outdoor living," "the appreciation and increased understanding of the natural world," and "the expansion of education to reflect the concern for conservation of our environment and energy resources," with **principal and interest to be expended after ten years** — so the fund's work falls across **1984-1994**, the decade running up to Kanawana's centennial. The grant was instigated by **Dr. Mary L. Northway**, whose own camp biography the notice supplies: camper and then staff member at **Glen Bernard Camp** near Sundridge, Ontario, from **1922 to 1939**, and in **1940** co-founder with **Flora Morrison** of **Windy Pine**, a tripping camp near Minden.^8bb

**Two Canadian training texts were in print for the 1984 season**, both by names this article already carries: ***The Camp Counsellor's Handbook*, second edition, by Brian Blackstock and John Latimer** — the magazine's editor of 1977-1981 and the chairman of the 1983 Congress — "totally revised," published March 1984 at $9.95, and covering programme, daily life, the needs of different age groups, and counselling physically or mentally disabled campers; and the CCA's own **CIT Manual and Instructor's Manual**, in eleven chapters of which the first is "Camp History & Philosophy."^8bb That a camp's own history was a formal element of Canadian counsellor-in-training curriculum by 1984 is worth knowing when asking what Kanawana's CITs were taught about their camp.

**And a national river-heritage system began without Quebec.** The **Canadian Heritage Rivers System** was announced on **18 January 1984**, its board meeting for the first time on 19-20 January with the federal government and **Newfoundland, New Brunswick, Ontario, Manitoba, Saskatchewan, the Yukon and the Northwest Territories** — Manitoba taking the chair. The first candidate rivers were the Athabasca and the South Nahanni, with the Bloodvein and the French to follow. **Quebec was not a participating government**, so the designation system that would come to protect Canadian canoe routes began without the province Kanawana's trips ran in.^8bb

**The Quebec association in 1983-84, in its own words.** The ACQ's report for the year, again signed **Claire Menard**, records that its activities were "ajustées aux besoins des membres" after **a questionnaire sent to member camps in the autumn of 1983** asking what they needed and what services they expected; thematic days on computerised management, new games, and the place of parents at camp; and two things new to this project. The first is a precisely dated promotional week: "**La Semaine des Camps du 23 au 28 avril sera la principale activité de promotion au printemps. Un projet spécial, en collaboration avec le Ministère du Loisir, de la Chasse et de la Pêche, nous permettra d'avoir un 'message radio' durant cette semaine**" — so **23-28 April 1984** carried a Quebec-wide camp promotion with government-funded radio. The second is regulatory: "**Au Québec, la Loi de la protection du consommateur a fait l'objet de plusieurs rencontres avec différents intervenants afin d'obtenir une exemption pour les camps de vacances**." **The ACQ spent the year seeking to have summer camps exempted from Quebec's Consumer Protection Act** — a question of how camp fees, contracts and refunds with parents were governed in law, applying directly to Kanawana.^8bc

**Quebec was 100% accredited a second year running.** The 1983 provincial table gives Quebec **117 member camps, all 117 accredited and none non-accredited**, after 129 of 129 in 1982 — which puts beyond a single year's accident the finding that in Quebec "member of the ACQ" and "accredited by the ACQ" named one status. Its other 1983 figures: 103 resident and 5 day camps plus 9 "other"; 16,568 camper-beds; 75,768 individual campers; 790,595 camper-days; 1,395 volunteer against 3,788 paid staff; $4.89M wages and $3.29M food; and a **return rate of 41%**, down from 48%. **Two cautions belong with those numbers.** Quebec's membership fell by twelve camps in the same year its response rate fell seven points, so year-on-year movement in this column mixes real change with sampling change. And **the printed 1983 table does not add up** — its national paid-staff total is roughly double the sum of its provincial columns, and Alberta's camper-days are given as 5,403 against neighbours in the hundreds of thousands — so the provincial figures are usable but that table's national totals are not.^8bc

**The French Language Committee dates from the autumn of 1974.** This article has traced the association's bilingualism from the 1971 name change without a date for the committee itself. Its incoming chairman supplies one: "**ce comité qui a été mis sur pied à l'automne 1974** a pour but d'assurer une présence active des camps francophones et de faire entendre le point de vue des camps de langue française au sein de l'Association." She also names an event previously unknown here — "**le congrès du printemps 1976 qui a réuni des directeurs de camps de vacances et personnes intéressées au phénomène des camps, venus de toutes les provinces, autour du thème de 'L'activité pédagogique du camp de vacances'**" — a national gathering of French-language camp directors from every province, after which many kept up the contacts they made. **Colette Pouliot-Marier** accepted the chairmanship on **22 April 1984**, succeeding **Pauline Rondeau, directrice du Camp Claret**, who resigned "due to severe time restraints"; her stated tasks were generating French articles for the magazine and establishing priorities and procedures for translation.^8bc

**Every member camp in Canada was to hold a celebration day in August 1985 — with a "historical roots" kit.** The International Youth Year Task Force, struck in March 1983 and meeting in Toronto on 16-17 April and 22-23 October 1983, settled three national projects for 1985: a **National Celebration Event**, "a special summer day in Aug. of 1985 where all campers in CCA help celebrate the international year of youth," for which "**program kits for the themes of fitness, environment, historical roots and peace will be developed**"; a **Youth Camp Director Institute** at Bolton Camp, **31 January to 3 February 1985**, at $145 all inclusive; and an entire issue of *Canadian Camping* devoted to youth.^8bc Since every ACQ member camp was an accredited member camp, **a "historical roots" programme kit was due to reach Kanawana in the summer of 1985**. What the camp did with it is not documented; the despatch is.

**Two smaller facts about Quebec, and where the magazine was made.** The national Consultant/Advisor programme sent speakers to seven provincial events in 1983-84 at a total cost of $2,474.45, and records two that did not happen: "**The Association des camps du Québec could not use their Consultant/Advisor due to insufficient registration for their Winter Conference.**" And **Marnie Ellis, the national publicity chairman who thanked Kanawana for the 1983 poster mailing, was based in Montreal** — the president lists her as "Marnie Ellis of Montreal," and the executive director describes Marjorie Booth helping "the chairman, in Montreal, coordinate with them and the provincial representatives," which is very likely why the national mailing went to a Montreal camp. Separately, the Newsletter Committee sets out the magazine's production chain: Jay Haddad editing, **Marc Côté** on layout and photographs, **Thérèse Wong** typesetting, and **Ken Smith at the National Sport and Recreation Centre** guiding it "through the printing and mailing process in Ottawa."^8bc

**Child abuse reaches a national camp directors' programme, January 1985.** The full four-day programme of the Youth Camp Director Institute at Bolton Camp, **31 January – 3 February 1985**, chaired by **John Latimer**, is printed in the autumn of 1984. Its sessions were "Values & Morals of Youth" (**K. Grant Kerr** of the United Church of Canada, then OCA President); "Stress & Addiction" (Norm Panzica, Council on Drug Abuse); "Volunteerism & Youth" (Marguerite Hudson, Girl Guides of Canada); "Responsibility, Loyalty & Commitment of Youth" and, on the Saturday afternoon, "**Child Abuse**" — both led by **Arnold Cohen of the Child Guidance Clinic of Winnipeg**; "'I've done it all' — Apathetic Youth" (Barry Lowes, Camp Timberlane); "The Family" (Alan Ingram, Litigation Counsel, Official Guardian Branch, Province of Ontario); "Marketing Your Camp" (Bruce Harris, Camp Kawabi); "Effective Leadership for Environment Programs" (Craig Copland, Camp Mini-Yo-We); and three sessions by Michael Brandwein of Chicago.^8bd

**This is the first appearance of child abuse as a named agenda item at a national gathering of Canadian camp directors anywhere in the digitized run of *Canadian Camping*, 1949-1988.** It is recorded here without extrapolation — nothing follows from it about any particular camp — because it establishes when the subject entered the profession's formal training programme, which is context a later reader of any camp's records from this period will need. The same programme shows **Ron Johnstone**, an Ontario figure throughout this article, now listed under the **Alberta** Camping Association.

### The money stops: why the national record thins after 1985

In the winter of 1985 the association's president published the reason its own paper trail — and this article's principal source — comes to an end. Fitness Canada had funded the CCA's national office since 1971, its full-time executive director since 1979, and the travel that let volunteers from every province attend board meetings. At the end of November 1984 its director, **Michel Bedard**, told the association that Fitness Canada **would no longer fund the executive director's post at all**, and that support for "all management aspects, re travel for meetings, office functions, administrative assistance, would be **reduced each year by 33-1/3% to terminate completely in 1988**."^8be

*Canadian Camping* ceased publication in 1988. The two facts belong together, and any account of the thinning of the national camping record after the mid-1980s should start here rather than treating it as an unexplained gap.

The same editorial is the association's own institutional autobiography and dates several things this article had only inferred. The **national office with paid staff and a permanent address dates from 1971**; before that "the CCA office resided in the home of the elected President," and the office was thereafter shared with the Ontario Camping Association. A **full-time executive director was funded from 1979**, a post **Marjorie Booth held until October 1984**. **National conferences were held in 1972, 1973, 1975 and 1978** before the 1983 International Congress. The **Canadian Recreational Canoeing Association "was an outgrowth of CCA with an initial board of CCA personnel."** And the board's 1982 decision to reduce its dependence on Ottawa produced "a hefty increase in CCA fees paid through the provincial associations" — so the national fees Kanawana paid through the ACQ rose in 1982 as a matter of national strategy. A consultant, **John Fisher**, was engaged in March 1985 to advise on restructuring, reporting first to the Regina board meeting and annual general meeting of **25-26 April 1985**.^8be

Palm's figures for the association at its high-water mark: **some 400,000 campers, about 11,000 of them from outside Canada, over 38,000 paid staff and over 16,000 volunteers, an estimated 80% of staff aged 13 to 24.**

**24 July 1985: the one day every member camp in Canada was asked to keep — and what arrived in the post.** The International Youth Year National Celebration Event was fixed for **Wednesday 24 July 1985**, with Ian Fowler of Moncton as national co-ordinator. The association undertook to "provide **each member camp with a kit of program material** suitable for the day's event," to be "**directly mailed to each camp in early May**," and the contents were credited by author: **"Historical Roots Activities — Dr. Bruno Morawetz, Camp Ponacka"**; "Environmental Activities — Mike Walters, Camp Chief Hector"; "Fitness Activities — Fitness Canada"; "Peace Activities — Canadian Peace Congress"; and an "IYY Campfire Program — Jack Pearse, Jane McCutcheon, John Jorgenson, Camp Tawingo." Camps were asked "at least [to] use the special IYY campfire program giving the CCA salute to youth one common denominator."^8bf

For this project the first of those is the significant one. **A named set of camp-history activities, written by the association's own archives chairman, was posted to every member camp in Canada in early May 1985** — Kanawana among them, since every ACQ member camp was an accredited member camp — for use on a fixed date eleven weeks later, in a period for which no Kanawana season report survives. It is a specific document with an author and a delivery window rather than a general exhortation, and a copy should be sought in the CCA fonds at Trent. The Quebec member of the task force that commissioned it was **Peter Goddard of Montreal**; that Kanawana's own director was therefore party to sending a history kit to his own camp follows from two documented facts and is stated here as an inference, not as a record.

The theme article that accompanied it, Bob Stewart's "Tracing Your Roots," is a genealogy primer for campers, and its advice on the limits of what can be done at camp is unexpectedly close to this project's own method: gather family stories, "but treat them with a bit of care. **Time has a way of adding to old family stories**… Sometimes they can be very interesting without being very true."^8bf

**Why the federal money stopped, from someone who was in the room.** Ken Willis, writing in the summer of 1985: "I was on the Board and Executive at the time discussions centred around what the Canadian Camping Magazine could do for the Association in its present financial crisis … **resultant upon Fitness Canada's thinking that camping is not within their jurisdiction**."^8bg The withdrawal recorded above was therefore a jurisdictional judgement about whether organized camping fell within a fitness mandate, not simply a share of general austerity — which is why the president's reply set out Fitness Canada's own three-part mandate and argued the association fitted inside it.

**A new president, and a shrinking membership.** **Janet Adamson** took office at the Regina annual meeting in **April 1985**, succeeding Jocelyn Palm. She counted **609 member camps** — against 616 in 1983 and 618 in 1982 — and reported John Fisher's preliminary restructuring report received at Regina with a final report due to the Executive in June. She also noted that a copy of the association's **1984 Annual Report** could be had from the office on request; that document, the last full one before the cuts took effect, has not been seen by this project.^8bg

**What it took to run an English camp for French-speaking children, set out by a Quebec director in 1985.** A Society of Camp Directors prize paper by **Madelene Ferguson Allen**, director of Camp Ouareau, was printed in both languages under the title "The Kid Who Doesn't Speak English… How Do We Help?" / "L'enfant qui ne parle pas anglais". Its opening judgement is blunt: "**In too many cases the most short changed children at our summer camps are these children who don't speak English. Camps are not in tune with this growing segment of our camper population.**"^8bg It is the fullest treatment of the question anywhere in the digitized run, it comes from the province where the question was sharpest, and it is set out in full in [[connections/related-camps/camp-ouareau|Camp Ouareau]]. It matters here because it establishes the standard of practice a Quebec English-language camp was being held to in 1985 — a translated pre-arrival letter and a fifty-word camp vocabulary, a deliberate refusal to seat the child beside a fluent speaker of his own language, a shared "core phrase" method across the whole staff, and the flat rule that "**a camp should not, under any circumstances, accept a child who does not speak English unless it is willing to become totally involved**." Kanawana's own practice in this period is not documented; this is what it should be read against.

**The magazine begins reprinting its own past.** From this issue a feature called "Camping… A Retrospective" reprints, each quarter, "a previously spoken or published work on some aspect of camping in Canada," so that readers might "examine to what degree the atmosphere, thrusts and initiatives of bygone days are relevant today." The first is **Barry Lowes's address to the Ontario Camping Association's annual banquet at the Inn-on-the-Park on 19 March 1966** — an attack on the movement's complacency ("we talk a better game than we play… the claims have been mostly shadows without substance"), a warning against letting the OCA's newly appointed first executive secretary, **Stan Wild**, carry work the volunteers should, and a rejection of the profit/non-profit distinction as "anathema… A camp is a camp, and a child is a child." Its most striking passage, written months before Canada's centennial year: "**Canada stands in danger of coming apart at the seams, after 100 years**… If the 3½ millions of dollars that have been poured down the drain on the Bilingual and Bicultural Commission had been spent instead to exchange young people, getting them into homes and camps across Canada, to live together for a time. **What a bridge of understanding such exchanges could form!**"^8bg That is very nearly the inter-cultural camping exchange the Secretary of State went on to fund, and which this project has traced through the 1967 Centenary Journey.

**A sitting president dates the association to about 1947 — in the same issue that dates it to 1936.** In the autumn of 1985 the magazine reprinted the presidential article **Douglas G. McEwen** had published in **Autumn 1967**, and one sentence in it cuts across everything the association said about itself in the 1980s: "**For the first 20 years of its life the Canadian Camping Association's strength has lain in strong provincial associations.**"^8bh Twenty years before 1967 is **1947**. That is not a recollection at eighty or a historian's inference; it is the sitting president describing the span of his own association's life, in its own magazine, and placing its beginning two decades back rather than three.

**The same issue carries the opposite dating twice.** Its president's message opens "In 1986 CCA/ACC will be celebrating fifty years of service to children and youth in Canada," and the publications catalogue bound into it repeats in both languages that "the Canadian Camping Association was founded in 1936."^8bh The association printed both datings in one issue without apparently noticing, which is the most useful thing about the find: people inside it were operating on both at once. Neither is preferred here. It is the strongest evidence yet for the two-stage reading offered above — a 1936 founding out of the Ontario association's circle, then constitution as a genuinely national body at Ottawa across 1946-49 — because a Manitoba-based president in 1967, with no Toronto institutional memory to defend, naturally dated the association from the second stage. See conflict `c_029`.

**The French section of the Quebec association was still outside the national body in 1967.** From the same article, listing the association's gaps: "**No Association exists in Saskatchewan**, some camps in the Maritimes are not members of the Association and **the French section of the Quebec Camping Association are still not members of the Canadian Association.**"^8bh This article has recorded the ACQ's founding in 1958 and its joining the QCA in 1961, with a Conseil Provincial coordinating the two sections from that year. What it has not recorded is that **joining the provincial body did not make the French section a member of the national one** — and that as late as 1967, thirty years after the QCA's founding and four years before the CCA took its bilingual name, the francophone half of Quebec camping stood formally outside the Canadian Camping Association. That changes the shape of the bilingualism story traced below from 1971: the name change and the 1974 French Language Committee were not a movement catching up with a French section it already had, but the machinery of bringing one in.

**What else the 1967 article fixes.** The association adopted its first national statement of practice that year — "**with the adoption this year of a set of Desirable Practices, camps now have a guide from their camping association**," explicitly a guide rather than standards. **The national executive sat in Manitoba, and had been in the western provinces for four years**, with the candid admission that "no response from Provincial Associations is taken as assent." There was **no full-time executive officer**; McEwen argued one should be employed, which did not happen until 1979. And at "the **Symposium of Recreation in Montreal**," twenty-five people concerned with leisure never reached the issues **Kirk Wipper** had raised, "because of an involved discussion that developed as to what was meant by the word, camping."^8bh

**"An Adventure into the Past", 1986 — every camp in Canada invited to stage its own history.** For the second consecutive summer the national association asked its member camps to put their own past into the programme, and this time it printed the invitation in both languages: "**CELEBRATE CCA's 50th BIRTHDAY WITH AN ADVENTURE INTO THE PAST — 1936-1986**… All across the country camps will participate by including one or all of the following activities in their programs: **historical journeys; re-enactment of early camping lifestyles; ceremonies and theme events.** Each participating camp will be recognized with **a wall certificate** and those camps pre-registering will receive **cards for each of their campers**," with registration forms distributed through the provincial associations.^8bi

Taken with the International Youth Year "historical roots" kit mailed to every camp in May 1985, **that is two successive national prompts, in 1985 and 1986, for camps to research and perform their own histories** — and the second leaves objects behind. A dated wall certificate is the kind of thing that hangs in a dining hall and survives in photographs; a camper's card is the kind of thing that survives in a drawer. Whether Kanawana registered is not documented. The notice is also one more statement of the 1936 dating, against the c.1947 dating its own past president used in 1967.

**The magazine changes hands.** The same issue announces that **Jay Haddad is retiring after "four and a half years" as volunteer editor** — placing his appointment in the middle of 1981 — and that "**the Newsletter under the Communications Committee will be the responsibility of a group from Camp Tawingo**," with this "transitional issue" to be followed by a "new look" in the spring.^8bi From 1986 the association's magazine is made by a camp rather than by an editor, which is why its last issues differ in character from everything before them.

**A French camp-management library, and a Belgium-Quebec comparison.** The new-additions list in the same issue is almost entirely French: an eight-part **"Série 'Un camp à faire'"** — five *Manuel de gestion* volumes on general administration, human resources, programmes, equipment and finance, plus *Évaluation du rendement*, *Les objectifs et le programme*, and *La planification des ressources humaines et sélection du personnel* — and, separately, ***Les colonies de vacances d'expression française en Belgique (communauté Wallonie-Bruxelles) et les camps de vacances au Québec***.^8bi Neither authorship nor publisher is given. The second title is the notable one: a study setting Quebec's camps beside those of francophone Belgium, sold nationally in 1986 and previously unknown to this project.

**The founder's daughter said 1938, and was talked out of it in print.** In the association's own fiftieth anniversary issue, the magazine interviewed **Adele Ebbs** — Taylor Statten's daughter, secretary to the founding circle, and the person who kept its minutes. Asked whether the received dates sounded right, she said: "**'36 sounds early to me**… '33 sounds right." Told that her father had helped F. M. Van Wagner form the Quebec Camping Association in 1937, she answered: "**Well that is why I thought the Canadian Camping Association was not until…** my impression was that it was **1938**." The interviewer replied, "We may be celebrating 50 years under false pretenses, so let's not upset that apple cart," and she conceded: "No, 50 years makes it '36. Well, I'll buy that."^8bj

**The best-placed living witness to the founding put it two years later than the association did, reasoning from the sequence of the Quebec association, and was overruled on the ground that the anniversary was already under way.** With Douglas McEwen's c.1947 dating of 1967, the founding-date question now has three answers, all from inside the association: **1936 in its 1980s literature, c.1947 from its president in 1967, and 1938 from the founder's daughter in 1986.** None is preferred here. A 1937 Quebec association followed by a 1938 national one is exactly the sequence a founding-circle secretary would remember, while 1936 is what a later corporate record of reorganisation would show — which is once more consistent with the two-stage reading. See conflict `c_029`.

**Before the association there was a supper club.** Ebbs describes what preceded it: "in the early 30's there were regular meetings, mostly social — **Edgar, Hamilton, Halliday, Chapman, Cochrane and Statten**, those six," meeting in each other's houses "4 or 5 times a year," where "**a great deal of it was social and anything they talked about was sort of trivial in a way because they had secrets. Nobody ever knew what anybody paid their staff.**" The six were Mary S. Edgar of Glen Bernard, Mary G. Hamilton of Camp Tanamakoon, Ferna Halliday of Camp Oconto, Ernie Chapman of Camp Kagawong, A. L. Cochrane of Camp Temagami, and Statten; **Ebbs became their secretary and "finally started to take notes sometime before I got married, and that was 1935"** — so minutes of the pre-founding meetings exist from about 1934-35. Charles Plewman and the Dansons joined soon after. Of Arnold Ward she remarks that the circle was of "private Camp Directors and maybe because Arnold Ward was an **agency** Camp Director" he was not part of it — the private/agency division at the association's very origin. She gives the early presidential sequence as **Taylor Statten, Dais Gass, Anne Vail, Ted Yard, Irv Haladner, F. M. Van Wagner**.^8bj

**The Quebec association also ran on two datings — and it celebrated the second.** In the summer of 1986 the Quebec column reported that its members would "**again celebrate the 25th anniversary of the Quebec Camping Association**" at their August meeting at Camp Trois-Saumons, explaining: "**Le regroupement des responsables des camps des communautés anglophone et francophone avait lieu notamment en janvier 1961**" — "the reorganization of the directors of the French and English speaking camping community took place primarily in January of 1961."^8bj **So the association dated itself from the January 1961 amalgamation rather than from the 1937 founding of the English Section**, which is why different sources answer the question of the QCA's founding differently: 1937 is the English Section, 1961 the bilingual association. It also fixes the amalgamation to January 1961 from the association's own mouth.

**How a Quebec camp was accredited in 1986.** From the same column: "**quatre consultants visiteront près de soixante (60) camps de vacances. Ce programme exige que les camps accrédités soient visités tous les deux ans.** Les camps de vacances qui ont connu des changements majeurs au cours de l'année (**le changement de directeur, par exemple**) sont également visités."^8bj Quebec had moved from the annual visitation praised in 1975 to a **two-year cycle** worked by **four consultants**, and — the point that bears on Kanawana — **a camp that changed director in a year was visited regardless of where it stood in the cycle.** Kanawana's directorship passed from Peter Goddard to Jay Netherwood inside the YMCA's FY1986, so on the ACQ's own stated rule Kanawana should have been visited in 1986 and a visitation report should exist. The column also records a new ACQ **parents' guide and directory** in members' hands that year.

**A directory of French-language camps outside Quebec.** The French Language Committee, chaired from 1985 by **Gérard Bachand of Rawdon, Quebec**, reports "**la publication d'un répertoire des camps francophones hors Québec**… rendue possible grâce à la collaboration du **Conseil de la Vie française en Amérique**. **Vingt-quatre camps figurent dans la première édition**."^8bj A 1985 directory of the twenty-four French-language camps outside Quebec then known — a document that would map francophone camping across Canada from the one body positioned to see it whole.

**Montreal and Toronto ran Canadian camping until the mid-1950s, and the magazine says so plainly.** Interviewing the widow of **W. E. (Ted) Yard**, CCA president 1953-55, in the autumn of 1986, the magazine stopped her account of the cross-Canada trip he made in office to insert a note of its own: he "visited and spoke to every Association going west from Ontario. We didn't go east. I don't know whether there were Associations at that time in the east. **(Ed's note: At that time, the Quebec Camping Association was the only Provincial Association east of Ontario)**." The interviewer then drew the consequence: "up until that time, **the President of the Canadian Camping Association was either from Quebec or from Ontario (and even more specifically from Montreal or Toronto)** and, shortly after you made your cross-Canada trip, it began that Presidents and Executives and Boards of the Canadian Camping Association were provincially-located."^8bk

This is the clearest statement in the run of the position Kanawana's own provincial association occupied at mid-century — **one of the two centres from which the national body was run.** **On when that ended, the run corrects itself.** The interviewer said "shortly after" the 1953-55 trip; **May Brown, CCA president 1965-67 and herself the second president from outside Ontario and Quebec, names the sequence**: "**Don Smith from Alberta was President just before I was and I think that was the first move out of Ontario and Quebec.** It really solidified our whole country, because, for the next 10 years, it moved from Alberta to B.C. to Manitoba, where **Doug McEwen** was President and, eventually, **David Hartry** in Nova Scotia became the President."^8bp A named sequence is more precise than "shortly after", so **the break is dated here to Don Smith of Alberta in about 1963-65 — a decade after the Yard trip, not immediately after it.** Brown supplies the mechanism too: "The CCA, at that stage, was very small and we had no money, so I took advantage of those trips [on the National Advisory Council for Fitness and Amateur Sports] to go all across the country, stopping off with my air ticket and going to camping meetings in Alberta and Winnipeg." It also supplies the far end of something this article has traced from the other direction: Quebec's drift out of national affairs through the 1980s is the late consequence of a national association that had, by design, stopped being run from Montreal and Toronto.

**Passing the hat, 1986.** Two years after Fitness Canada's withdrawal the association appealed for private money over the names of **Janet Adamson**, president, and **Jack Pearse**, chairman of a new CCA/ACC Patrons' Foundation: "**An additional income of $30,000.00, this year, represents the difference between a giant step forward and an irretrievable step backward**… We are sending this plea to approximately **100 people** — some current Camp Directors, some retired Camp Directors, some friends of camping." A first letter had gone out in April 1986; the autumn issue reprinted it for the whole readership with a blue pledge card tipped in, and Pearse's drive had by then raised "nearly $10,000 in five months." The long-term aim was a standing Patrons' Foundation "whose proceeds will perpetuate camping in Canada."^8bk The 50th Anniversary Patrons' Fund is what the funding cut of November 1984 looked like by its second year.

**Training pushed out to the provinces, Moncton, 1-5 October 1986.** The national workshop that preceded the **first regional conference held in Eastern Canada** worked through **Dorothy Walter's Curriculum for Camp Directors** — then going to print in English and French with Fitness Canada support — and built "three sample prototype workshops which could be used by Provincial Camping Associations to educate their Camp Directors." **Bob Latimer of the National Training Institute** led the first two days; the delegates spent a third on volunteer board development and a fourth studying **Michael Brandwein's** conference sessions as a model of delivery. The workshop closed with a device worth recording: "**A contract was produced which the delegates were asked to sign along with the President of CCA**," binding each delegate to carry the curriculum home by a named date and the president to follow up by another. A proposal was taken up "to ask the Executive of CCA to produce **a certificate to acknowledge a Camp Director's completion of, or attendance at, training**" — the director-certification question this article has followed since 1982, returning as a certificate of attendance rather than a licence to practise.^8bk

**What the ACQ did with the summer of 1986.** Its own bilingual report: Employment and Immigration Canada funded **a three-person team that spent the summer reclassifying all of the association's archives and camp dossiers** — the summer, that is, that determined the present arrangement of the ACQ's own records, and a thing worth knowing before asking what it holds on Kanawana. **Three accreditation consultants — Leo Venditti, Yves Legault and Louis Jean — visited 76 camps**, four of them new members. **Jean-Marc Amyot** of the Régie des loteries du Québec's audio-visual service shot **400 slides** at Quebec camps for a bilingual slide show. A pamphlet produced with the ministry promoting outdoor-education classes was printed in **50,000 French and 8,000 English copies** and mailed in September to every school principal and pedagogical consultant in the province, with a second on "Employment in a Summer Camp" planned.^8bk

**An inventory of French-language camping, second edition.** The Commission des Camps d'Expression française reported a new member, **M. Michel Gratton of Hearst, Ontario** — "we thus have two 'out-of-Quebec' members which should help promote the reality of French in the camping world" — and the launch of the **second edition of the Répertoire de l'Inventaire des Camps d'Expression française (INCEF)**, with **M. Jean-Luc Côté of the Conseil de la Vie française en Amérique** gathering the data. The next task it set itself was to "**élaborer notre politique linguistique pour l'Association des Camps du Canada**."^8bk Read against the twenty-four-camp directory of 1985 recorded above, the association's francophone wing was by 1986 doing the census work no one else in Canadian camping was doing.

**The board in the autumn of 1986** confirms the Quebec presence this article has been tracking: **Ken Willis of Magog** as vice-president (central), **F. M. Van Wagner of Pointe-Claire** still listed as President Emeritus fifty years on, **Colette Pouliot-Marier** as Quebec's provincial representative, and **Gérard Bachand of Rawdon** chairing the French Language committee. The **Second International Camping Congress** is advertised in clean type for **3-5 March 1987 at the Shoreham Hotel, Washington**, jointly sponsored by the American, Canadian, Jamaican and Japanese associations; the Canadians named as contributors are Dorothy Walter, Jack Pearse, Fred Okada, Jocelyn Palm and Rob Heming, and **no Quebec name appears among them**.^8bk


**A fourth date for the birth of Quebec camping — from the Quebec seat on the national board.** In the winter of 1987 **Ken Willis**, writing as CCA/ACC Vice-President (Quebec Region), opened his column with a flat statement and a plan: "**The organized camping movement in Quebec was born in 1938.** Tony Shorgun, who has been involved in camping during many of the intervening years, is already at work laying plans for the **50th Anniversary of this birth** which, obviously, will be held next year."^8bl

This article already carries three datings for Kanawana's own provincial association: **1937** from the archival holdings, corroborated by Alta Kahn's "coming-of-age party" of 1958; the founding of the **French section in 1958**; and **January 1961**, the amalgamation the ACQ itself commemorated as its twenty-fifth anniversary in 1986. Willis adds **1938**, and not in passing — a fiftieth anniversary was being organised around it for 1988, which means the year was the premise of an event rather than a slip in a column.

**It is also the year Adele Ebbs gave for the national association, and for the opposite reason.** Ebbs dated the CCA to 1938 *because* she remembered her father helping F. M. Van Wagner form the Quebec association in 1937. Willis dates Quebec camping itself to 1938. The two recollections cannot both stand as offered, and what they establish between them is that **the 1937/1938 boundary was genuinely unclear to the people who were present at it** — which is a finding about the record, not a reason to prefer either. See conflict `c_029`. If the 1988 anniversary took place it will have produced a programme or commemorative publication compiled by Shorgun, a name new to this project; that document would be a participant's history of Quebec camping, and Kanawana would be in it.

**Quebec gets its own vice-presidency, 1987.** The board that had run three regional vice-presidencies — Eastern, Western and Central — now runs **four**: Eastern (Ian Fowler), Western (Jan James), **Quebec (Ken Willis)** and **Ontario (Sally Moore**, newly appointed and welcomed in the president's message).^8bl Quebec is no longer half of a "central" region shared with Ontario but a named seat in its own right. Against the drift this article has traced from 1983 — Quebec absent from the Bolton programme, its national seat interrupted for months in 1985, no Quebec name on the Washington congress faculty in 1987 — this is the opposite motion, and belongs in the record alongside it.

**Where the ACQ stood at the start of 1987.** From the same column: "**The A.C.Q. has, over the past years, received very significant funding from the Provincial Government. The degree of this funding has been reduced somewhat over the past couple of years and the A.C.Q. now expects there will be significant cut backs due to the financial condition of the Government.**" The association was shifting to self-financing, and Willis warned members that they would have to give much more of their time "or they will have to dig further into their pockets." Membership was "holding well at **about 110**" — against 117 accredited in 1983 and 129 in 1982 — "with a few being dropped each year because they do not meet standards and a few new ones being added," the board "each year agonizing over a few camps which seem careless about the paperwork."^8bl So the provincial body Kanawana belonged to was losing government money on the same curve as the national one, and shedding a handful of member camps a year to the standards it enforced.

**A national conference in February 1963, and a caution about the conference list.** The cover of the same issue is captioned as taken "at a Canadian Camping Association Conference at **Banff, Alberta in February, 1963**" — supplying the month for the 1963 annual meeting recorded above as the first occasion the affiliation formula was raised.^8bl It is also a reminder that Jocelyn Palm's 1985 list of national conferences ("1972, 1973, 1975 and 1978" before the 1983 Congress) is not the complete series: Banff 1963 precedes it, and the Mary S. Edgar profile in the same issue mentions a **CCA Conference at Geneva Park in 1973** at which Edgar, aged 83, gave the keynote. Palm's list should be read as the post-reorganisation conferences.

**The founding six were the Ontario association's, and a second witness says so.** Interviewed about Mary S. Edgar, **John Gilchrist** listed the circle unprompted — "**Taylor Statten, Ferna Halliday, A. L. Cochrane, Mary Hamilton, Ernie Chapman**… and **Mary S. Edgar**. They got together and decided they wanted to form a Camping Association as a vehicle where they could exchange ideas and learn things from one another" — and the interviewer identified it: "**That was the Ontario Camping Association. Taylor Statten was the first President, in 1933.**"^8bl That is the same six Adele Ebbs named in 1986, from an independent witness, and it attaches them to the **provincial** founding. The supper club of private directors this article recorded from Ebbs is therefore the OCA's founding circle; whatever happened nationally in 1936, 1938 or 1947 was a separate act, which removes one recurring source of confusion from `c_029`.

**The immigration protocol, 1986-87.** A new requirement of "**a Medical Examination for all foreign camp counsellors**" disrupted the working protocol during 1986; the committee chairman **Barry Lowes** met **Jake Epp**, Minister of Health and Welfare, in early May to have it amended, and a further meeting in Ottawa in November simplified it. The examination stayed. Lowes's summary of the position is worth preserving as the framework any Canadian camp's foreign hiring ran through: "Our aim must continue to be to hire Canadians first. Our practices are monitored by computers at the point of entry into Canada… **Hiring foreign camp counsellors is a privilege granted, not a right of Canadian camps.**"^8bl

**Provincial archives, not only Trent.** Bruno Morawetz's archives report records that the provincial associations had been asked to send newsletters, minutes and notices to Trent so that they "would constitute a permanent record of camping in Canada during 1986," and that the archivist **Bernadine Dodge** had received "several of the brown envelopes commonly known as the '**Brown Bag**'." He adds that "**some Provincial Associations have their own archives** where they collect printed material about their member camps," and hopes all will establish archives committees.^8bl With the ACQ's own summer-1986 archive reclassification (recorded above), that is two independent reasons to ask the Quebec association what it holds rather than only Trent.


**What the Patrons' Fund actually raised, and where the money came from.** The appeal for $30,000 recorded above was accounted for in the spring of 1987: "**To date, the donations total $11,918.11**" — about forty per cent of the target — from a list of fifty-five individuals and eighteen camps, printed in full.^8bm

**Every one of the eighteen camps is Ontarian.** Canadian Adventure, Gay Venture, Haliburton Hockey Haven, Kawabi, New Moon, Oconto, Robin Hood, Tanamakoon, Tawingo, the Taylor Statten Camps, Trailfinder, Wahanowin, White Pine and YMCA Wanakita. **No Quebec camp gave, and no camp from any other province.** Quebec appears only among the individual donors — **Gérard Bachand** of Rawdon, **Maurice Blaquière**, **Jacqueline Lavoie**, **Dr. Ken Willis** of Magog, **Peter and F. M. Van Wagner** of Pointe-Claire, and **Eileen Mayotte**, the Quebec association's secretary of the 1940s, here alive and giving money in 1987, which is the latest fixed point this project holds for her.^8bm That distribution is the finding: at the moment the national body needed money to survive the federal withdrawal, the institutional giving came from the private camps of one province, and Quebec's contribution was personal rather than corporate.

**The association acquired a language policy in 1987 — to comply, not on its own initiative.** Gérard Bachand's committee reported that it had "**élaboré une politique linguistique pour l'ACC/CCA afin de nous rendre conformes aux lois du bilinguisme des organismes nationaux**," with a translation policy attached, for board approval by spring.^8bm **That is thirteen years after the French Language Committee was founded in the autumn of 1974 and sixteen after the association took its bilingual name in 1971** — and the stated reason is federal bilingualism requirements on national organisations. It is a useful corrective to the article's own account above: the machinery of bringing the French section in was built slowly, and the formal policy came last and under external pressure.

**INCEF '87.** The same note records that "**dès la fin janvier nous publiions la deuxième édition du répertoire des camps d'expression française, hors Québec (Incef '87)**," produced with the **Conseil de la Vie française en Amérique** and the **Ministre des Affaires Intergouvernementales**, with copies held at the CCA office in Toronto and a needs survey of the listed camps under way.^8bm The first edition of 1985 carried twenty-four camps; the second exists, is dated, is jointly funded by a French-Canadian cultural body and a Quebec ministry, and was findable in Toronto.

**A letter to the provinces, February 1987 — including an instruction to record oral history.** Bruno Morawetz reported that "in February of this year a letter was sent to **all Provincial Archives Chairmen, or to the Provincial President** concerning the collection of material for C.C.A./A.C.C. Archives at Trent University," and urged member camps "to collect as much written material as possible and **have it lodged in your own provincial archives**." He then added the instruction that matters most here: "**One of the ways of collecting archival material is to interview persons who are playing important roles in camping — these too can be lodged and catalogued in an archival collection.**"^8bm With the ACQ's own archive reclassification of summer 1986 and Morawetz's note that some provincial associations already held their own archives, that is a third documented prompt inside eighteen months for Quebec camping records — and Quebec camping voices — to be gathered somewhere other than Trent.

**The Washington congress, and a Quebec silence.** The congress drew **1,700 delegates**, **over 150 CCA members with 27 of them presenting**, more than 160 exhibitors, and delegations from sixteen to eighteen countries; the Canadian reception was at the residence of Ambassador **Allan Gotlieb**, and Nancy Reagan opened it. Alongside it, delegates from the USA, Canada, Jamaica, Japan, India, Australia, England, Ghana, Costa Rica, Ecuador, Bermuda, Kenya, Israel, Belgium and the People's Republic of China adopted a five-point agreement, presented by **Tom Slater** of Australia, "**to do these things under the name of the International Camping Fellowship**" — the founding text of that body, printed in full in both languages in the CCA's own magazine.^8bm Alberta reported 27 delegates and the Atlantic region 18. **"Camping From Coast To Coast" in that issue carries no Quebec report at all** — Atlantic, New Brunswick, Ontario, Nova Scotia, Alberta and British Columbia file; Quebec does not. That is a null result, not evidence that no Quebec delegates attended, and is recorded as one.


### A third claimant to "Canada's oldest camp", and why the ranking is withdrawn

In the summer of 1987 the national magazine carried two separate statements dating **YM-YWCA Camp Stephens**, on the Lake of the Woods, to **1891**. The camp's own directors: "Y.M.-Y.W.C.A. Camp Stephens **has been providing resident camping experiences for youth since 1891**. **It is one of the oldest resident camps in North America**… Camp Stephens is looking forward to celebrating its **100th anniversary in 1991**." And the "What's In A Name?" feature: "**Originally known in 1891 as the Lake of the Woods Institute**, the Y.M.C.A. Camp Stephens took its present name in 1896," after G. F. Stephens of the Stephens Paint Co., a president of the Winnipeg YMCA.^8bn

**If Camp Stephens has run since 1891, Kanawana is not Canada's second-oldest camp.** And the difficulty is not a stranger's assertion, because this wiki carries a different date for the same camp **from a Kanawana founder**: [[people/t-duncan-patton|T. Duncan Patton]]'s memoir gives his first visit to the Lake of the Woods islands as **July 1894**, "and from that date on camps were promoted and held annually," the islands passing to the Winnipeg YMCA only in 1907. Two documented sources, both from inside the same institution, ninety years apart.

A reconciliation suggests itself — the 1891 "Lake of the Woods Institute" may have been a men's conference-encampment rather than a children's resident camp, with the youth camping Patton describes beginning in 1894 — but **neither source says so**, and adopting it would be inventing the explanation that happens to preserve this camp's ranking. Set beside Big Cove's own director dating that camp to **1891** (recorded above, against 1889 and 1890), **three of the four candidate dates for the country's oldest camp now fall in 1889-1891, and Kanawana's 1894 sits behind all of them.**

What is not in doubt is the claim worth making: **Kanawana is Quebec's first residential summer camp, and one of the oldest in Canada.** The ordinal ranking has been removed from this article, from [[connections/related-camps/quebec-camp-landscape|The Quebec Camp Landscape]] and from [[history/timeline-overview|Timeline Overview]], and the disagreement is recorded as conflict `c_031`.

### Continuity, tested: every claimant has an interruption

The ranking above was withdrawn on founding dates. A separate question is whether Kanawana can claim
to be Canada's **oldest continuously operating** camp — a stricter test, since a camp that stops and
restarts is disqualified however early it began. That test was run in September 2026 against all three
claimants, and the results are set out here because the answer is not the flattering one and should not
have to be rediscovered.

**Big Cove stopped for four summers, and its owner both documents and contradicts this.** The YMCA of
Greater Halifax/Dartmouth's own 165th anniversary yearbook states on page 8 that "**Our very own Big
Cove YMCA Camp, in Pictou County, Nova Scotia, is the oldest continuously running residential camp in
Canada**."^14 Twenty pages later, in the same publication's historical timeline, it records: "**1975 -
1980 Big Cove Camp operated the last season since opening as the children's residential summer camp,
until 1980 when it reopened for resident camping once again.**"^14 Resident camping therefore ceased
after the 1975 season and resumed in 1980 — **four missed summers, 1976 through 1979** — in the same
document that calls the camp continuously running. The two statements are not reconciled there.

**It was a closure, not a planned fallow period.** Both independent accounts describe a cessation
followed by a reopening, and both give the reason as the state of the plant: the camp's own director
Kelley Byrne wrote in 1987 that it "closed in 1976 for want of repairs and reopened in 1980,"^8bl and
the owner's yearbook speaks of the camp having "reopened for resident camping once again."^14 Neither
describes a deliberate rest of the site. Big Cove's current website and its Wikipedia article both
carry the "oldest" claim and mention the interruption nowhere.^15

**Camp Stephens stopped for a wartime summer.** Its alumni association, writing in December 2020,
records that COVID restrictions "made it impossible to open camp this past summer (**the first closure
since 1917**)."^16 That dates its First World War closure to 1917; secondary summaries of the camp's
history give 1918 instead, and no source consulted gives both, so the year is recorded as disputed.
Either way Kanawana **ran** that season — the 1918 report gives 223 campers — while Camp Stephens did
not. The camp's own current pages describe "over 130 years" and mention no closure of any kind.^17

**And Kanawana stopped for two summers.** The 2020 and 2021 residential seasons were lost to Quebec's
prohibition on overnight camps, which the YMCAs of Quebec's own impact report calls "two years of
closures."

**The pandemic does not separate anyone.** All three claimants lost overnight seasons in 2020, and the
distinction that suggests itself — that Kanawana kept its site and staff working while others simply
shut — does not survive checking. **Big Cove did the same thing.** It ran no overnight camp in 2020 but
rented cabins to families; in 2021 it operated as a campground offering canoeing and archery and
sustained itself through its leadership training programme; it returned to normal operations in
2022.^18 Kanawana's alternative operations in those summers — ten day camps in city neighbourhoods, a
virtual campfire, a fundraiser — are the same species of thing. Whether Camp Stephens ran in 2021 could
not be established from any surface consulted, and is recorded as unknown rather than assumed either
way.

**What survives is a different and better-defined claim.** Measured as the longest unbroken run of
consecutive seasons — one neutral metric applied identically to all three — the order is unambiguous:

| Camp | Interruptions on record | Longest unbroken run |
|---|---|---|
| **Camp Kanawana** | 2020–21 (provincial prohibition) | **126 seasons, 1894–2019** |
| Camp Stephens | **1918** (war); 2020 (provincial prohibition) | ~102 seasons, 1919–2019 |
| Big Cove | 1976–79 (want of repairs); 2020–21 | ~87 seasons, 1889–1975 |

**Camp Stephens's wartime year was settled on 2026-09-05.** This table read "1917 or 1918" while the
two dates stood unreconciled. The camp's own 125th Anniversary Chapel Service and the Lake of the Woods
Museum's newsletter, both of 2016, give **1918** and give the same reason — not enough men left to act as
counsellors; the 1917 was a parenthesis in a 2020 fundraising post. Camp Stephens closed in 1918, so its unbroken run begins in
1919 [f_4838]. See [[history/oldest-camp-question|Is Kanawana the Oldest Camp in Canada?]], which also
records a larger question raised by the same document: that service dates the first *boys'* camp at
Stephens to 1911 (conflict c_063).

Kanawana leads by roughly a quarter-century of seasons, and does so on any of Big Cove's three
candidate founding dates. **The defensible superlative is therefore not "oldest" but "longest
continuously operating" — 126 consecutive seasons, 1894 to 2019.**

**The assumption inside that number is named rather than buried.** It depends on Kanawana having missed
no season between 1894 and 2019. No source records one, and the years most likely to have broken the
run are documented as operating: the camp ran through both world wars with boy-weeks flat to rising
across 1940–45, ran in 1918 while Camp Stephens did not, and stayed open through the 1946 polio
outbreak on Dr. Cushing's advice with only canoe trips cancelled. But [[meta/attendance-series|the
attendance series]] had no figure at all for many years, and **absence of a figure is not evidence of
operation**.

**Twenty-two of those years were closed on 2026-09-05, from documents already held here.** The camp's
own directors' reports and the association's annual reports attest seasons for 1900, 1902–03, 1905,
1907–08, 1950–51, 1960–62, 1970–71, 1973–79 and 1985–87 — the 1970s now run unbroken with camper-week
figures for every season from 1970 to 1979 [f_4843, f_4845, f_4846, f_4847, f_4848]. The gap was not an
absence of evidence but an absence of assembly: every one of those documents was already catalogued
here and marked read. A second pass the same day read two bodies of physical evidence as season
records — the plaque corpus, whose boards name campers and counsellors by summer, and the L&V trophy,
which carries a shield for every season from 1947 to 2025 but one — and closed every remaining year from
1976 forward [f_4850, f_4851]. **Three seasons are still blank: 1911, 1914 and 1920**, each needing an
annual-report volume this project does not hold. The trophy carries its own caveat: there is a 2020
shield and the camp did not run in 2020, the Games having been played remotely, so a shield attests a
season and not necessarily a residential one.

### The association after the money stopped: a delivery agent with a federal programme

The last chapter of the funding story this article tells is not a collapse but a change of form. By the summer of 1987 the association that had lost its executive director's funding in November 1984 had **a Fitness Canada-named programme and a Fitness Canada-funded employee**: "**PARC stands for Physical [Activity] Responsibility Centre, so named by Fitness Canada.** Through PARC, and our new PARC employee, **Aniko Varpalotai**, the CCA/ACC plans to take an active leadership role in increasing the quality and quantity of physical activity in Canadian Camps," under five headings — Awareness, Leadership, Programs, Network, Management. Fitness Canada had also funded the printing of the Curriculum for Camp Directors and "received, reviewed and applauded" it.^8bn

**Stated plainly: the national association survived the withdrawal of its operating grant by becoming a delivery agent for a federal programme.** Its own retiring Quebec vice-president was not entirely comfortable with the new emphasis, and said so in the same issue — Ken Willis's "Whither C.C.A.'s Physical Activity Emphasis?" argues that camps taking up a physical-activity mandate must not "slip into the disastrous, competitive maelstrom experienced in school and community team programs," and urges keeping individual records of growth rather than pitting campers against each other.^8bn

**The national body met in Quebec City, 1-3 May 1987.** The Board of Management and President's Council held their annual meeting there, hosted by the ACQ, with visits to the **Camp Keno** headquarters office (Marist Fathers, chemin Saint-Louis) and to **Camp Oasis Notre-Dame** (lac Simon, comté de Portneuf), where **Père Paul Bélanger**, a former CCA vice-president, received the President's Council.^8bn In the whole span this project has read from 1983 onward, the national body had not met in Quebec before; it did so in the year Quebec gained a vice-presidency of its own. The ACQ's own annual colloque, at the **Base de plein air La Vigie** on lac Saint-Joseph in Portneuf, 2-5 April 1987, drew **more than 125 camp directors, cadres and moniteurs** under the theme "**Demain… c'est maintenant**."

**Ken Willis's six years, and both Quebec seats in Quebec hands.** The board notice: "We welcome **Gérard Bachand as Vice-President for Quebec**. To retiring Quebec VP, **Ken Willis**, we offer a thank you for a job very well done over the **past six years**. The CCA/ACC **Certificate of Honour** that Ken received is indeed very well deserved."^8bn That fixes Willis's national tenure at about **1981-1987**, closing a figure this article has followed since he invited the CCA to Quebec in 1980, and **Colette Pouliot-Marier of Quebec City** succeeded Bachand as French Language chair — moving that committee's seat from Rawdon to Quebec City.

**Mary Northway died on 26 February 1987, and the archives this project relies on are largely her doing.** Jocelyn Palm's memorial records that Northway "established the OCA Research Committee and also **engineered the collection and organization of the OCA and CCA Archives now at Trent University**," and that her gift funded the **Neathern Trust** recorded above. She had attended Northway Lodge, Camp Wapomeo and Glen Bernard, was at Glen Bernard "from 1922 until 1939" as camper and staff member, founded **Windy Pine** with Flora Morrison and deeded it to Trent in 1983, lectured at the Institute of Child Study, founded the Brora Centre on retirement, and took an honorary doctorate from Trent in 1979.^8bn Palm adds a precise archive lead: "**In 1981, I interviewed Dr. Northway in order that the tape could be added to the CCA Archives. I have just listened to that tape**" — so a dated Palm-Northway interview tape was in the Trent fonds and playable in 1987.


### The Quebec association in 1987: full camps and a growing membership

The last full report the ACQ gave the national magazine is the most positive in the run, and it comes from a president new to this project. **Henri Chalifoux** — also the Quebec provincial representative from the autumn of 1987, replacing Colette Pouliot-Marier — reports on the summer:^8bo

- **The camps were full.** "Attendance at our camps was excellent, with the **no-vacancy sign up throughout the summer for most of the province's camps** and many had a long waiting list."
- **"Over eighty (80) camps were visited this past summer by five consultants. Out of this number ten (10) were trying to gain provincial recognition and become part of the provincial association for the first time."** Against 76 camps and three consultants in 1986, that is a larger visitation programme and **ten first-time membership applications in a single year**.
- A **three-part triennial development plan** — an orientation plan accepted in May 1987, a revision of each programme's specific objectives due before spring, and a finance-committee plan for self-financing.
- **Civil liability insurance**, with the executive "closely working with the provincial government" on a problem "devenu complexe pour les directeurs de camps, depuis quelques années."
- **A study to identify who actually attended Quebec camps**, funded by the **ministère des Loisirs, de la Chasse et de la Pêche**, renewing user research first done in **1982**.

The last is a specific and findable document, and the closest thing to a demographic survey of Quebec camp clientele this project has encountered; if it and its 1982 predecessor survive, they describe the population Kanawana drew from. *(The magazine's own English translation garbles the funder into two ministries; the French names one, the M.L.C.P., and is the text to trust.)*

**The picture this gives is worth stating against the national one.** In the year the CCA was passing the hat among a hundred camp directors and had become a delivery agent for a federal fitness programme, **the Quebec association's camps were full, its accreditation programme had grown from three consultants to five, and ten camps were applying to join it.** Whatever was failing in Canadian camping in the late 1980s was not failing in Quebec.

**The language policy was adopted.** Pouliot-Marier, re-accepting the French Language Committee chair in May 1987, credits Gérard Bachand's two years with the INCEF '87 needs analysis and with "l'élaboration des politiques linguistiques, **dont les recommendations faites au Conseil des présidents furent acceptées**."^8bo So the policy first drafted in January 1987 was formally adopted by the President's Council that year — the end of a thread this article has followed from the founding of the French Language Committee in the autumn of 1974. An **INCEF 1988** was planned, to carry a review of the three inventories made since 1985. The committee for 1987-88 was **Carmelle Deveau (New Brunswick), Michel Gratton (Ontario), Eileen Mayotte (Saskatchewan) and Ken Willis (Quebec)** — Willis staying on as the committee's Quebec member after retiring from the vice-presidency, and **Eileen Mayotte**, the Quebec association's secretary of the 1940s, still serving nationally.

### "Every camp should be actively archiving"

The clearest statement of the movement's own archive practice comes from an unplanned source: the previous winter's article on the Camp Oconto fire brought in "**a goldmine of material which might otherwise have been lost… from one person who worked on staff at one small camp in Ontario for two years in 1936 and 1937.**" The magazine drew the lesson:^8bo

> "We must recognize that **today's snapshots, camp brochures, crests, cards and programs are tomorrow's priceless archives. Every camp should be actively archiving. Every camping association should be actively archiving as well. Without gathering our histories together, in a safe organized way we are all losers.**"

And it set out the routing, which matters for anyone looking for Quebec material now: the magazine would publish what it could "**before forwarding it to the Canadian Camping Association Archives at Trent University or to the appropriate provincial camping association archives in the camp's home province**." **That is the third statement in three consecutive issues that the provincial associations held archives of their own** — so material sent to the magazine from a Quebec camp in these years may have gone to the ACQ, not to Trent.

**A documented gap in the run itself.** The same issue's editorial note explains why several provinces are silent in it: "**the postal system blocked**" a number of provincial reports and committee items, and a telephone failure blocked the Personality Profile of Lorne Brown.^8bo An absence in the autumn 1987 issue is therefore evidence about Canada Post, not about the associations.


### "Will we survive?" — the association states the ending itself

The last issue *Canadian Camping* ever published opens with the president saying plainly what this article has assembled from Jocelyn Palm's 1985 editorial onward:^8bp

> "**For those intimately involved with the Canadian Camping Association, 1988 is an historic year. It is this year that the Canadian Camping Association is no longer receiving funding for its operating expenses from the Federal Government through Fitness Canada. Will we survive?**
>
> For many years the C.C.A. received handsome funding for its general operation from the Federal Government. **We had an executive director's salary, twice yearly board meetings to which all provincial presidents were flown at government expense, conferences funded by government, and Federal Government administrative money. This funding has been phased out over the past three years, and despite economies, budget cutting and fund raising we are now operating at a deficit each year.**"

Janet Adamson counts the association at "**nine provincial associations and six hundred camps and their directors**… for fifty years." Two details from her appeal are facts rather than rhetoric and belong in the record: "**Because we are facing a deficit in this year, Camp Tawingo is underwriting the complete cost of production of this winter issue of the magazine**" — the last issue of the national magazine was paid for by one camp — and, of the foreign-counsellor protocol this article has followed since 1986, "**Each year Barry [Lowes], at his own expense, flies to Ottawa and negotiates the foreign camp counsellor agreement for C.C.A. He donates his time, the office work involved and the airfare.**" The board's own minutes, two pages later, are blunter: "**There is no money left in the budget to produce this very C.C.A./A.C.C. Magazine you now hold in your hands**," and "**Deep concern was expressed by all over the financial outlook for C.C.A./A.C.C.**"^8bp

**That is why the run ends where it does, said by the association in the issue that turns out to be its last.** The same minutes record intentions that this corpus never shows carried out: a third International Congress proposed for Toronto, a 1989 Directors' Institute at Bolton on 9-12 November, and a national conference for 1989.

**The national survey the association managed to complete first.** The P.A.R.C. programme sent a questionnaire to "close to 600 CCA member camps" in the autumn of 1987 and got **197 back — a 33% response**, composed of **70 religious camps, 52 private, 50 agency, 14 special-needs and 11 other; 179 residential and 18 day camps**.^8bp That composition is the best available answer this project has to what a "Canadian camp" institutionally was in 1987: a third of them religious, a quarter private, a quarter agency-run — the category Kanawana belonged to. Its findings: 53% call physical activity "very important"; 67.5% say more than half their programme is physical activity; the top five priority programmes are **swimming, religion, canoeing, boating and land sports**; 70% have water, forest and fields on site; **campers are mostly 8-15 in almost equal numbers of girls and boys**; and "**a large number of camps expressed a preference for cooperative 'New Games', as opposed to the more traditional, competitive, team and individual sports.**" It is the only designed survey with a stated response rate in the entire run.

**Quebec at the start of 1988.** Gérard Bachand's first report as vice-president records a new leadership programme for administrators, an advanced directors' course ("Directeur 2") begun in December, a "Formateur-plus" course for camps' own trainers, **two studies with the M.L.C.P. — one on insurance and one a market study of summer-camp clientele** (confirming from a second source the study Henri Chalifoux described in the autumn), the development plan going to the Conseil d'administration in mid-January, and "**une Foire des Camps se prépare pour le début de 88, où l'ACQ est le maître d'oeuvre. Le tout se déroulera au Complexe Desjardins de Montréal**."^8bp He also records a handover on the provincial board: "I also want to extend a warm thank you to **Mr. Ken Willis** for all the years he spent working for the QCA… By the same token, I wish to welcome **Mrs. Madelene Allen** who will be taking Ken's place" — so **the director of Camp Ouareau joined the ACQ's board of directors at the start of 1988**.

**The last of the language question.** Colette Pouliot-Marier reports that under the new policy Quebec francophones had for some months been doing the magazine's translation in both directions, "**une amélioration importante**" that francophone readers appreciated — and then appeals, twice, for French copy, because "**les derniers numéros témoignent toujours de la rareté des textes en français**."^8bp Thirteen years of committee work produced a policy, a translation service and an inventory; what it could not produce was contributors.


## Academic Study

The Canadian camping movement has received growing scholarly attention. Sharon Wall's *The Nurture of Nature: Childhood, Antimodernism, and Ontario Summer Camps, 1920-55* (UBC Press, 2009) is the principal study, analyzing camps as "hybrid institutions" navigating antimodernism and modernity.^10 Grace McMorris's thesis on Kanawana (Concordia, 2023) examines how the camp shaped "distinctly masculine and Canadian young men" from 1894 to 1967.^11

The CCA published *Canadian Camping* magazine from 1949 to 1988; digitized issues are available through Trent University Archives on the Internet Archive.^12

A related "camping evening course" at McGill was held in 1952. No direct Kanawana staff participation has been confirmed, though the institutional overlap is strong. Arthur S. Lamb (1886-1958), a former Vancouver YMCA PE director and Springfield College graduate, directed McGill PE for 37 years (1919-1956) and established Canada's first university PE department — a key institutional bridge between YMCA and university training. McGill PE students attended a "Camp School" at Camp Nominingue (founded 1925) learning camping, paddling, portaging, and axe use in the 1940s-1950s.^8p A newly-identified, previously unflagged Concordia sub-series, P0145/12B05 ("Staff and Counsellors," Kamp Kanawana), is a plausible but untested lead for finding direct staff overlap — not yet examined (2026-07-09). No named individual confirmed on both sides of the connection so far (checked Camp Nominingue counsellor Marc Beique specifically — no Kanawana link found).
5. [Largely resolved 2026-07-09] What is in the OCA "Kanawana (1940)" brochure file at Trent? The file's precise physical location is now confirmed: Box 1, Folder 5 of the "Camp Brochures and Photographs" series (OCA fonds 72-007), alphabetically between "Glen Mohr" and "Kiawa." No content description exists online beyond this catalog entry — confirmed as a genuine physical-archive-access requirement, not an online research gap.
6. [Partially resolved] Philip D. Fagans, executive secretary of Ernest Thompson Seton's Woodcraft League of America (headquartered at 13 West 29th St, NYC), visited Camp Kanawana in July 1918 as reported in the *Montreal Gazette* (July 11, 1918).^8q This is the earliest documented Woodcraft League connection. Fagans was previously a YMCA camp director at Trenton NJ and West Side YMCA NYC, and later founded Greenkill Camp in Kingston NY. A Woodcraft League charter cost $3.00 annually; it "certif[ied] that the Tribe is registered at Headquarters" and entitled the tribe to recognize achievements per the *Birch Bark Roll of Woodcraft* and wear League badges.^8q "Almost all [Woodcraft] tribes operated within the YMCA" — the YMCA was the primary institutional host for Woodcraft Indian tribes.^8q The League peaked at ~5,000 members in the 1920s-1930s and dissolved after Seton's death in 1946.^8q The 1923 Kanawana brochure mentions "a large Council Ring where woodcraft meetings are held" but does not explicitly reference the charter, possibly indicating the formal affiliation was being de-emphasized by 1923.^8q
