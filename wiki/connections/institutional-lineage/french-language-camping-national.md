# French-Language Camping in the National Association

*Status: draft | Sources: 9*
*Last Updated: 2026-09-06 (spun out of The Canadian Camping Movement under p_452)*

## Overview

The Canadian Camping Association became bilingual at a single meeting in **Regina in 1971**, on the
argument of one Quebec priest, and spent the following sixteen years working out what that meant. This
article follows that thread: the constitutional change and the man who made it, the past president who
says plainly that it did not work, the Comité and later **Commission des Camps d'Expression française**,
the two editions of a directory of French-language camps outside Quebec that nobody else in Canadian
camping was compiling, and the language policy the association finally adopted in 1987 — to comply with
a rule, not on its own initiative.

**Why it belongs in Kanawana's wiki.** Kanawana was an English camp inside a province whose camping was
majority francophone, accredited by a provincial body with two language sections, and a member of a
national association that took thirty-four years to admit the francophone half of its own Quebec
membership. The structural position that produced Kanawana's own French-language experiments — set out
at [[traditions/french-language-camping|French-Language Camping at Kanawana]] — is the position
described here from the other end, the national one.

The provincial body's own story is at
[[connections/institutional-lineage/quebec-camping-association|The Quebec Camping Association /
Association des Camps du Québec]]; the wider argument this was cut from is at
[[connections/institutional-lineage/canadian-camping-movement|The Canadian Camping Movement]].

## The thread, 1967 to 1987

**How the CCA became a bilingual association, and who made it one.** The constitutional change is dated to a single meeting by the tribute the association printed when its Quebec vice-president left in December 1977: "**When the C.C.A. Board was struggling with the revision of our constitution at a meeting in Regina in 1971, it was Paul Belanger who convinced us in his delicately kind and inimitable way that we ought to give warm recognition to our two languages in the C.C.A. We became convinced, committed and constituted and, out of the Regina meeting, emerged Le Comité des Camps Français and a new constitution with this stated purpose — 'To promote the development of Camping in Canada with the highest ideals and traditions of both language groups consistent with the national heritage of Canada.'**"^1

**Abbé Paul Belanger** founded **Camp-École Keno in 1964**, about sixty miles northwest of Quebec City; sat on the Quebec Camping Association's Provincial Board; was President of the Association des Camps du Québec and the **first President of the CCA's French Language Camping Committee**; was the ACQ's delegate to the Fédération de canot-camping; chaired **the first Canadian Conference on French Language Camping, at the Montmorency Forest in April 1976**; and was Vice-President of the CCA until his order, the Marists, called him to Rome as an Assistant General in late 1977, with responsibility for Quebec, New England and France.^1 He was honoured at an ACQ-QCA dinner in Quebec City on **24 November 1977**. A French tribute by **Rosaire Poussard** was printed beside the English one, itself an artefact of the bilingualism Belanger had argued for.

For Kanawana the point is structural. The association that accredited it was a single English body until 1971 and a formally bilingual one afterwards, and the Quebec section Kanawana belonged to sat on both sides of that change — the English Section under Derek Walsh's presidency, the French Section under Belanger's, each supplying a vice-president to the joint Provincial Board.^1

**And a past president says plainly that it did not work.** Everything above is drawn from the association's own copy, which is uniformly positive about its bilingualism. The one dissenting voice in the run is **John Latimer**, national president from 1971 to 1975 — the years the constitution changed — asked in August 1982 to name the volatile issues of his presidency:

> "**I personally felt uncomfortable about the relationship between French camps and camps in English-speaking Canada. This, unfortunately, is an ongoing concern even today, wrought with political overtones — I know I've made enemies over this issue** but I keep thinking we're forgetting the campers and their collective interests which aren't being served by our politics."^2

That is his view and is recorded as his view, not as a finding about what happened. But it is the only candid statement of the problem this project has, and it comes from the man who held the chair while the constitution was being rewritten. Read beside the disappearance of the Quebec English Section from the printed lists and the ACQ's emergence as the province's single national voice, it suggests that what those printed lists show as a tidy institutional simplification was experienced from inside as something harder. In the same interview Latimer records that the association never resolved two other questions of his term: it wanted to change its name, because "**we were and still are assumed by the public to be 'that group in charge of trailer camping throughout Canada'**", and never did; and on national standards, "**this issue still isn't resolved — there are no national standards**, and I think it would be very difficult to implement the same standards to all camps across the country."^2

**The French Language Committee dates from the autumn of 1974.** This article has traced the association's bilingualism from the 1971 name change without a date for the committee itself. Its incoming chairman supplies one: "**ce comité qui a été mis sur pied à l'automne 1974** a pour but d'assurer une présence active des camps francophones et de faire entendre le point de vue des camps de langue française au sein de l'Association." She also names an event previously unknown here — "**le congrès du printemps 1976 qui a réuni des directeurs de camps de vacances et personnes intéressées au phénomène des camps, venus de toutes les provinces, autour du thème de 'L'activité pédagogique du camp de vacances'**" — a national gathering of French-language camp directors from every province, after which many kept up the contacts they made. **Colette Pouliot-Marier** accepted the chairmanship on **22 April 1984**, succeeding **Pauline Rondeau, directrice du Camp Claret**, who resigned "due to severe time restraints"; her stated tasks were generating French articles for the magazine and establishing priorities and procedures for translation.^3

**What it took to run an English camp for French-speaking children, set out by a Quebec director in 1985.** A Society of Camp Directors prize paper by **Madelene Ferguson Allen**, director of Camp Ouareau, was printed in both languages under the title "The Kid Who Doesn't Speak English… How Do We Help?" / "L'enfant qui ne parle pas anglais". Its opening judgement is blunt: "**In too many cases the most short changed children at our summer camps are these children who don't speak English. Camps are not in tune with this growing segment of our camper population.**"^4 It is the fullest treatment of the question anywhere in the digitized run, it comes from the province where the question was sharpest, and it is set out in full in [[connections/related-camps/camp-ouareau|Camp Ouareau]]. It matters here because it establishes the standard of practice a Quebec English-language camp was being held to in 1985 — a translated pre-arrival letter and a fifty-word camp vocabulary, a deliberate refusal to seat the child beside a fluent speaker of his own language, a shared "core phrase" method across the whole staff, and the flat rule that "**a camp should not, under any circumstances, accept a child who does not speak English unless it is willing to become totally involved**." Kanawana's own practice in this period is not documented; this is what it should be read against.

**The French section of the Quebec association was still outside the national body in 1967.** From the same article, listing the association's gaps: "**No Association exists in Saskatchewan**, some camps in the Maritimes are not members of the Association and **the French section of the Quebec Camping Association are still not members of the Canadian Association.**"^5 [[connections/institutional-lineage/quebec-camping-association|The Quebec Camping Association]] records the ACQ's founding in 1958 and its joining the QCA in 1961, with a Conseil Provincial coordinating the two sections from that year. What it has not recorded is that **joining the provincial body did not make the French section a member of the national one** — and that as late as 1967, thirty years after the QCA's founding and four years before the CCA took its bilingual name, the francophone half of Quebec camping stood formally outside the Canadian Camping Association. That changes the shape of the bilingualism story traced below from 1971: the name change and the 1974 French Language Committee were not a movement catching up with a French section it already had, but the machinery of bringing one in.

**A French camp-management library, and a Belgium-Quebec comparison.** The new-additions list in the same issue is almost entirely French: an eight-part **"Série 'Un camp à faire'"** — five *Manuel de gestion* volumes on general administration, human resources, programmes, equipment and finance, plus *Évaluation du rendement*, *Les objectifs et le programme*, and *La planification des ressources humaines et sélection du personnel* — and, separately, ***Les colonies de vacances d'expression française en Belgique (communauté Wallonie-Bruxelles) et les camps de vacances au Québec***.^6 Neither authorship nor publisher is given. The second title is the notable one: a study setting Quebec's camps beside those of francophone Belgium, sold nationally in 1986 and previously unknown to this project.

**A directory of French-language camps outside Quebec.** The French Language Committee, chaired from 1985 by **Gérard Bachand of Rawdon, Quebec**, reports "**la publication d'un répertoire des camps francophones hors Québec**… rendue possible grâce à la collaboration du **Conseil de la Vie française en Amérique**. **Vingt-quatre camps figurent dans la première édition**."^7 A 1985 directory of the twenty-four French-language camps outside Quebec then known — a document that would map francophone camping across Canada from the one body positioned to see it whole.

**An inventory of French-language camping, second edition.** The Commission des Camps d'Expression française reported a new member, **M. Michel Gratton of Hearst, Ontario** — "we thus have two 'out-of-Quebec' members which should help promote the reality of French in the camping world" — and the launch of the **second edition of the Répertoire de l'Inventaire des Camps d'Expression française (INCEF)**, with **M. Jean-Luc Côté of the Conseil de la Vie française en Amérique** gathering the data. The next task it set itself was to "**élaborer notre politique linguistique pour l'Association des Camps du Canada**."^8 Read against the twenty-four-camp directory of 1985 recorded above, the association's francophone wing was by 1986 doing the census work no one else in Canadian camping was doing.

**The association acquired a language policy in 1987 — to comply, not on its own initiative.** Gérard Bachand's committee reported that it had "**élaboré une politique linguistique pour l'ACC/CCA afin de nous rendre conformes aux lois du bilinguisme des organismes nationaux**," with a translation policy attached, for board approval by spring.^9 **That is thirteen years after the French Language Committee was founded in the autumn of 1974 and sixteen after the association took its bilingual name in 1971** — and the stated reason is federal bilingualism requirements on national organisations. It is a useful corrective to the article's own account above: the machinery of bringing the French section in was built slowly, and the formal policy came last and under external pressure.

**INCEF '87.** The same note records that "**dès la fin janvier nous publiions la deuxième édition du répertoire des camps d'expression française, hors Québec (Incef '87)**," produced with the **Conseil de la Vie française en Amérique** and the **Ministre des Affaires Intergouvernementales**, with copies held at the CCA office in Toronto and a needs survey of the listed camps under way.^9 The first edition of 1985 carried twenty-four camps; the second exists, is dated, is jointly funded by a French-Canadian cultural body and a Quebec ministry, and was findable in Toronto.

## Open Questions

1. [Important] **Did Kanawana appear in either edition of the INCEF directory?** It was an English camp,
   so probably not — but the directory covered French-language camps *outside* Quebec as well as inside,
   and its compiler worked from the Conseil de la Vie française en Amérique rather than from the camping
   associations. Neither edition is held here.
2. [Important] **What did the association's 1987 language policy actually say?** It is described in the
   committee's own report as *élaborée* and as required for compliance. The text is not held. The
   Canadian Camping Association fonds at Trent would have it; see `project-docs/trent-access-plan.md`.
3. [Nice-to-have] **When did the French section of the Quebec association finally join the national
   body?** It was still outside in 1967, thirty years after the provincial association's founding and
   four years before the CCA took its bilingual name. No source here dates the change.
4. [Nice-to-have] **Was Madelene Ferguson Allen's 1985 paper on running an English camp for
   French-speaking children read at Kanawana?** It won a Society of Camp Directors prize and was printed
   bilingually across six pages. The camp's own staff training material for those years is not held.
