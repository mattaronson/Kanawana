# The National Office and the Federal Money, 1969-1988

*Status: draft | Sources: 7*
*Last Updated: 2026-09-06 (spun out of [[connections/institutional-lineage/canadian-camping-movement|The Canadian Camping Movement]])*

## Overview

The Canadian Camping Association had a paid national office for most of twenty years, and it had it
because the federal government paid for it. When Ottawa stopped, the association's paper trail
stopped with it. That is why the digitized run of *Canadian Camping* — the single largest body of
source material this project holds, and the source of most of what this wiki knows about the
movement Kanawana belonged to — ends in 1988 rather than continuing. **The end of the record is not
a gap in the digitization and should never be described as one.**

The arc runs: a permanent office with paid staff in Ottawa from **February 1969**, on a grant from
the Fitness and Amateur Sports Branch; the abandonment of that office in under two years, on the
association's own judgement that it "would be of no benefit"; the move into a $45-a-month desk in
the Ontario association's Toronto office from January 1971, alongside the reorganisation that ended
the travelling executive and gave Quebec a standing regional vice-presidency; a full-time executive
director from 1979 on Fitness Canada money; **the withdrawal of that money, announced at the end of
November 1984**; an appeal to the membership in 1986; a Patrons' Fund raised almost entirely from
Ontario camps; survival as a delivery agent for a federal programme; and the president asking, in
the last issue the magazine ever published, "Will we survive?"

**One point is genuinely contested and is left contested.** The association's own account of when
its office began, written in 1985, disagrees with what the association printed at the time. Conflict
`c_064` holds both, and the paragraphs below set out why the contemporaneous text is the harder one
to explain away.

## A permanent office on a federal grant, 1969

In the winter of 1985 the association's president published the reason its own paper trail — and this article's principal source — comes to an end. Fitness Canada had funded the CCA's national office since 1971, its full-time executive director since 1979, and the travel that let volunteers from every province attend board meetings. At the end of November 1984 its director, **Michel Bedard**, told the association that Fitness Canada **would no longer fund the executive director's post at all**, and that support for "all management aspects, re travel for meetings, office functions, administrative assistance, would be **reduced each year by 33-1/3% to terminate completely in 1988**."^1

**The office's own history is older than the association later remembered, and this article had taken
the later account.** The paragraph below, and the one after it, are drawn from Jocelyn Palm's winter
1985 editorial. On one point the association's contemporaneous copy disagrees with it, and the
disagreement is recorded as conflict `c_064` rather than settled here.

The **CCA had a permanent national office with paid staff from February 1969, in Ottawa, on a federal
grant.** The magazine reported it from the annual meeting of **28 February 1969**: "Through a
[grant] from the **Fitness and Amateur Sports Branch of the Department of National Health and
Welfare**, CCA is now able to maintain its own **National Office in Ottawa**, plus the services of an
**Executive Secretary in the person of Mr. Barry Bryant**," at Box 2773, Station D, with extra
secretarial help available through the Downtown Branch of the Ottawa YM-YWCA.^2


## It lasted under two years, and the president wrote down why

**It lasted under two years, and the president wrote down why.** David Hartry's "C.C.A. — New National
Office", dated **14 December 1970**, records the executive's decision after meetings with the Fitness
and Amateur Sport consultant **Cor Westland**, the Ontario Camping Association executive and the head
of the Administrative Centre in Ottawa: "It was felt strongly that the establishment of a National
Office in Ottawa **would be of no benefit to C.C.A.**" The association moved instead into the OCA's
Toronto office from January 1971 — a desk and filing cabinets at **$45.00 a month**, with use of the
OCA's typewriter, duplicator, collator and board room, and the president picking up the mail daily —
and Hartry states the financial position plainly: "**Project Grants are still available to C.C.A. but
little is available for administrative Grant to operate office anywhere whether Toronto or Ottawa.
With the offer of O.C.A., C.C.A. will be able to operate from its own income.**"^2

So on the contemporaneous record the office does not date from 1971 and federal administrative money
did not begin in 1971 — it *stopped* around then, and the 1971 arrangement is what an association
operating on its own income could afford. Palm, writing fourteen years later, most likely dates the
office from the Toronto arrangement she knew and reads later Fitness Canada support back over it. That
is a reading and not a source; both texts are the association's own, and `c_064` is open.


## 1971: the travelling executive ends and the office settles in Toronto

**1971 also ended the travelling executive, which is why Quebec's seat becomes a permanent one.** John
Latimer's first president's letter, in the summer of 1971: "since the Association was founded, **the
Executive moved from province to province every two years. Now there are five Vice-Presidents on the
Executive of C.C.A. who represent various regions in Canada**" — **Tom Creighton** (Atlantic), **Bob
Lazanik** (Quebec), **Jack Pearse** (Ontario), **Eileen Mayotte** (Prairie), **Tanya Krisman** (British
Columbia) — with Brian Graham as treasurer and Rick Bendera as secretary, both of Toronto. "The Board
of C.C.A. is now made up of the Executive, the Provincial Association Presidents and the Committee
Chairmen."^2

This is the structural change behind everything this article records about Quebec's place nationally
from the 1970s on. Before 1971 a province held the national executive for two years and then handed it
on — which is why the head office had been in Montreal to 1963, then Calgary, then Winnipeg, and why
[[people/a-ross-seaman|A. Ross Seaman]] could be the national secretary in 1962 simply by living in
Lachine while the office was on Belgrave Avenue. After 1971 Quebec holds a standing regional
vice-presidency instead, and the office stays in Toronto. The seat this article follows through Bob
Lazanik, Derek Walsh, Paul Belanger and Ken Willis is that seat, and it exists because of this
reorganisation.

*Canadian Camping* ceased publication in 1988. The two facts belong together, and any account of the thinning of the national camping record after the mid-1980s should start here rather than treating it as an unexplained gap.

Palm's winter 1985 editorial is the association's own institutional autobiography and dates several things this article had only inferred. The **national office with paid staff and a permanent address dates from 1971**; before that "the CCA office resided in the home of the elected President," and the office was thereafter shared with the Ontario Camping Association. A **full-time executive director was funded from 1979**, a post **Marjorie Booth held until October 1984**. **National conferences were held in 1972, 1973, 1975 and 1978** before the 1983 International Congress. The **Canadian Recreational Canoeing Association "was an outgrowth of CCA with an initial board of CCA personnel."** And the board's 1982 decision to reduce its dependence on Ottawa produced "a hefty increase in CCA fees paid through the provincial associations" — so the national fees Kanawana paid through the ACQ rose in 1982 as a matter of national strategy. A consultant, **John Fisher**, was engaged in March 1985 to advise on restructuring, reporting first to the Regina board meeting and annual general meeting of **25-26 April 1985**.^1

Palm's figures for the association at its high-water mark: **some 400,000 campers, about 11,000 of them from outside Canada, over 38,000 paid staff and over 16,000 volunteers, an estimated 80% of staff aged 13 to 24.**

## Why the money stopped, from someone who was in the room

**Why the federal money stopped, from someone who was in the room.** Ken Willis, writing in the summer of 1985: "I was on the Board and Executive at the time discussions centred around what the Canadian Camping Magazine could do for the Association in its present financial crisis … **resultant upon Fitness Canada's thinking that camping is not within their jurisdiction**."^3 The withdrawal recorded above was therefore a jurisdictional judgement about whether organized camping fell within a fitness mandate, not simply a share of general austerity — which is why the president's reply set out Fitness Canada's own three-part mandate and argued the association fitted inside it.

## Passing the hat, 1986

**Passing the hat, 1986.** Two years after Fitness Canada's withdrawal the association appealed for private money over the names of **Janet Adamson**, president, and **Jack Pearse**, chairman of a new CCA/ACC Patrons' Foundation: "**An additional income of $30,000.00, this year, represents the difference between a giant step forward and an irretrievable step backward**… We are sending this plea to approximately **100 people** — some current Camp Directors, some retired Camp Directors, some friends of camping." A first letter had gone out in April 1986; the autumn issue reprinted it for the whole readership with a blue pledge card tipped in, and Pearse's drive had by then raised "nearly $10,000 in five months." The long-term aim was a standing Patrons' Foundation "whose proceeds will perpetuate camping in Canada."^4 The 50th Anniversary Patrons' Fund is what the funding cut of November 1984 looked like by its second year.
**What the Patrons' Fund actually raised, and where the money came from.** The appeal for $30,000 recorded above was accounted for in the spring of 1987: "**To date, the donations total $11,918.11**" — about forty per cent of the target — from a list of fifty-five individuals and eighteen camps, printed in full.^5
**Every one of the eighteen camps is Ontarian.** Canadian Adventure, Gay Venture, Haliburton Hockey Haven, Kawabi, New Moon, Oconto, Robin Hood, Tanamakoon, Tawingo, the Taylor Statten Camps, Trailfinder, Wahanowin, White Pine and YMCA Wanakita. **No Quebec camp gave, and no camp from any other province.** Quebec appears only among the individual donors — **Gérard Bachand** of Rawdon, **Maurice Blaquière**, **Jacqueline Lavoie**, **Dr. Ken Willis** of Magog, **Peter and F. M. Van Wagner** of Pointe-Claire, and **Eileen Mayotte**, the Quebec association's secretary of the 1940s, here alive and giving money in 1987, which is the latest fixed point this project holds for her.^5 That distribution is the finding: at the moment the national body needed money to survive the federal withdrawal, the institutional giving came from the private camps of one province, and Quebec's contribution was personal rather than corporate.

## A delivery agent with a federal programme

The last chapter of the funding story this article tells is not a collapse but a change of form. By the summer of 1987 the association that had lost its executive director's funding in November 1984 had **a Fitness Canada-named programme and a Fitness Canada-funded employee**: "**PARC stands for Physical [Activity] Responsibility Centre, so named by Fitness Canada.** Through PARC, and our new PARC employee, **Aniko Varpalotai**, the CCA/ACC plans to take an active leadership role in increasing the quality and quantity of physical activity in Canadian Camps," under five headings — Awareness, Leadership, Programs, Network, Management. Fitness Canada had also funded the printing of the Curriculum for Camp Directors and "received, reviewed and applauded" it.^6

**Stated plainly: the national association survived the withdrawal of its operating grant by becoming a delivery agent for a federal programme.** Its own retiring Quebec vice-president was not entirely comfortable with the new emphasis, and said so in the same issue — Ken Willis's "Whither C.C.A.'s Physical Activity Emphasis?" argues that camps taking up a physical-activity mandate must not "slip into the disastrous, competitive maelstrom experienced in school and community team programs," and urges keeping individual records of growth rather than pitting campers against each other.^6

**The national body met in Quebec City, 1-3 May 1987.** The Board of Management and President's Council held their annual meeting there, hosted by the ACQ, with visits to the **Camp Keno** headquarters office (Marist Fathers, chemin Saint-Louis) and to **Camp Oasis Notre-Dame** (lac Simon, comté de Portneuf), where **Père Paul Bélanger**, a former CCA vice-president, received the President's Council.^6 In the whole span this project has read from 1983 onward, the national body had not met in Quebec before; it did so in the year Quebec gained a vice-presidency of its own. The ACQ's own annual colloque, at the **Base de plein air La Vigie** on lac Saint-Joseph in Portneuf, 2-5 April 1987, drew **more than 125 camp directors, cadres and moniteurs** under the theme "**Demain… c'est maintenant**."

**Ken Willis's six years, and both Quebec seats in Quebec hands.** The board notice: "We welcome **Gérard Bachand as Vice-President for Quebec**. To retiring Quebec VP, **Ken Willis**, we offer a thank you for a job very well done over the **past six years**. The CCA/ACC **Certificate of Honour** that Ken received is indeed very well deserved."^6 That fixes Willis's national tenure at about **1981-1987**, closing a figure this article has followed since he invited the CCA to Quebec in 1980, and **Colette Pouliot-Marier of Quebec City** succeeded Bachand as French Language chair — moving that committee's seat from Rawdon to Quebec City.

## "Will we survive?" — the association states the ending itself

The last issue *Canadian Camping* ever published opens with the president saying plainly what this article has assembled from Jocelyn Palm's 1985 editorial onward:^7

> "**For those intimately involved with the Canadian Camping Association, 1988 is an historic year. It is this year that the Canadian Camping Association is no longer receiving funding for its operating expenses from the Federal Government through Fitness Canada. Will we survive?**
>
> For many years the C.C.A. received handsome funding for its general operation from the Federal Government. **We had an executive director's salary, twice yearly board meetings to which all provincial presidents were flown at government expense, conferences funded by government, and Federal Government administrative money. This funding has been phased out over the past three years, and despite economies, budget cutting and fund raising we are now operating at a deficit each year.**"

Janet Adamson counts the association at "**nine provincial associations and six hundred camps and their directors**… for fifty years." Two details from her appeal are facts rather than rhetoric and belong in the record: "**Because we are facing a deficit in this year, Camp Tawingo is underwriting the complete cost of production of this winter issue of the magazine**" — the last issue of the national magazine was paid for by one camp — and, of the foreign-counsellor protocol this article has followed since 1986, "**Each year Barry [Lowes], at his own expense, flies to Ottawa and negotiates the foreign camp counsellor agreement for C.C.A. He donates his time, the office work involved and the airfare.**" The board's own minutes, two pages later, are blunter: "**There is no money left in the budget to produce this very C.C.A./A.C.C. Magazine you now hold in your hands**," and "**Deep concern was expressed by all over the financial outlook for C.C.A./A.C.C.**"^7

**That is why the run ends where it does, said by the association in the issue that turns out to be its last.** The same minutes record intentions that this corpus never shows carried out: a third International Congress proposed for Toronto, a 1989 Directors' Institute at Bolton on 9-12 November, and a national conference for 1989.

**The national survey the association managed to complete first.** The P.A.R.C. programme sent a questionnaire to "close to 600 CCA member camps" in the autumn of 1987 and got **197 back — a 33% response**, composed of **70 religious camps, 52 private, 50 agency, 14 special-needs and 11 other; 179 residential and 18 day camps**.^7 That composition is the best available answer this project has to what a "Canadian camp" institutionally was in 1987: a third of them religious, a quarter private, a quarter agency-run — the category Kanawana belonged to. Its findings: 53% call physical activity "very important"; 67.5% say more than half their programme is physical activity; the top five priority programmes are **swimming, religion, canoeing, boating and land sports**; 70% have water, forest and fields on site; **campers are mostly 8-15 in almost equal numbers of girls and boys**; and "**a large number of camps expressed a preference for cooperative 'New Games', as opposed to the more traditional, competitive, team and individual sports.**" It is the only designed survey with a stated response rate in the entire run.

## Open Questions

1. **Did the association survive?** The record this project holds ends with the question being
   asked, in 1988, and answers it nowhere. The Canadian Camping Association exists today; what
   happened between 1988 and now is entirely outside this corpus.
2. **What became of the national office's files?** Mary Roy's account of the office describes her
   pressing provincial associations to send newsletters, board minutes and conference reports to
   it. If those were kept, Quebec association paper of 1975-1983 is in the CCA fonds at Trent.
3. **Was the $30,000 Patrons' Fund appeal ever met?** Eighteen camps are named as subscribers and
   every one of them is Ontarian. Whether the target was reached, and whether any Quebec camp gave,
   is not stated in anything read here.
4. **Who was Barry Bryant, and what did the Ottawa office do in its two years?** He is named once,
   with a post-office box and a borrowed secretary, and never again.
