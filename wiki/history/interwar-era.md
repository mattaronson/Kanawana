# The Interwar Era (1920s–1930s)

*Status: draft | Sources: 10*
*Last Updated: 2026-07-11*

## Overview

Between the end of the First World War and the outbreak of the Second, Camp Kanawana grew from a small, tented boys' camp into a sophisticated physical and institutional operation. This period saw the construction of the Council Ring and the arrival of a totem pole, a formal charter in the national Woodcraft League movement, the camp's first stable multi-year staff core, and the writing of its first self-history. No dedicated article previously narrated these two decades as continuous history; the material was documented but scattered across [[people/directors-index|Directors and Staff of Camp Kanawana]], [[site/council-ring|The Council Ring]], and [[connections/institutional-lineage/canadian-camping-movement|The Canadian Camping Movement]].

## Staff and Structure, 1921–1923

The 1921, 1922, and 1923 camp brochures, together with the 1923 *Gas Bag* reunion newsletter, provide the most detailed staffing records of the camp's first postwar seasons.^1 ^2 ^3 ^4 Several individuals served across multiple years, indicating an already-stable core staff: Harry Smith as camp chef from around 1913 (documented through his 9th and 10th years, 1921–1922), Johnston W. Abraham as Business Manager from around 1914, F.H. Spinney as Educational Director from around 1917, and Allan B. Cornell as Educational Director from around 1918 through at least 1923, holding the camp's highest staff honour, the Large K badge.^1 Directors during these years were G.D. Brandon (1921), Lemuel P. Ereaux (1922), and Philip G. Paterson (1923) — the 1923 season is the best-documented, with the *Gas Bag* separately naming R.E.G. Davis as "Chief Davis," creating an unresolved ambiguity about whether Paterson or Davis held overall command that year.^1 ^4 See [[people/directors-index|Directors and Staff of Camp Kanawana]] for the full staff rosters and the reasoning behind that ambiguity.

## The Woodcraft League Charter (1922)

In 1922, Kanawana obtained a charter as a tribe of the Woodcraft League of America, the youth movement founded by naturalist and author Ernest Thompson Seton.^5 A charter cost $3.00 annually, obtained by application to the Council of Guidance at the League's National Headquarters in New York City, and entitled the tribe to be recognized within the national organization's structure.^6 The League itself had grown to roughly 5,000 members at its peak in the 1920s–1930s, with groups in some 20 countries, before going out of existence after Seton's death in 1946.^7 ^8 A Kanawana season report from 1925, quoted in Grace McMorris's thesis, describes the Woodcraft program as "easily adapted to camp."^9 By contrast, the camp's 1923 brochure mentions only "a large Council Ring where woodcraft meetings are held," without explicitly citing the League charter — possibly indicating the formal national affiliation postdates the Council Ring's own construction, or simply that the brochure's author did not think the affiliation worth naming.^4 An earlier, informal connection predates the charter: in summer 1918, Philip D. Fagans, the League's Executive Secretary, personally visited Kanawana and demonstrated Woodcraft League activities to the boys.^10

## The Council Ring and Totem Pole (1922, 1927)

Senior campers built the Council Ring in 1922, sited between the cabins and the chapel — a tiered outdoor amphitheatre for evening programs, campfires, and ritual events.^9 The council-ring form itself traces to landscape architect Jens Jensen and to Seton's own Woodcraft Indians movement (Silver Bay camp, 1910), through which it spread across North American organized camping generally; Kanawana's 1922 adoption sits within that wider movement rather than being a unique invention. Under Harold Cross's directorship in 1927, the site gained a totem pole and a teepee built from old canvas — additions McMorris characterizes as part of Cross's broader expansion of the camp's "Indian programming," a suite of activities and symbols borrowing from Indigenous cultures that was common across the YMCA camping movement of the period. See [[site/council-ring|The Council Ring]] for the ring's later history, including the "Tribes of Kanawana in Council" ceremony script (c. 1925–1927) and the site's eventual 2020s reconciliation-era renaming.

## The Mid-1930s and the Camp's First History

By 1935, staffing had further stabilized under Camp Chief Greig Macdiarmid, with Howie Langille as Senior Director, Ernie Taylor as Junior Director, and Lorne Hamilton as Juvenile Director, overseeing 35 counsellors.^9 Macdiarmid's tenure is independently confirmed continuing through at least 1938 by that July's *Green Triangle* newsletter.^11 In 1933, Ralph Dawson wrote "History of Kamp Kanawana" — the camp's first attempt at documenting its own past — and R.L. Charlton's companion 1943 manuscript, "Notes re Early Days of YMCA Camps at Lake St. Joseph and Kanawana," would later corroborate and extend Dawson's account. See [[people/ralph-dawson|Ralph Dawson]] and [[people/rl-charlton|R.L. Charlton]] for both men's fuller biographies. No source specific to this KB documents how the camp weathered the Depression financially or in attendance; the staffing and programming record continues without evident interruption, but that silence should not be read as confirmation that the camp was financially untouched by the era — see Open Questions.
