# Project Noosphere (1971)

*Status: draft | Sources: 4*
*Last Updated: 2026-09-07 (spun out of traditions/programs-activities.md when a 1972 interview with its research analyst doubled the material)*

## Overview

**In the summer of 1971 an outside body rented Camp Kanawana for a fortnight and used it to run a model of the world.** Project Noosphere was a simulation game conceived by the "Noosphere Committee," whose members were affiliated with the **Canadian International Development Agency** and **CUSO**, and financed by a federal **Opportunities for Youth** grant.^4 Forty-six teenagers were divided into three villages by language — an English "rich" village in the Farmhouse with electricity and running water, a bilingual "middle-class" village in platform tents, and a French "poor" village that had to build its own shelters — given unequal money and resources, armed with colour-coded water pistols, and left to find out what would happen.^3 ^4

What happened was coups, trade agreements, massacres and a plan for war crimes trials, followed by the players voting capitalism back in because they were bored.^4

**This article was spun out of [[traditions/programs-activities|Programs and Activities]] on 2026-09-07.** The material there was built from Gabor Zinner's 1973 MA thesis and two French-language newspaper reports; a McGill Daily interview with Zinner found the same day roughly doubled it and supplied the mechanics the thesis account had not carried. The parent keeps a paragraph and a link.

## The event, and how it is documented

Four sources, and they are not equally good.

A pre-event notice in *Le Soleil* of **7 July 1971** described the project as organised by **Perspective-Jeunesse** for approximately 100 young Canadians aged 16 to 19, modelled on a "Third World Village" run in Winnipeg the previous year and the first such experience attempted in Quebec.^1 A second report appeared in *Montreal-matin* on **24 August 1971**.^2

**Zinner's MA thesis is the best witness on every number**, and this article prefers it wherever the sources differ.^3 It gives 46 participants aged 15–20, not the ~100 aged 16–19 of the July notice — and explains the gap itself, since organisers "had hoped to attract nearly double the number of participants that were in fact engaged," so the hundred was a recruitment target. Participants arrived on **22 August**. The villages were named **Cannabis** (12 people, English), **Slide dans Slutch** (17, bilingual), and **Triquenimo** (16, French — its name built from the first syllables of Trois-Rivières, Québec, Nicolet and Montreal, the home regions of its members). The scenario's turning point came on **27 August**, when a "capitalist" faction in the English village staged an in-game coup, killing six opposing members with coloured water pistols and co-opting the one neutral holdout, after the village had briefly voted to abolish money. The acknowledgements credit **Pierre Devrud** as co-author of the scenario and game master, and Professor **Harold M. Waller** of McGill Political Science as Zinner's adviser.

**And the thesis never names the camp.** Across 176 pages it does not write "Camp Kanawana," "YMCA" or "Saint-Sauveur" once, describing the venue only as "a summer camp…in the Laurentian Mountains near Montreal," rented for three weeks.^3 Until 2026-09-07 the identification therefore rested entirely on the two French-language newspaper reports.

**It rests on more than that now.** *The McGill Daily* of **27 October 1972** carries an interview with Zinner by Phyllis Piatt, published fourteen months after the event and a year before the thesis, under the heading "Strange games in the Quebec bush." Its first sentence: "Project Noosphere is a simulation game which was played in the summer of 1971 **at Camp Kanawana** in the Laurentians."^4 The research analyst who co-authored the scenario, naming the camp in print, is a materially better attestation than a report about him.

*What it still does not give.* "YMCA," "Otoreke" and "Saint-Sauveur" occur **zero** times in that issue. **The Association's own part in hosting the event is undocumented from any source held here** — no rental agreement, no board minute, no mention in a director's report. Something was signed and nothing about it has been found.

## How the world was built

The design is in the interview and not in the wiki's earlier account.^4

**Money.** "As soon as you walked into the camp (and these things were arbitrarily done) you were given an envelope, and in the envelope you were given X amount of capital, plus control over whatever resources you were given. We printed up currency. They were referred to simply as **newspheres**, and the rich camp started out with I think **55% of all the newspheres** programmed into the game. The middle-class camp had **30%** and the poor had **20%**."

**Resources**, distributed so that no village could feed itself. The English village had running water, electricity, canned goods, and video and tape recording equipment. The bilingual village, in tents on platforms, controlled fresh produce and the transportation and real estate industries. The French village had one out-house and one pump, no tents at all — "they had no tent and had to erect their own shelters" — and controlled crude and raw materials, oil and kerosene. It was also **the only group with an oven to bake bread**.

**Weapons, colour-coded to economic rank.** The rich village's squirt guns were filled with **yellow** paint, which "represented nuclear capability and could 'kill'." The other two had **red** paint, "conventional weapons," which could only "wound." Zinner's reason is the clearest statement of the whole design: "the reason that we had the red and the yellow distinction in armaments was because **we had to make military capability a function of economic capability, which is the way it happens in the real world**."

**Casualties cost the village, not the player.** "Death meant that an individual that was shot would be taken to an isolation area, which meant that he couldn't participate in the group's activities or productive processes. If someone was shot rather than killed, it meant that he would remain within the camp, but he couldn't contribute to the productive and recreational aspects of the camp, which meant that they would actually **constitute a burden to the village**."

**Each village had classes of its own**, modelled on real economies: the rich village on the United States, with "vast concentrations of wealth in the hands of a few individuals"; the bilingual village on "Scandinavian countries," "essentially middle-class and you didn't have such great disparities"; and the poor village with "lots of indigents but one or two extremely wealthy people," for which Zinner's analogy was "the oil magnates of the Near East."

**Nothing else was prescribed.** "We didn't structure the relationship in any way; in other words we wanted to see what would evolve from a given set of structures that were programmed into the game. We also gave them the opportunity to totally abolish the structure."

## What happened

"I have a day-to-day account as to what happened — there were coups, there was warfare, there were trade agreements, there were massacres, there were going to be war crime trials. Then there was inter-village cooperation in many spheres, there was an underlying necessity to exchange."^4

**Zinner read the antagonism as Canadian.** "There were also cultural antagonisms which surfaced, which were very analogous to what we have here in Canada, in Quebec (because in part this was a **Canadian** simulation even though it attempted to be international in scope), in that the English were constantly the aggressors, and they espoused a capitalist ideology in terms of the way they wanted to conduct their own affairs, whereas the French and bilingual camps were more in accord with a communal spirit."^4 His conclusion is more precise than the setup would predict: "The English camp felt very menaced by demands for equity, and for the abolition of capital, and I would say that **the English paranoia was based on economics primarily, rather than on language**. They knew they were sitting on the goods, and they knew the other camps weren't."

That is a striking thing to have been staged in a Laurentian camp in **August 1971** — ten months after the October Crisis, six years before Bill 101 — by federal development staff, with Montreal teenagers playing the parts.

**The children took it seriously enough to frighten themselves.** "They actually plundered the poor camp and massacred people — it was really incredible. You couldn't imagine the fervor, I mean there were actual tears. Two guys went and wiped out thirteen people, and one of the guys, when he came back — these are idealistic kids, you know, they go in with the notion that the adult world is fucked up and that they're really pure, and they see themselves engaging in the same type of phenomena when it comes to haggling over concrete material goods — and **the kid actually cried when he realized what he'd been reduced to**."^4

**And then they voted capitalism back in.** Given the power to abolish the money system, the players restored it. Their stated reason was pedagogical: "we came here to learn about the real world and we don't want to be fantasizing in an idyllic Noosphere. Let us grapple with structures as they exist in the world and let us proceed from there — otherwise we're not going to be getting an education." Zinner gives a second reason and does not rank it below the first: "One of the reasons they advocated this, was that **they were really bored** — the thing wasn't challenging enough for them — they wanted stimulus."^4

## Where the sources disagree

Set against each other, with the thesis preferred throughout:

| | Thesis (1973)^3 | Interview (Oct 1972)^4 |
|---|---|---|
| Participants | 46 | 45 |
| Ages | 15–20 | 15–19 |
| Duration | rented for three weeks | "a two-week period"; "we had two weeks" |
| Village split | 12 / 17 / 16 | "one-third" each |
| Poor village housing | ground tents | "no tent… had to erect their own shelters" |

**None of this needs a conflict record.** It is the expected shape of a man talking from memory fourteen months on, against a document he wrote with his notes in front of him. The three-weeks-against-two is probably not a disagreement at all: a three-week rental with a fortnight's game inside it satisfies both. Where the interview is the better source is on everything the thesis account here does not carry — the CIDA and CUSO affiliations, the mechanics, the currency, and Zinner's own reading of the result.

## Why it belongs in Kanawana's history

The camp is the venue and not the author, and this article does not pretend otherwise. Its interest is what the venue was being used for. **Noosphere is the earliest documented instance of Kanawana's site being rented to an outside organisation for programming that had nothing to do with the YMCA's own aims** — anticipating the camp's later role as a year-round conference and retreat centre by decades. The Front Camp Farmhouse, which the camp's own records describe as a winterised building with a kitchen, is recognisable in the interview's "comfortable farm house, with running water, electricity"; the physical arrangement the game needed was one the site already had. See [[site/the-kanawana-site|The Kanawana Site]].

## Open Questions

1. [Critical] **Who at the YMCA agreed to this, and on what terms?** No rental agreement, board minute, correspondence or director's report mentioning Noosphere has been found. The Association hosted a federally funded political simulation in which teenagers shot each other with paint and staged massacres, and nothing in its own paper record acknowledges it. Concordia's Fonds P145 director's reports for 1971 are the place to look.
2. [Important] **Was the camp's own staff involved at all**, or did the organisers simply take the site? The interview says the organisers "arranged for the use of the Kanawana facilities" and mentions no camp personnel.
3. [Important] **What was Perspective-Jeunesse's relationship to the Noosphere Committee?** *Le Soleil* attributes the organising to Perspective-Jeunesse; the interview attributes it to a Noosphere Committee with CIDA and CUSO affiliations and an Opportunities for Youth grant. Perspective-Jeunesse was the French name of the Opportunities for Youth programme, which would reconcile them — but that is an inference and is not asserted here.
4. [Nice-to-have] **Who were Pierre Devrud and the other committee members?** Devrud co-authored the scenario and was game master, and appears nowhere else in this project.
5. [Nice-to-have] **Did anything come of it?** The thesis is the only documented output. Whether the Noosphere Committee ran a second iteration anywhere, or whether the model was taken up by CIDA or CUSO in their own education work, is unknown.
