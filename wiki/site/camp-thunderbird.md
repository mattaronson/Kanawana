# Camp Thunderbird

*Status: draft | Sources: 6*
*Last Updated: 2026-09-06*

## Overview

Camp Thunderbird was a one-week boys' camp run by the Montreal YMCA's **Central branch** on an
island in the **rivière des Mille Îles**, twenty miles from the city, in **two consecutive summers in the
early 1940s** — one of them certainly 1943, the other either 1942 or 1944, for reasons set out
under *How many summers* below.^1 ^2 The association called it what it was: "an **experimental near-the-city camp**."^3
It took twenty-two people in its 1943 Central Branch period, boys aged twelve to fifteen, at
$7.00 a week.^1 ^2

Until 2026 this project knew Thunderbird only as a sub-series number in the Concordia finding aid
(P0145/12I).^5 Two documents in the digitized fonds — a brochure and a director's report, both
from 1943 — describe it from inside.

**It is not the Camp Thunderbird of Victoria, British Columbia** that appears elsewhere in this
wiki in connection with Derek Walsh.

## Where it was

The brochure's cover reads "Camp for Boys **on an island in Mille River**, 20 miles from
Montreal, operated by Central YMCA," and its text places the island "in the Mille Isle River,
**near Ste. Eustache**."^2 The mail address is the sharpest locator anyone has: "Camp Thunderbird,
**c/o Mme. Lupi, Ste. Rose West, Quebec**."^2 The 1944 annual report puts it "on a small island in
Mille Isles River **near Plage Laval**."^3 Those three place-names — Sainte-Eustache, Sainte-Rose
and Plage Laval — sit within a few kilometres of each other along the river's south and north
shores at the western end of Île Jésus, so the island is locatable to a stretch of river but not
yet to a name.

Boys slept in tents on wooden floors "pitched on high land," each with a straw mattress; there was
a kitchen and dining tent, a tested spring-well, food brought in daily, and the brochure promised
"sufficient row boats for all."^2 The season report is drier about that: "**only two row-boats for
twenty-two people**."^1

## The 1943 session

The Central Branch period ran **6–13 August 1943** with twenty-two staff and campers.^1 It was
directed by **A. T. Dodds**, Associate Boys' Work Secretary of the Central YMCA, assisted by
**Simon Toye**, "a leader of many years experience at Central Branch and a scout leader at Camp
Tamaracouta." **Mrs. Dodds** — Verlie Dodds — "attended as dietician and cook."^1 ^2

Five boys, none over fourteen, served as assistant leaders at reduced fees "in recognition of
their leadership at the **Indoor Camp**": George Staples, Joseph St. Dennis (bugler), John
Atkinson, Don MacPherson and Ken Young.^1

The day ran from rising at 7.50 through tent inspection, camp projects, two general swims, a rest
hour, a free period, camp fire at nine, "Bible Reading by tent leader" at 9.30 and taps five
minutes later.^1 Hikes went to nearby woods, a life-saving class of eight drilled twice a day, the
camp-fire site was rebuilt, work parties collected firewood on the neighbouring islands, and
excursions went to a place called **Kozy Korners**.^1

It did not go smoothly. The weather was cold and wet. The stove's oven could not be used, so an
outside fireplace was built and did all the cooking for the last five days. "Many kitchen utensils
listed must have been stolen." There were several cases of homesickness, and one camper, James
McIsaac, was diagnosed in time to reach the city and undergo an appendix operation that evening.^1

Food cost **14.5 cents per person per meal**.^1

**The war is in the paperwork.** "Ration books must be [brought] to Mr. Dodds at the Central 'Y'
on Thursday, before the boys go to camp."^2

## What the association concluded

The 1944 annual report, after a second summer: "Camp Thunderbird: an experimental near-the-city
camp was again operated on a small island in Mille Isles River near Plage Laval. **Our experience
has proven the need for a permanent camp of this nature, which could be used by boys and girls
throughout the summer.**"^3 Thunderbird has its own line in the camper-numbers table in both the
1943 and 1944 reports, beside Kanawana and Otoreke, so the association counted it as a camp and not
a branch outing.^3 ^4

Dodds made the same argument from inside, at the end of the 1943 report: "in a small camp of this
nature there is an excellent opportunity to know the personal problems of the boys, and **there is
a need for this type of camp within the close range of the city**."^1

### How many summers, and which

Thunderbird has its own line in the "Summer Camps" camper table of the 1943 and 1944 annual
reports, beside Kanawana and Otoreke.^3 ^4 **It is absent from the 1945 table, where Perrot stands
in its place** — "Kanawana for boys 400, Otoreke for young people 825, Perrot for boys and girls
225" — and from every later report checked through 1952.^6 So the camp appears in exactly two
consecutive tables and stops, in the same report in which Perrot first appears.

**But those tables appear to lag the season by a year**, which unsettles the dates. The 1945
report's Kanawana figure of 400 is this wiki's figure for the **1944 season**, and the 1947
report's 302 is its figure for **1946**.^6 Two clean anchors, both a year off. A third is
unreadable rather than contradictory: the 1946 report reads "52h" where this scan renders 4 as *h*
throughout, so 524, or a misread 324 against the attendance series' 324 for 1945.

If the lag holds, Thunderbird's two tabled summers are **1942 and 1943**, and the 1944 report's
"again operated" describes the summer of 1943. Against that, the 1943 season report calls its
August week "the first session of Central Y.M.C.A. boys at Camp Thunderbird," which reads as a
first ever unless it means the first of that summer. **The years are not settled here.** What is
documented beyond doubt: one session, 6–13 August 1943; two consecutive tabled years; and the end
of them in the year Perrot begins.

**A reading, offered as a reading.** [[site/camp-perrot|Camp Perrot]] opened in **1944** — a
permanent, low-cost, branch-referral camp **for boys and girls**, near the city, on Île Perrot.
That is precisely the camp the 1944 report says the Thunderbird experiment proved the need for,
and A. T. Dodds directed both. **No document found says Perrot succeeded Thunderbird**, and none
is claimed here. The Camp Perrot founding papers (P0145/12H) or the 1944–45 camp committee minutes
would settle it.

## Open Questions

1. [Critical] **Which island?** Sainte-Eustache, Sainte-Rose West and Plage Laval bracket a
   stretch of the rivière des Mille Îles a few kilometres long. Historical topographic maps of the
   Laval/Deux-Montagnes sheet, the Commission de toponymie, and municipal or parish records for
   Sainte-Rose would narrow it. So might "Mme. Lupi," whose house took the camp's mail.
2. ~~[Critical] Did Camp Thunderbird run in 1945 or later, or did it stop at two summers?~~
   **[Resolved 2026-09-06]** Two summers. "Thunderbird" occurs zero times in the 1945 through 1952
   annual reports, and the 1945 camper table carries Perrot where Thunderbird had been.^6
3. [Critical, raised by resolving the above] **Do the annual reports' camper tables report the
   season of their own year or the one before?** Two Kanawana figures say the year before. That
   decides whether Thunderbird's summers were 1942–43 or 1943–44, and it bears on every 1940s row
   of [[meta/attendance-series|Attendance at Kanawana]]. The reports' own cover dates and
   fiscal-year statements would settle it and have not been read — these tables were reached by
   grep, not by reading the volumes.
4. [Important] Is Camp Perrot documented anywhere as Thunderbird's successor? See the reading
   above; P0145/12H and the 1944–45 committee minutes are the places to look.
5. [Important] What was the **Indoor Camp** that the 1943 assistant leaders had led? The phrase is
   used as though familiar and occurs nowhere else in this project.
6. [Nice-to-have] What does sub-series P0145/12I actually contain? Its extent and date range would
   say whether Thunderbird has more paper than these two documents.
