# Hedley Dimock and the Science of Camping

*Status: draft | Sources: src_aca_dimock_bio, src_concordia_atom_12B05, src_mcmorris_thesis, src_concordia_seaman_fund*
*Last Updated: 2026-03-02 (RALPH R1 completed)*

## Overview

Two individuals named Hedley Dimock made significant contributions to the study and practice of camping, and both are connected to the YMCA of Montreal and Camp Kanawana. Hedley Seldon Dimock (1891–1958) was a pioneering camp researcher who co-authored *Camping and Character* (1929), the foundational study of camp-based character education. Hedley G. Dimock (born c. 1928) conducted camp counsellor effectiveness research at Kamp Kanawana in 1960–1961 and went on to found the Department of Applied Social Science at Sir George Williams University (now Concordia), building a department that grew directly from SGW's institutional roots in the YMCA of Montreal.^2 ^4 Their exact familial relationship has not been confirmed, though the shared surname, YMCA involvement, and overlapping fields strongly suggest a father-son connection.

## Hedley Seldon Dimock (1891–1958)

Hedley Seldon Dimock was born February 11, 1891 in South Rawdon, Hants, Nova Scotia, the son of Charles Turner Torry Dimock and Catherine E. Dimock.^1 He served in the First World War with the Princess Patricia's Canadian Light Infantry. In August 1918, he was severely wounded after throwing himself on a German grenade to protect his unit.^1 It was months after the war before he could return to Canada and resume his studies.^1

He married Elizabeth Marguerite Gardiner and had two children.^1

### Camp Research and *Camping and Character*

In 1923, Dimock and Charles E. Hendry began a pioneering study at Camp Ahmek in Algonquin Park, Ontario, which was published as *Camping and Character: A Camp Experiment in Character Education* (Association Press, 1929/1931).^1 This was among the first systematic studies to measure the effects of summer camp on child development.

Dimock became dean of YMCA College in Chicago (later George Williams College), where in 1930 he launched institutes on the role of camps in "character education" of children.^1 During the Second World War, he served as Director of Training for the United Service Organization (USO).^1 After the war, he led philosophical discussions on employing "the small-group-centered method" at camp, alongside L.B. Sharp, Louis Blumenthal, Roy Sorenson, and Lois Goodrich.^1

He remained a leading figure in camping education until his death in 1958. The American Camp Association established the **Hedley S. Dimock Award** in 1965 to recognize contributions to camping through related fields such as outdoor education, conservation, medicine, education, or social sciences.^1

## Hedley G. Dimock (c. 1928–?)

### YMCA and Kanawana Research

Hedley G. Dimock served as Coordinator of Staff Development and Training for the Montreal YMCA and lecturer in Group Development at Sir George Williams University.^2 In 1960–1961, he conducted research at Kamp Kanawana on camp counsellor effectiveness, publishing two studies: "A Study of the Change in Attitudes and Temperament of Counselors during a Summer in Camp" (1961) and "A Study of the Prediction of Success of Twenty-Seven Camp Counselors" (1961).^2 ^3

### Founding the Department (1963)

In 1962, Dimock co-initiated a Community Development Study of the Cree of Mistassini-Chibougamau, Quebec.^2 In 1963, with the support of SGW Principal Robert C. Rae, he became the first Chairman of the Department of Applied Social Science and Director of the Centre for Human Relations and Community Studies at Sir George Williams University.^2 ^4

The department grew directly from SGW's institutional roots in the YMCA of Montreal. In the 1940s, YMCA/SGW had offered courses in counselling, group work, organization development, and supervision as part of a B.A. program.^4 Dimock formalized these into a degree-granting department. A. Ross Seaman, who had been Director of Kamp Kanawana, joined as part-time faculty in 1963 and became an active collaborator in the Centre's research.^5

### Publications and Later Career

Dimock authored twelve books, including *How to Observe Your Group*, *Planning Group Development*, *Administration of the Modern Camp*, *The Child in Hospital*, *Training for Planned Change*, and *Intervention and Empowerment*.^2 Five of his books were published in Japanese.^2

From 1972, he collaborated extensively with Dr. Raye Kass (hired as Assistant Professor of Applied Human Sciences at Concordia in 1972) on group development publications, including *Leading and Managing Dynamic Groups* (Captus Press, 2008) and *How to Observe Your Group* (4th edition, 2007).^2

Richard D. McDonald, who joined the department in 1965, succeeded Dimock as Centre Director upon Dimock's retirement in 1980.^2 In November 1997, Applied Social Science amalgamated with Leisure Studies to form the Department of Applied Human Sciences.^2

After retirement from Concordia, Dimock became Director of the Centre for Human Resource Development, an independent consulting and research organization in Puslinch, Ontario, and adjunct professor at the University of Guelph. He served on the faculties of eight universities including McGill, Columbia, Toronto, and Concordia, and worked with over 300 organizations across Canada.^2

## The YMCA-to-University Pipeline

The institutional connection between the YMCA, SGW/Concordia, Dawson College, and Camp Kanawana is one of the most distinctive features of this story. The Department of Applied Social Science literally grew from the YMCA of Montreal.^4 Dimock built it; Seaman — a former Kanawana Director — was founding-era faculty.^5 Seaman then went on to chair the Community Recreation and Leadership Training (CRLT) Department at Dawson College from 1974 to 1984, training the next generation of camp leaders and community recreation professionals.^5 After Seaman died in 1987, five organizations established memorial scholarships in his name.^5

This pipeline produced the academic infrastructure that studied, trained for, and theorized about organized camping in Montreal for half a century.
