# Taylor Statten and the Canadian Camping Movement

## Overview

Emerson Taylor Statten (1882–1956) was a foundational figure in Canadian organized camping whose influence shaped programs at Camp Kanawana and across the country.^1 ^2 A Boer War veteran and YMCA Boys' Work Secretary, Statten founded Camp Ahmek (1921) at Canoe Lake in Algonquin Park, pioneered the Canadian Standards Efficiency Training (CSET) program, co-founded the Ontario Camping Association (1933) and the Canadian Camping Association (1936), and established the model of wilderness canoe tripping that Kanawana and dozens of other camps adopted.^1 ^3

His collaboration with Hedley S. Dimock — who conducted pioneering camping research at Ahmek from 1923 and later joined Sir George Williams University — created a direct intellectual link between the Statten camping tradition and the Kanawana-connected Dimock research program.^4

## YMCA Career and CSET

Statten began Toronto YMCA boys' work around 1905–1906 and rose to national Boys' Work Secretary by 1912.^1 ^2 In 1914, he developed the Canadian Standards Efficiency Training (CSET) program, which standardized youth development across YMCA camps using a "fourfold" development philosophy: intellectual, physical, spiritual, and social.^5

The CSET program was formally launched in October 1918 with two tiers: Trail Rangers (ages 12–14) and TUXIS (ages 15–17).^5 A parallel girls' program, Canadian Girls in Training (CGIT), was also created.^5 Statten organized the first Ontario TUXIS and Older Boys' Parliament in 1917, reflecting his belief that boys should have leadership roles in program decision-making.^5 By 1928, the TUXIS and Trail Rangers programs enrolled over 30,000 boys across Canada.^2

In 1916, Statten embarked on a "Coast to Coast Tour" to promote the CSET program.^5 The program was co-managed with Protestant churches via the National Boys' Work Board, later dominated by the United Church of Canada. The YMCA eventually stepped back, and the program declined after the Second World War.^5

## Camp Ahmek

In 1921, Statten mortgaged his home to fund Camp Ahmek at Canoe Lake in Algonquin Park, named after the Ojibwa word for "Great Beaver."^1 ^6 The camp opened for six weeks with 60 boys living in tents.^6 In 1924, his wife Ethel Page ("Tonakela") opened Camp Wapomeo for girls on the same lake.^1 Pierre Trudeau attended Camp Ahmek as a camper.^1

Statten styled himself "Chief" and took the Ojibwa name Gitchi-Ahmek.^3 In the summer of 1922, he invited Ernest Thompson Seton to Canoe Lake to create a formal Indian Lore program, which became a template for camps across Canada.^3 In 1926, the Taylor Statten Camps commissioned 42-foot war canoes from the Peterborough Canoe Company, reportedly still in use.^6

Their son Taylor Statten II ("Dr. Tay," 1915–2016) took over as Camp Director in 1954.^6

## Dimock Collaboration and Academic Legacy

Hedley S. Dimock and Charles Hendry, both affiliated with Columbia University, conducted their pioneering research at Camp Ahmek starting in 1923.^4 Their work was published as *Camping and Character* (1929/1931), with a foreword by William H. Kilpatrick, a progressive educator and colleague of John Dewey.^4 Dimock and Statten later co-authored *Talks to Counselors* (1939), establishing a direct 16-year intellectual collaboration.^3

Dimock subsequently founded the Applied Social Science department at Sir George Williams University (later Concordia) in 1963, where A. Ross Seaman — Kanawana's director from 1959 to 1967 — became a part-time faculty member the same year. This institutional connection is one of the clearest threads linking the Statten camping philosophy to Kanawana's program development.

## Camping Association Leadership

The Ontario Camping Association was founded in 1933 with Statten as first chairman, alongside founding members A.L. Cochrane, Mary Edgar, Mary Hamilton, Ferna Halliday, and Ethel Statten.^7

On May 20, 1936, the Canadian Camping Association was founded at the Central Y.M.C.A. in Toronto, with Statten as first president and Mary Edgar as first vice-president.^8 Statten was also elected president of the American Camp Association in 1941 but resigned due to illness; Vice-President Wes Klusmann assumed responsibilities.^9

## Connection to Kanawana

Statten's influence on Kanawana was primarily indirect: through the CSET program adopted by YMCA camps nationally, the Indian Lore programming model, the CCA organizational framework, and the Dimock collaboration. Concordia University Archives contain Taylor Statten weekly letters from 1915, suggesting an early connection to the Montreal YMCA network that has not yet been explored.^3

Taylor Statten Camps first sent groups to Quetico in 1965 as part of the OCA Centennial Program, which became a permanent tradition — a parallel to Kanawana's own canoe tripping evolution in the La Vérendrye region during the same period.^3
