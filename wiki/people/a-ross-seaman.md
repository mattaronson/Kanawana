# A. Ross Seaman

*Status: E1-reviewed | Sources: 28*
*Last Updated: 2026-09-07 (very probably at Kanawana in 1940, nineteen years before he directed it)*

## Overview

A. Ross Seaman (**1921 – 23 October 1987**) was director of Kamp Kanawana from 1959 to 1967, a pivotal period that included the 1959 section renaming and the launch of the La Vérendrye canoe trip program.^1 A Sir George Williams University graduate, Seaman built a career spanning the YMCA, academia, and community recreation. **The birth year was found on 2026-09-07** in Concordia University Magazine's own obituary of January 1988, headed "ROSS SEAMAN / 1921-1987" and recording that he died "at age 66" — which closes the half of open question 1 that had stood since this article was written.^19 Five organizations — Dawson College, Concordia University, Kamp Kanawana, the YMCA, and Vankleek Hill Collegiate — honour his memory through scholarships.^2 ^3

## Early Career and YMCA

**A full biography appeared in *Canadian Camping* in June 1967**, when Seaman was President of the
Quebec Camping Association, and it fills in most of what this article previously had to infer:^9

> "Ross Seaman was born and educated in Montreal, receiving his B.A. degree from Sir George Williams
> University and later his **Master of Education degree from Springfield College**. But he really began
> his career **at the age of 10 when he attended Camp Kanawana as a camper**. From then on, he spent
> his summers at camp working his way up from C.I.T. to Director. His first position was with the YMCA
> **in 1945 as Community Secretary at the Point St. Charles Branch** in Montreal. Later he was
> appointed **Executive Secretary of the Lachine Branch** of the YMCA and then to the Metropolitan
> Staff in Montreal where he is the Metropolitan Programme Secretary and Director of Camping…
> Presently, Mr. Seaman directs two camps, Camp Kanawana, a boy's camp located near St. Sauveur des
> Monts in the Laurentians, 50 miles north of Montreal, and **'Les Voyageurs' a pioneer wilderness camp
> for teen agers**."

Two things follow. The camper-to-director progression is not a later reconstruction — it was how the
camping movement itself described him in 1967, and it begins at **age ten**. And the profile names his
wife: **"Mr. Seaman married Margaret Bradley and has three children, Derek, aged 16, Suzanne, aged 14
and Dawn aged 3."**^9 Margaret Seaman's maiden name was **Bradley**, which this wiki did not have.

**With the birth year, the camper year follows.** The profile dates the start of everything to "the age of 10 when he attended Camp Kanawana as a camper";^9 born in 1921, that is about **1931**, the camp's 38th season — twenty-eight years before he became its director and thirty-six before he left it.^19 The figure is only as exact as a round "age of 10" allows, but it fixes the shape of the life: the boy arrived during the Depression, and the man was still at the association's conferences in 1977.

After graduating from Sir George Williams University, Seaman entered community work and the camping movement through the YMCA.^2 ^3 **And he went back to it.** Concordia's *Thursday Report* describes him as "**an adviser to the YMCA Fellowship staff**" — the same Training Fellowship he had come through — and credits him with having "**originated** the Dawson College Community Recreation and Leadership Programme," which is a stronger claim than the chairmanship recorded below and is his own university writing about an award in his name, so it is reported here as theirs rather than adopted flat.^24 See [[connections/institutional-lineage/sgw-concordia-connection|The SGW–Concordia Connection]] for what the Fellowship was.

**He was on the Association's staff while he was a student there.** "Ross's history at Concordia goes back to the 1940s when he attended Sir George as a **YMCA Fellowship Secretary**," his obituary says.^19 That reverses the order the sentence above implies: the Y came first and Sir George — itself a YMCA institution — was an extension of it rather than a separate chapter. It also sits beside the 1967 profile's "His first position was with the YMCA in 1945 as Community Secretary at the Point St. Charles Branch,"^9 without quite fitting it; either Fellowship Secretary was a student arrangement rather than the "position" the profile means, or the profile's 1945 marks the first full appointment and not the first Association role. Neither source is explicit enough to settle it. **What the title itself meant is no longer in doubt.** A classmate of the same years spells it out in the 1943 yearbook — MacArthur Pat. Gordon, Arts: "**Y.M.C.A. Secretary in training**… **Have been Mtl. Fellowship Secretary for 2 years**, with Central Branch doing Hi-Y & Gra-Y club work and in Rosemont Community doing Community Boys Work."^20 It was a paid apprenticeship in the secretaryship, worked in the branches, with attendance at the college as its academic half. The Montreal YMCA called it a **Training Fellowship** and ran it for "young men looking to the Assn. secretaryship as a career"; [[people/murray-shantz|Murray B. Shantz]], who would hold Kanawana between Locke and Seaman, came through the same scheme in 1943. Seaman's route into the profession was the association's standard one. At some point in his career, he offered a colleague a position at the Cornwall YMCA^3 — and by **1974** he was that association's **Executive Director**, at 421 - 5th Street East, per the *Directory of Canadian Welfare Services* for that year.^17 Cornwall therefore comes **after** Kanawana, not before. The chronology and extent of his YMCA roles before Kanawana are undocumented. A newly found lead (2026-07-10): Camp Perrot's 1952 annual report thanks Seaman as "Past Director of Camp Perrot," a YMCA service camp on Île Perrot, and a 1959 internal memo from that camp records him personally planting trees there "about 1950" — placing a Camp Perrot directorship before his Kanawana tenure.^7 A "Margaret Seaman" served as Camp Perrot's Girls' Camp Director in 1952, plausibly the same Margaret who was Seaman's wife (see Personal Life and Legacy, below). **[Confirmed 2026-08-25.]** A June 1967 profile of Seaman in *Canadian Camping* states of his wife: "Mrs. Seaman, also a keen camper, has directed Camp Perrot (girls' section), Camp Chapleau of the Old Brewery Mission and the Junior League Camp of Montreal."^8 That attributes the Camp Perrot girls'-section directorship to Mrs. Seaman directly, closing the identification, and establishes a camp-directing career of her own across three separate organizations — the YMCA, the Old Brewery Mission, and the Junior League of Montreal. See [[connections/related-camps/camp-lighthall|Camp Lighthall]], which is the Junior League camp under its later name. See [[site/camp-perrot|Camp Perrot]] for the primary-source detail.

## Camp Kanawana (1959–1967)

Seaman served as director of Kamp Kanawana from 1959 to 1967.^1 During his tenure, he oversaw the 1959 section renaming and the expansion of the La Vérendrye canoe trip program, which grew from exploratory trips in 1958 to a formal satellite operation with a lease at Lac Landron by the early 1960s.^1 ^5 **His obituary names him the programme's founder and gives it its full name in one phrase**: "founder of the wilderness program **Les Voyageurs de la Vérendrye**."^19 The wiki had the thing from two directions without a sentence joining them — the La Vérendrye canoe trips launched under his directorship, and "Les Voyageurs" named as one of three YMCA camping sites in the Montreal Y's release of 28 November 1969.^18 This is the join, and it places the founding inside his YMCA career.

The YMCA of Montreal's 1966-67 Annual Report lists Seaman's title as "Director of Camping" and gives a detailed snapshot of the camp under his leadership: Kamp Kanawana's 73rd season drew 488 campers aged 7 to 17, totalling 1,323 camper-weeks (up from 1,182 in 1965).^6 A two-week training program preceded the season for 45 program staff, several of them Montreal YMCA Secretaries serving as section directors, alongside an eight-week program for Counsellors-in-Training and Junior Counsellors.^6 The same report quotes Seaman describing the camp's operating philosophy as a "participatory democracy," with campers involved in planning their own activities rather than following a fixed program — the clearest documented statement of his programming approach found to date.^6

## Academic Career

In 1963, while still directing Kanawana, Seaman became a part-time faculty member in Applied Social Science at Sir George Williams University, concentrating on group work techniques with youth.^2 He was an active collaborator in the program and research of the Centre for Human Relations and Community Studies.^2 **1963 is also the year the department itself was formed**, and Seaman was in it from the start: "Ross had been a part-time faculty member in Applied Social Science **since the department was formed in 1963**."^19 That is a second source for a founding date this wiki approached from the other side when the *Georgian* sweep found Kanawana's staff advertisement of 20 February 1962 naming Dr. H. G. Dimock as "Co-ordinator of staff training and development, Metropolitan YMCA" — an association post held the year before he founded the department. Two of the men who would teach in it were on the Y's payroll when it was formed.

**Camp practice went into the university calendar, not the other way round.** The obituary places it in his YMCA years: "During this period, he introduced **camping courses to the Sir George curriculum**."^19 See [[connections/institutional-lineage/sgw-concordia-connection|The SGW–Concordia Connection]].

In 1974, Seaman became teacher and chairperson of the Community Recreation and Leadership Training (CRLT) Department at Dawson College, a position he held until 1984.^3

After retiring from Dawson, Seaman served as acting chair of Concordia University's Leisure Studies program during the 1986–87 academic year.^2 During this period, he and his wife Margaret were instrumental in developing the Elder Hostel Program at Concordia's Loyola Campus.^2

**[SUPERSEDED 2026-09-07: the paragraph above is not wrong but is much too small, and the Elderhostel dates in it are two summers late. Kept as written; corrected here.]** The obituary states that "at the time of his death, he was in his **third year as a full-time Concordia faculty member in Leisure Studies**, where he served as Acting Chair in 1986-87."^19 Third year at October 1987 puts the full-time appointment's start in **1985**, immediately after the Dawson retirement — so the last years were a third career of at least three years, cut short, rather than the one-year coda the acting chairmanship alone suggests. On Elderhostel it says that "Ross and his wife Margaret played central roles in the development of Elderhostel on Loyola campus, acting as **host and hostess** of the program **over the past three summers**" — from a January 1988 issue, the summers of **1985, 1986 and 1987**, the last of them the summer before he died.^19

**He also consulted at a francophone CEGEP.** "As a consultant, he played a major role in the development of techniques of specialized education, **active retirement**, and volunteer training at Collège Marie-Victorin."^19 This is the only French-language institution anywhere in his record. "Active retirement" is the Elderhostel interest pursued in a second place, and "volunteer training" and "specialized education" are the same leadership-development thread that runs from the camp through Dawson's CRLT programme to the fund named after him.

## Personal Life and Legacy

Seaman's home community was Vankleek Hill, Ontario, where he was remembered for church and community involvement.^2 He died in October 1987; Concordia's Applied Human Sciences department described his death as "a loss to countless people from many places where he expended his energies and warmth to help others to become the best they could be."^2 **The exact date is 23 October 1987**, given in the fund's founding notice printed in *Canadian Camping Magazine* in the winter of 1988: "the passing of **A. Ross Seaman on October 23, 1987**."^12 That notice is also where the sentence quoted above originates.

The A. Ross Seaman Memorial Leadership Development Fund was established in 1987 at Concordia University (designation code ES0042) to honour his dedication to Concordia, Dawson, Kamp Kanawana, and the YMCA.^2 The fund is notable for raising money through an annual awards evening with auction — described as possibly the only active Concordia scholarship using this fundraising model.^4 **It was five awards, not one.** Concordia's *Thursday Report* of 15 November 1990, reporting the first Concordia presentation: "Seaman died in 1987. **Five Leadership Awards have been created in his name** to be presented annually. **Two** awards are presented to Concordia students in the **APSS and Leisure Studies** programmes. The other awards are presented to students at **Dawson**, those involved with the **YM/WCA** and with **Kamp Kanawana**."^24 That explains the 1995-96 calendar's plural "Scholarships," which had looked like loose wording. Note "YM/**W**CA": the YWCA is in the scheme here, and no other source held in this project puts it there.

**A sixth constituency, and it is his home county.** The *Thursday Report* of 6 June 1991 lists the groups the award is offered to — "the YMCA, Kamp Kanawana, Concordia's Department of Applied Social Science, as well as Dawson College **and in his home community of Prescott-Russell**."^24 Prescott-Russell is the united counties of eastern Ontario containing Vankleek Hill. So the home-town scholarship recorded above was not a separate local memorial; **his home community was written into the award scheme itself.**

**Margaret Seaman was still part of it in 1990**, photographed at the presentation with APSS Professor Dick McDonald and APSS Chair Richard Cawley, three years after his death.^24 McDonald's line about her husband is the best short account of him this project holds: "**No matter who [Seaman] was with, they always came out of the experience more upbeat than when they went in.**"

*On the winners.* These articles name their recipients and describe them. Consistent with the decision registered as **`r_0006`**, the institutional facts are taken here and the winners' personal circumstances are not. One name is kept — **Peter Clark**, Leisure Studies, 1991 — because "the first time the award was given to a student in that department" is a fact about the award rather than about him, and because the same name appears in the fund's 2007 honour roll, which independently confirms that roster's accuracy.

**And the event had a name, a format and a guest list.** *The Link* of 17 March 1992: "Friday, March 20. **A. Ross Seaman Memorial Cup**: friendly competition of '**Spaceball**' between **Leisure Studies, APSS, Dawson's CRLT programme, and YMCA's Camp Kanawana**. Also: variety show, t-shirts, buffet dinner, an auction, & awards. 2:30pm, Loyola Campus Athletic Complex, 7141 Sherbrooke St. W."^23

The four teams are the four communities the scholarship's own terms name — Leisure Studies and Applied Social Science standing for the two Concordia departments Seaman taught in, CRLT for the Dawson programme he built, and the camp for itself. **The four constituencies of the award played a ball game against each other to raise the money for it**, which is a more exact and a stranger thing than "an awards evening with auction." It also matches the 2007 booklet's praise of one winner for work on "the T-shirt Committee, A. Ross Seaman Cup and Bill Lewis Race at the YMCA" — t-shirts and Cup both appear here; the Bill Lewis Race is still unidentified.

**The Cup's own invitation survives, and it had a theme.** From the *Thursday Report* the week before the 1992 event: "To the students of Concordia University Leisure Studies and A.P.S.S., Dawson College C.R.L.T. and YMCA Camp Kanawana. You are invited to participate in this year's A. Ross Seaman Memorial Cup, '**Recreation and Leisure, a Door to the Future**.' This is a **one-day mission to raise money for the scholarship fund, learn leadership development and have a great time**. The day will kick off with an **intergalactic game of Spaceball** and includes other **out-of-this-world events**, such as variety show, t-shirts, free dinner, an auction and awards."^24 **The Cup ran from 1988 and grew.** By its tenth anniversary in March 1997 there were **nine** Seaman Awards rather than the five of 1990, about **250 people** attended, and the *Thursday Report* explains the format this article could not: students planned it, and the "friendly competition" was in "**novelty events that change from year to year**" — which is why 1992 was Spaceball.^24 Rector Frederick Lowy attended that year, with Patrick Woodsworth, Director-General of Dawson, and Russ Williams, MNA for Nelligan. **The keynote was [[people/notable-alumni/stuart-mclean|Stuart McLean]]**, who "graduated from Concordia's Applied Social Sciences program **when Seaman taught here**," worked as a student advisor at Dawson, "talked about his memories of Seaman," and donated copies of his bestsellers to an auction that raised more than $2,000 for the fund.^24 So the camp's most famous alumnus was taught by the camp's 1959–67 director, in the director's own department.

**The Cup has a year-by-year history, written by its own committee, and it corrects the picture above.** The 2007 booklet closes with "A brief history" giving a paragraph to every year from 1988 to 2003 and then a table of Year, Activity, Host and Winners.^21 **The first Cup was between two bodies, not four.** In the spring of 1988, at Loyola, "the competition was between the students of the Leisure Studies Program at Concordia and the students from the Community Recreation Leadership Training program at Dawson College," playing touch football "on a very snowy field," with supper afterwards at the Golden Moon Restaurant. Dawson ran the second at Westmount High School in 1989 (volleyball, $85 raised); 1990 was broomball at the Loyola arena, in hats, and produced a cheque for $700.

**Kanawana entered in the fourth year and won in the fifth.** The 1991 entry, at the Beaconsfield Recreation Centre: "The CRLT students organized floor hockey and **Kamp Kanawana made their debut**." The table's next row is 1992 — Spaceball, hosted by Concordia, winners "**Kamp Kanawana (The Spirit)**." So the camp did not found the Cup; it joined a going concern in 1991 and took it the following March, in the game the surviving invitation describes. From there it moved inward: in 1995 "Dawson College students created a committee **with the YMCA, Kamp Kanawana and Concordia students**" to run the event, and in 1999 "the YMCA of Montreal, **YMCA Kamp Kanawana** and Dawson College CRLT provided food and beverages." A weekend at the camp became the prize everyone wanted — the last narrated year records that "this year's big winners in the silent auction for **the Kamp Kanawana weekend** were the students from Dawson CRLT."^21

**And it has an end.** The table stops at 2003, the sixteenth. "In 2004 and subsequent years, awards presentations are decentralized at a time and place best suited for their individual community. **Special 25th year combined celebrations will occur in 2012.** This change in policy is ensuring continued success meeting the Fund's objectives."^21 Whether the 2012 reunion happened is not documented here. *(One dating problem, left open: the narrative runs 1988 through 2002 and then jumps to a paragraph headed "2008," describing an awards evening of over 100 guests — in a booklet published in May 2007. Since the table's last row is 2003, "16th Anniversary," and the decentralization note begins in 2004, the heading is most likely a misread of 2003; the booklet is a scan and 3 and 8 confuse readily. It is not corrected here, only flagged.)*

**The committee that ran it seated the camp in its own right.** The closing page lists the agency representatives: Concordia University, 7141 Sherbrooke Street West, **Randy Swedburg**, Applied Human Sciences; Dawson College, 3040 Sherbrooke Street West, **Holly Bockus**, CRLT; "YMCA, 1435 Drummond, 4th Floor, **Marc Chamberland**"; and, on a line of its own, "**YMCA Kanawana**, 1435 Drummond, 4th Floor, **Sean Day**." Directors at Large were Richard J. Cuttell, Charles Poxon and J. William Russell, with Dr. Steven Henle for the Applied Human Sciences alumni.^21 The camp sat at that table as a fourth agency rather than as a branch of the third, at the same downtown address — and [[people/sean-day|Sean Day]], whose Kanawana executive directorship this wiki dates from roughly 2005 on the YMCA's own reports, is documented in the role by an outside publication in May 2007.

**The committee's own account of the 1997 keynote** agrees with the *Thursday Report* and adds a line to it: "Renowned author **Stuart MacLean**, broadcaster and **professor of Journalism at Ryerson Polytechnical Institute**, recollected antidotes from Ross' friends."^21 The booklet spells the name MacLean where every other source has [[people/notable-alumni/stuart-mclean|McLean]], and "antidotes" is plainly anecdotes; both are left as printed.

*The event moved.* It was held in **May 1991 at the Beaconsfield Recreation Centre** and in **March 1992 at Loyola** — fixed to neither a month nor a place.^24 (The two notices for 1992 give the Loyola complex as 7141 and 7200 Sherbrooke St. W.; the discrepancy is recorded and not resolved.) **Its founding is documented.** "A group of **family, friends, and colleagues met in early November** and proposed a living memorial through the establishment of a **fellowship/scholarship/bursary fund** in his name for the purpose of leadership development… Our aim is to use **only the interest of the fund, leaving the capital intact**, so that the fund will benefit others throughout the years. Therefore, we have set as our **minimum goal the sum of $25,000.00**." Contributions went to "**Fonds Commémoratif A. Ross Seaman, Université Concordia, Bureau des finances AD 130, 7141 ouest, rue Sherbrooke, Montréal, Québec H4B 1R6**" — the same address in English in *Concordia University Magazine*, "Financial Aid Office, Loyola Campus, AD-130-4, 7141 Sherbrooke St. West"^19 — and the notice states in capitals that "**THIS IS NOT AN OFFICIAL CCA/ACC FUND-RAISING EFFORT. IT HAS BEEN ORGANIZED FOR AND THROUGH CONCORDIA UNIVERSITY.**"^12 The same notice records what his colleagues took his work to have been — "camping and leadership training, recreation leadership, and community organization, particularly as it relates to adolescent life and latterly as it related to an aging population" — one of them calling him "**a cheerleader of the human race**," and notes that "one of Ross' philosophies was to '**pass it on**'."

**The meeting has a date and a guest list.** The *Canadian Camping* notice describes it in personal terms and does not date it — "a group of family, friends, and colleagues met in early November." Concordia's own magazine does both: "Representatives from **Concordia, Dawson College, the YMCA and Camp Kanawana** met on **November 10** to set up a fund to provide scholarships for students in leadership development programs."^19 Near certainly the same meeting. The detail worth keeping is the fourth name on that list: **Kanawana sent someone**, to a Montreal meeting eighteen days after his death and twenty years after his directorship of the camp had ended. The Dawson College CRLT scholarship awards $500 annually to a second-year student who displays leadership, community service, caring, and genuine concern for others.^3

**And the fund went on giving its bursary to Kanawana people for eighteen years.** Concordia printed a commemorative booklet in May 2007, *A History of Bursary Award Winners 1989-2006*, with a citation for every winner. **"Kanawana" occurs twenty-seven times in it** — the camp is the most frequently named institution in the winners' backgrounds after Concordia itself and the YMCA generally, and the roles named run from camper and counsellor-in-training through junior and senior counsellor to nature specialist and senior camp programme lead.^21 The meeting of 10 November 1987 at which the fund was set up had a Kanawana representative in the room; the fund then spent eighteen years awarding money to people that camp had formed. **The circuit Seaman himself ran — camp into college into the profession — was still running twenty years after his death, under his name.**

**[CORRECTED the same day, 2026-09-07 — the sentence above is kept as written and claims more than the evidence carries.]** Concordia's own *Undergraduate Calendar* states the award's terms, in six editions from 2000-01 to 2006-07, word for word: the scholarship honours Seaman's dedication "to the communities of Concordia University, Dawson College, Kamp Kanawana and the YMCA," and **"each year, students from these four institutions are recognized for their leadership and scholarship."**^22 Kanawana is named in the scholarship's own constitution as one of four constituencies. So its recurrence across the honour roll is **partly structural**, not the camp spontaneously producing winners, and "the circuit still running" reads a designed allocation as a spontaneous one. **What survives is the better fact:** the people who set the fund up in November 1987 wrote a summer camp into the terms of a university scholarship, beside two colleges and the association, and it was still there twenty years later. The calendar also gives the mechanism — students "can apply or be nominated," through the Department of Applied Human Sciences — and keeps the word *Memorial* in the name that the 2007 booklet drops.^22

**The entry runs from at least 1995-96, and its wording changes once, informatively.** The 1995-96 calendar has "A. Ross Seaman Memorial **Scholarships**" — plural, so more than one was given a year — and directs applicants to "the **Departments of Applied Social Science and Leisure Studies**."^22 By 2000-01 it is a single scholarship administered by the **Department of Applied Human Sciences**, the successor unit, so the award's administration simply followed the department's own renaming. The detail worth stopping on is that those two 1990s departments are **exactly the two Seaman himself taught in**: Applied Social Science part-time from its formation in 1963, and Leisure Studies full-time from 1985 until his death. For at least its first decade the scholarship was run by the two units that had employed him. One winner came from **Vankleek Hill Collegiate**, his home-town high school a hundred kilometres away in eastern Ontario, so that connection runs through the Montreal fund too and not only as the separate home-town scholarship recorded above.^21

**The only testimony this project holds from anyone he taught is in that booklet.** A winner of Applied Human Sciences' Human Relations Award "caught the 'leadership bug' early in her professional career **as a camp nurse, under the mentorship of Ross Seaman**," and went on to be a community volunteer, team builder and student facilitator.^21 This wiki has a great deal about what Seaman did and nothing else from anyone he formed. It also has almost nothing on camp nursing as a role.

**The booklet also reprints the memorial statement, which makes three witnesses to it, and they differ in small ways worth keeping.** It has "a cheerleader **for** the human race" against the *Canadian Camping* notice's "cheerleader **of**"; "consummate teacher **and mentor**" against the January 1988 obituary's "consummate teacher"; and it calls the fund the A. Ross Seaman Leadership Development Fund, without *Memorial*, as the magazine does — so both names are the university's own and neither is an error to correct.^21

*The winners, and why they are inside a marker.* The booklet names every winner from 1989 to 2006 and gives each a paragraph on their character, career and camp service. Those are living private individuals, most of them students when they were named, the youngest awarded within twenty years of this reading; Concordia published all of it, so nothing here is being disclosed against anyone's wishes, but a wiki assembling a searchable biography out of an honour roll is doing something the university's commemorative booklet was not. **This wiki was for one day summarising them away instead**, which loses the record and leaves a later reader unable to tell a silent source from a silent editor — the failure `kb/restricted/README.md` exists to prevent. So the Kanawana-connected citations are set down here in full, labelled, dated and registered as **`r_0006`**, review 2036.

## Provincial and national office

Seaman held office in organised camping for most of his career, and the camping press dates the steps.^10

He was **Secretary of the Quebec Camping Association** from about 1949, and the April 1951 issue records how it ended: "Ross Seaman, secretary of the QCA for the past two years, has been **forced to resign from that position due to his recent appointment to the Lachine YMCA**." That independently dates his move to the Lachine branch, which this article had from the YMCA's own records alone. He was in charge of registration for the National-Quebec Conference held that spring at the Sir Arthur Currie Gymnasium.

**The 1962-63 secretaryship was national, and this article had it wrong.** An earlier version of this
paragraph read "He is QCA Secretary again in the 1962 and 1963 officer lists." The list in question is
the **Canadian Camping Association's** masthead, printed under the heading "CANADIAN CAMPING
ASSOCIATION, 2233 Belgrave Avenue, Montreal 28, Quebec," and it reads "Secretary — Mr. A. Ross Seaman,
4140 Jean Renou, Lachine, Que."^16 The address is the giveaway: 4140 Jean Renou is his own, and it is
printed in the *national* masthead. He stands there in February 1962, June 1962 and February 1963,
having taken the post from **Mrs. Agnes Mutchler**, who held it as Corresponding Secretary in December
1961.^16 So while he was directing Kanawana he was also secretary of the national association — an
office that was in Montreal at all only because the CCA's head office was, throughout **Kenneth H.
Murray's** presidency.

It ended when the office left town. The **April 1963** masthead is headed "CANADIAN CAMPING
ASSOCIATION, 4322-4a Street S.W., Calgary, Alberta," with **Miss Eleanor Logan** of the Edmonton YMCA as
Secretary under a new president, **Dr. Don Smith** of Edmonton.^16 Seaman's national secretaryship
therefore ran from February 1962 to no later than February 1963, and the Montreal era of the CCA ended
with it.

*Whether he held the provincial secretaryship in those same years is not settled here, only unproven.*
No Quebec Camping Association officer list appears in any cached issue between December 1961 and June
1963 — across those eight issues the phrase "Quebec Camping Association" occurs exactly once, in the
notice of the joint CCA / QCA / Association des camps du Québec convention of 22-24 March 1962 at the
Sheraton Mount Royal — and the April 1962 and December 1962 issues carry no masthead page in the
digitized text at all.^16 That is a fact about what survives in this run, not about what the
association did.

**His two association offices can now be dated exactly, where this article previously had only a range.** He became the **Canadian Camping Association's vice-president for Quebec** in **December 1966**, replacing Robert Lazanik on the masthead, and was off the national executive by the **spring 1968** masthead, which gives Quebec's vice-presidency to Mrs. N. D. Demetelin.^13 And he was **president of the Quebec Camping Association** until **23 September 1967**, when a new Provincial Board was elected at International Camping Day at Expo 67 — Sophia Demetelin president, with "Robert Lazanik, **A. Ross Seaman** and Reverend Lionel Perras, vice-presidents" — so he stepped down from the presidency to a vice-presidency on that date, which brackets his QCA presidency to roughly 1966 to September 1967.^14 The winter 1968 issue's description of him as "immediate past president" of the English section follows from this. **So through the whole of Kanawana's centennial year Seaman was simultaneously the camp's director, the province's association president, and Quebec's vice-president on the national board** — which is worth holding beside the [[history/centennial-1967|Centenary Journey]], since the man writing Kanawana's letters to parents that August was also the man Quebec camping was being run by.

**And in his last months as director he was teaching method to another region.** At the **first Atlantic Provinces Regional Camp Directors' and Administrators' Seminar**, Moncton, N.B., **17–19 November 1967**, "Ross Seaman, immediate past president of the English Section of the Quebec Camping Association, was the resource person" for eighteen delegates drawn from Newfoundland to Halifax — Anglican, Baptist and United church camps, YMCAs, Guides, Scouts, Boys' Clubs, a Co-op Services camp, the 4H Clubs and the Zionist Organization. The theme was "the Human Relations Aspect in Camping," and among the sessions he carried were "**Methods of Measurement in Camping**" and "**Methods of Evaluation**."^15 That bears on how the camp's own paperwork should be read: **any Kanawana staff-assessment material from this period is more likely to reflect a considered method than casual note-taking**, since its director was the man the Maritimes imported to teach the subject. In summer 1973 he led a national workshop on **funding**, which delegates reported "left us the most to grapple with in terms of new information."

**He was still in Quebec camping ten years after leaving Kanawana.** The report of the Quebec Camping Association English Section's **40th anniversary conference, 25-27 March 1977**, names "an impressive list of past presidents of Q.C.A. in attendance, namely; **Norrey Owens, Zave Ettinger, Ross Seaman, Bob Lazanick, Peter van Wagner, Bill McCarthy and Val Willis**."^11 That is a dated appearance for Seaman a full decade after his Kanawana directorship ended and twenty-eight years after he first became the association's secretary — and it places him in the same room as **O. N. H. Owens**, the Kanawana Committee chairman who had endowed the Order of Owens at Kanawana the previous summer (see [[traditions/order-of-owens|The Order of Owens]]). The two men had also served together on the QCA executive in 1954, Owens as president and Seaman as conference chairman.

**[SUPERSEDED 2026-09-07 — the release was right, and three passages of this same article already said so. Kept as written:]** **A press release calls him a past president of the QCA, and this article does not adopt it.** The Montreal YMCA's bilingual release of 28 November 1969 announces that the three main camping sites — "Kanawana, Les Voyageurs et Perrot" — now constitute a special branch of the association, headed by "Monsieur A. Ross Seaman, du Métropolitain, **ancien Directeur de Kanawana et ancien Président de l'Association des Campeurs du Québec**."^18

That is a claim of the **presidency** of the Quebec Camping Association. Everything above documents him as its **secretary**, from about 1949 until the forced resignation of 1951, and the 1962-63 office as secretary of the *national* association rather than the provincial one. A French press column paraphrasing an English-language office is exactly where "secretary" becomes "président," and the QCA's own officer lists in *Canadian Camping* are the better evidence. Recorded as a claim someone made in 1969, not as an office he held.

**The correction.** "Everything above documents him as its secretary" was written without regard to material standing elsewhere in this same file, and the word "everything" is where it fails. Three passages, each with its own source, put him in the chair. The Early Career section above: the June 1967 *Canadian Camping* biography appeared "**when Seaman was President of the Quebec Camping Association**."^9 The Moncton seminar of 17–19 November 1967: "Ross Seaman, **immediate past president of the English Section** of the Quebec Camping Association, was the resource person."^15 And the 40th anniversary conference of 25–27 March 1977, which names "an impressive list of **past presidents** of Q.C.A. in attendance, namely; Norrey Owens, Zave Ettinger, **Ross Seaman**, Bob Lazanick, Peter van Wagner, Bill McCarthy and Val Willis."^11 He held the presidency of the QCA's English Section, on this evidence for a term ending in 1967 — after the secretaryship of about 1949–1951 and the national secretaryship of 1962–63, and two years before the release. The masthead argument above is sound for the years it covers and says nothing about 1966–67. Concordia's obituary of January 1988 adds a fourth institution's account of the same relationship, in a different word: he was "**a primary organizer of the Quebec Camping Association**."^19 Organizing and presiding are separate claims and this one settles neither; what it does is put a leading part in the association's building on the record from an independent side. See [f_5390].

**The same release dates the camping branch.** "Les trois principaux sites de campement du YMCA — Kanawana, Les Voyageurs et Perrot — **constituent maintenant une succursale spéciale du Y**," with Seaman directing it.^18 This wiki knows that reorganisation only from a Concordia listing, "Montreal YMCA Camping Branch Board of Management. – 1969-1970." The release puts it in place by **November 1969**, names its head, and lists its three constituent sites — which is also independent evidence that Camp Perrot was still an active YMCA camping site at the end of 1969.


**The two counts that looked like a contradiction are a year apart for a reason.** *Concordia's Thursday Report* of 12 April 2001 reports that "the **13th annual awards evening** took place March 22."^25 A thirteenth evening in 2001 counts from **1989**, which is exactly where Concordia's own commemorative booklet starts its winners: *A History of Bursary Award Winners 1989-2006*. That sits beside the 1997 report's tenth anniversary of the **Cup**, which counts from 1988. The numbers are not in conflict — on this evidence the Cup ran for a year before the awards evening it came to fund, and the fund's two halves have separate birthdays.

**A sixth constituency becomes a school.** The same report lists the groups Seaman shaped: "not only of Concordia students, but also of those at **Dawson College** (Community Recreation Leadership Training — CRLT), **the YMCA, Kamp Kanawana and Van Kleek High School**."^25 The 1991 report gave that last constituency as Prescott-Russell, the eastern-Ontario county Vankleek Hill sits in, and this article already had Vankleek Hill as his home community. This is the first source to place him with **students at a school there** rather than simply living in the town, and it is the plainest statement anywhere of the five places he worked. The report also states flatly that "he died in 1987," agreeing with the obituary.

**Kanawana keeps its seat, and the seat beside it moves.** The 2001 winners were Barbara Jack, Elizabeth Anderson and Jamie Patterson (Concordia AHSC), Chantale Victoria Lewis (Dawson–CRLT), Farah Wikarski (YMCA) and **Matthew Busbridge (Kamp Kanawana)**.^25 The following year, at a ceremony on **20 March 2002 at Dawson College**, the winners were Jacinthe Morsani, Kimberley Jackson, Ruxandra Vlad and Shawn Jaffray (Concordia), **Justin Desforges (Kamp Kanawana)**, Anna Tafuto (Dawson) and Elizabeth Sweeney (**YWCA**), with Donna O'Bomsawin absent from the photograph.^26 Two things follow. A third and fourth consecutive documented year of a named Kanawana recipient supports the corrected reading above: the award's terms allocate a seat to Kanawana, so the recurrence is structural rather than a standing flow of candidates. And the list is **not fixed** — 2001 has the YMCA and no YWCA, 2002 the reverse. Whether that is a substitution, a caption error, or a year the YMCA seat went unclaimed cannot be told from a photo caption.

**The department was a merger, not a renaming.** This article and the McLean material had noted only that the *Thursday Report* calls the programme "Applied Social Sciences" in 1997 and "Applied Human Science" in 2004. The 2001 report explains it, in a tribute to **Richard D. McDonald**, who died in October 2000: "a long-standing member of the Department of Applied Social Sciences, Dick was an integral part of **the merger between the APSS and Leisure Sciences that created the Department of Applied Human Sciences**."^25 The two departments that merged are the two Seaman himself belonged to, which is why both names attach to him in every notice. All the 2001 auction proceeds went to a new scholarship in McDonald's memory. A second scholarship, funded by sales of a book called *Tapestry of Adulthood: Women's Reflections*, was given at the same 2002 ceremony to two further winners.^26

**The fund had a website, and it is gone.** The 2001 report closes by directing readers to `http://arossseaman.tripod.com/`.^25 That is where a full winners list, the award's terms and the fund's own account of Seaman would have lived — material this project has had to reassemble from a dozen scattered notices. **The Wayback Machine has no capture of it.** Its availability API returns an empty result for the bare host, for `/index.html` and for the `www.` form, against a control (`concordia.ca`) that returns a snapshot on the same call, so the null belongs to the site and not to the tool. One limit, stated so it is not read as more than it is: the fuller CDX enumeration, which would catch captures of deeper pages, **could not be run** — `web.archive.org/cdx` is unreachable from this environment while `archive.org` answers normally. Before the site is called lost for good, that check should be repeated from somewhere that can reach it.

**A camp nurse, and the camp is not named.** Of the 2001 winner Barbara Jack, the report adds that she "was also lucky enough to have **worked with Ross Seaman as a Camp Nurse** early on in her professional career."^25 Seaman ran Kanawana from 1959 to 1967 and the sentence sits inside an article about an award with a Kanawana seat, so Kanawana is the obvious reading. The report does not say Kanawana, he worked at other camps, and the obvious reading is recorded here as a lead rather than as a line on Kanawana's medical staffing.

**The camp nurse of the 2001 citation is very probably Kanawana's, in 1965.** The note above records
the *Thursday Report*'s line that Barbara Jack "worked with Ross Seaman as a Camp Nurse early on in her
professional career," and leaves the camp unnamed because the report does not name it. *The Chestnut*
of 1965 profiles Kanawana's staff, and among them: "**Barbara Jack** — the nurse, is 21 years of age
and is a nurse by occupation. She came to camp on advice of Dave Hardcastle. **This is her first camp
job** and she wishes to return next year."^27 Four particulars converge — the same name, the same
role, the same director, and a first camp job that would indeed be "early on in her professional
career" for someone taking a Concordia degree decades later. That is not a documented identity and is
not recorded as one, but it is a good deal more than a coincidence of surname, and the 1965 camp is
Kanawana.

**And it shows him arguing for coeducation two years before the decision.** Asked by the boys what he
would change about camp, Seaman named "a good staff lounge and **a co-educational camp**," and,
pressed, "said boys and girls should be together and it would be good for the morale."^27 He gives
the camp's size in the same interview — he "likes to see a full camp (**226 campers and 69 staff**)" —
and mentions that his wife was herself a camp director. See
[[history/coeducation-gender|Coeducation and Gender]] for the staff poll around him.

**He was at Kanawana in 1940, nineteen years before he directed it.** *The Green Triangle*'s account
of the camp circus of 25 July 1940 ends: "The clowning of **Ross Seaman**, Jack Liddy, and Chief Kolah
gave the afternoon a real circus spirit."^28 This article begins his Kanawana story with the
directorship of 1959. Born in 1921, he would have been **nineteen** in the summer of 1940 — the right
age for a junior counsellor, and consistent with the account elsewhere here of a young man who
attended Sir George Williams as a **YMCA Fellowship Secretary** in the 1940s, a scheme that placed its
apprentices in the association's own operations.

**The caution is the ordinary one and it is small.** The paper gives no initial and no role, and
"Ross Seaman" is not an uncommon pair of names. But a Ross Seaman clowning at a Montreal YMCA camp
circus in 1940, in a camp this man would run from 1959, at exactly the age and in exactly the years
his own Fellowship placed him inside the association, is a good deal more than a coincidence of name.
Recorded as very probably him, and as the earliest date this project has for his connection to
Kanawana.

## Open Questions

1. [Critical, **birth year answered 2026-09-07**] What are Seaman's full biographical details — birth year, full name, education beyond SGW? **The birth year is 1921**, from the headline "ROSS SEAMAN / 1921-1987" over Concordia University Magazine's obituary, which also gives his age at death as 66.^19 Since he died on 23 October, his birthday fell on or before that date — a test any birth record found later has to pass, and a narrowing of the search from a year to ten months. The 1967 profile adds that he was "**born and educated in Montreal**" and took a **Master of Education from Springfield College** after the Sir George B.A.,^9 so "education beyond SGW" is answered too; what remains open is the **full given name behind the initial A.**, the exact birth date, and the Springfield years. **The death date is exact: 23 October 1987**, from the memorial fund notice in *Canadian Camping Magazine* (Winter 1988), which narrows any obituary search to the last week of October and the first weeks of November 1987, and is now held on **two** independently produced documents rather than one, the Concordia obituary agreeing with the fund notice.^19 A further pass (genealogy sites, FindAGrave, Vankleek Hill obituary aggregators) found nothing new. The Vankleek Hill newspaper "The Review" (archived from 1893) very likely carries an October/November 1987 obituary, but its own online archive is not full-text searchable — this requires a paid GenealogyBank-style subscription search or direct contact with the newspaper or a Vankleek Hill funeral home, not further web queries.
